# Authorship audit (repair) — The Pointing Game

*scene: scenes/the-pointing-game.md · model: claude-opus-4-8 · framing: repair · protocol: authorship-audit-v1-pilot · prompt-sha: 374133b02af5 · run: 2026-09-07*

## Passage 1

> "He knew the difference between a woman performing release and a woman delivered to it. She had been performing, the first week. She was not performing now."

**DIAGNOSIS** — The flag is right about the mechanism but the fix is narrow. The problem isn't that he assesses her — that's the whole POV — it's the flat declarative "He knew the difference," which the narration underwrites as fact rather than as his belief. It reads as the author certifying a man's connoisseurship of female orgasm. Recast it as *his* certainty and the same content survives without the endorsement.

**ALTERNATIVES**

*a.* "He stayed with it longer than she needed, reading the slackening in her thighs, her breath going from held to spilled. He trusted himself to know the difference between a woman performing release and a woman delivered to it — and by that reckoning she had been performing, the first week. She was not performing now."

*b.* "He stayed with it longer than she needed, reading the slackening in her thighs, her breath going from held to spilled. He thought he could tell a woman performing release from a woman delivered to it, and he'd have staked something on the reading: she had been performing, the first week. She was not performing now."

*c.* "He stayed with it longer than she needed, reading the slackening in her thighs, her breath going from held to spilled. Performed release and the real thing did not feel the same under his mouth — or he believed they didn't, and he believed it about her: performing, the first week; not now."

**CUT OPTION** — A trim doesn't beat the recast here, because the "performing / not performing" beat is doing real narrative work (it dates the relationship, it sets up how well he reads her). Deleting only the middle sentence leaves the claim implied but unowned: "He stayed with it longer than she needed, reading the slackening in her thighs, her breath going from held to spilled. She had been performing, the first week. She was not performing now." — clean, but it loses the epistemic hedge that is the actual repair. Prefer a rewrite.

## Passage 2

> "still swollen and still pulsing around nothing, still given over to her body, undefended. He had decided this beforehand."

**DIAGNOSIS** — "pulsing around nothing" is the male tell — it inventories her interior from a vantage he doesn't have yet (he's not inside her at this line; the "nothing" is a camera looking into her body). "Undefended" is fine and even load-bearing; it's the clinical interior report that reads authored-from-outside.

**ALTERNATIVES**

*a.* "He came up over her before she was done, while she was still coming down, still open, still given over to her body, undefended. He had decided this beforehand."

*b.* "He came up over her before she was done, the aftershocks still going through her, still given over to her body, undefended. He had decided this beforehand."

*c.* "He came up over her before she was done, while the clenching still had her, still shaking with it, still given over to her body, undefended. He had decided this beforehand."

**CUT OPTION** — Strong here. Drop the physiological clause entirely and the sentence loses nothing it needs — "undefended" already carries the opportunism, and "before she was done" already carries the timing: "He came up over her before she was done, still given over to her body, undefended. He had decided this beforehand." This is the cleanest of the three fixes.

## Passage 3

> "it took her all at once — she came, hard, her sex clenching around him, her legs drawing him in, her arms coming up to lock him against her, far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it. […] She had wanted it, and her body had said so before she could make the wanting mean anything safer."

**DIAGNOSIS** — Two things. The orgasm-on-a-whisper as proof-of-power is a porn-logic beat, but note it's already partly defused by "far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it" — the text itself flags the implausibility and attributes the cause to the *question*, not his cock, which is more interesting than the standard trope. The sharper flag is the closing sentence: "her body had said so" ratified by the narration as truer than her speech. That's the male-authored move — body-as-confession endorsed as fact.

**ALTERNATIVES** (for the closing sentence; the orgasm clause I'd keep or lightly trim)

*a.* "She had wanted it — or her body had, and had said so ahead of her, before she could make the wanting mean anything safer. He took the body's answer for the true one. He usually did."

*b.* "Her body had answered before she could, and answered in a way he chose to read as the wanting getting out before she could dress it as anything safer. He might have been wrong. He did not think he was wrong."

*c.* "She had wanted it. That was how he read the clench and the too-fast arrival of it: the wanting saying itself before she could make it mean anything safer. It was the reading he wanted to be true, and it felt true, and he let the two be one thing."

For the orgasm clause itself, a light trim of the anatomical stack reads more female-authored without softening: "it took her all at once — she came, hard, her legs drawing him in, her arms coming up to lock him against her, far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it."

**CUT OPTION** — Trimming "her sex clenching around him" from the list (keeping "her legs drawing him in, her arms coming up to lock him against her") de-catalogues the orgasm and beats a full rewrite of that clause. But the closing sentence needs the recast, not a cut — deleting it loses the dramatic-irony seed that Passage 4 pays off. Combine: trim the clause, recast the sentence.

## Passage 4

> "She had not taken back the wanting — she could not, her body had said it twice and she knew he'd heard. She had moved it. She had handed the appetite to him and made herself its generous instrument: not a woman who hungered for another woman, which was unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act. A different owner."

**DIAGNOSIS** — I'd contest this flag, mostly. The design note already sees why: this is free indirect discourse doing dramatic irony — his confident reading of her *is* the thing the book is arraigning, and "unsurvivable" is so obviously his projection onto her interior that the narration is not ratifying it, it's exhibiting it. The one word that leaks from irony into endorsement is "beautiful"/"generous instrument" territory elsewhere, but within this span the only real seam is "she could not" — a flat narration-fact about what's possible for her. Put a hair of air there and the hinge sharpens rather than dulls, because his certainty becomes visibly *his*.

**ALTERNATIVES**

*a.* "She had not taken back the wanting — she couldn't, he was sure; her body had said it twice and she knew he'd heard. So she had moved it. As he read her, she had handed the appetite to him and made herself its generous instrument: not a woman who hungered for another woman, which he took to be unsurvivable for her, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act, to his eye. A different owner."

*b.* "She had not taken back the wanting — how could she, with her body having said it twice and no doubt in her that he'd heard. She had moved it instead. That was his name for what she did: handed the appetite to him, made herself its generous instrument — not a woman who hungered for another woman, which he was certain she'd find unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act. A different owner. He was pleased with the reading, and did not examine the pleasure."

*c.* (near-minimal, if the panel wants the hinge left almost intact) "She had not taken back the wanting — she could not, her body had said it twice and she knew he'd heard. She had moved it, or so he read it: handed the appetite to him and made herself its generous instrument — not a woman who hungered for another woman, which he was sure would be unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act. A different owner."

**CUT OPTION** — No. Cutting anything here damages the load-bearing irony; the passage's power is the completeness of his confidence. The repair, if any, is a few inserted markers of attribution ("as he read her," "he was sure"), not excision. A defensible answer is to leave it and note the flag as a false positive.

## Passage 5

> "Tall, and built on a lavish scale, lush in a way that had nothing curated about it and nothing apologized for — full breasts, a deep waist, hips with real width to them, the kind of ripe hourglass the year's clothes were not cut for and that did not care."

**DIAGNOSIS** — The appraisal is the scene's grammar and stays; the tell is the itemized triad "full breasts, a deep waist, hips with real width" — the proportion catalogue, measured part by part like a spec sheet, which is the reflex a woman writing this same appraising male gaze tends to render as a total impression or as effect-on-the-viewer rather than an enumerated list. The framing clauses ("nothing apologized for," "the year's clothes were not cut for and that did not care") are exactly the lived-sizing knowledge the discounting model caught — keep them.

**ALTERNATIVES**

*a.* "Tall, and built on a lavish scale, lush in a way that had nothing curated about it and nothing apologized for — all of her generous at once, the kind of ripe hourglass the year's clothes were not cut for and that did not care."

*b.* "Tall, and built on a lavish scale, lush in a way that had nothing curated about it and nothing apologized for — breasts and waist and the real width of her hips all of a piece, none of it dieted at, the kind of ripe hourglass the year's clothes were not cut for and that did not care."

*c.* "Tall, and built on a lavish scale, lush in a way that had nothing curated about it and nothing apologized for — a body that filled its own outline and then some, ripe where the year's clothes were cut to deny ripeness, and unbothered by the denial."

**CUT OPTION** — Viable and possibly best for this book: delete the triad, keep the frame. "Tall, and built on a lavish scale, lush in a way that had nothing curated about it and nothing apologized for — the kind of ripe hourglass the year's clothes were not cut for and that did not care." The em-dash elaboration was the only catalogue; removing it leaves the appraisal fully intact and loses only the inventory.

## Passage 6

> "the shoulders met, Randi sleek and narrow against the redhead's lush curves and the half-head of height she had on her"

**DIAGNOSIS** — Partly contestable. The physical contrast is doing legitimate blocking work — it's the geometry of the engineered collision, and "sleek and narrow" / "half-head of height" are spatial, not appraising. The single word that tips it into two-bodies-composed-for-the-watcher is "lush curves," which re-invokes the Passage-5 appraisal vocabulary at the exact moment the prose should be watching an event, not re-cataloguing the woman. Swap that and the contrast reads as choreography rather than a diptych staged for him.

**ALTERNATIVES**

*a.* "the shoulders met, Randi sleek and narrow against the fuller woman and the half-head of height she had on her"

*b.* "the shoulders met, Randi sleek and narrow going into all that abundance, and the half-head of height the redhead had on her"

*c.* "the shoulders met, small sharp Randi against the size and softness of the other woman, the half-head of height she had on her"

**CUT OPTION** — Clean trim wins: drop the appraisal noun and keep the spatial fact. "the shoulders met, Randi sleek and narrow against the redhead and the half-head of height she had on her." The contrast, the height differential, and the "smaller woman seemed the one put upon" payoff all survive; only the restaged "lush curves" is gone.
