Most candidate "tells" are simply this POV faithfully executed: Vee's hyper-attention to her friends' micro-reactions ("a shift in the air of the room," "Vee felt it more than saw it") could be characterization of an emotionally literate observer of any gender. What pushes the score positive is not the observation itself but what the narration *values*: silence as kindness, emotional triage over truth-telling, friendship maintenance as the chapter's moral climax. A male author writing this same POV would more likely resolve the scene toward the data or the father's wrongdoing than toward the ethics of not saying the name.

### Rationale

The usable evidence is all structural: the chapter's emotional economy (attunement, protective silence, friend-caretaking as the highest good) is rendered with the confidence of lived interior knowledge, and the prose's texture ("a breath a beat too long and too level, a breath with work in it") privileges felt social atmosphere over external spectacle. That confidence across multiple independent scenes earns +20 rather than higher; the evidence is real but stylistic rather than diagnostic, and a highly empathetic male author imitating a female POV could in principle produce it.
