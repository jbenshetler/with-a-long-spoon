### Tells toward male-authored

- "She was a small thing, five feet of her and every inch in motion even standing still, a round face gone a little sallow under makeup. Too much of it, slightly off" — a brief external inventory of a woman's appearance from the POV character's eye, but it is brisk, affectionate, and culminates in interiority ("worn like she'd dare you to mention it"), so it moves little.

### Tells toward female-authored

- "call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas." She did the sing-song and then killed it flat. "Ironic. Isn't it." — Sheri's grief about estrangement from her family is rendered from inside, with the social pressure ("people who've never lost anything think grief is a choice") carrying emotional and relational consequence, not spectacle.
- "She slid the plates back to true and drew the shake to her by its base like something owed. ... Sheri ate like she rode, all of her folded down into it, no ceremony and no apology" — lived, unselfconscious appetite; a woman eating without performance or apology, rendered with bodily specificity.
- "She reached over, stole a fistful of his fries, and ate them." — the easy physical idiom of a close female friendship, given without sexual framing.
- "She looked at me like I was one more of your harem applicants." — a woman's social read of another woman, wry and status-aware, voiced by a female character with authorial precision.

### POV notes

- "Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you" and "Yeah. The one with the big tits." — superficially male-gaze material, but it is placed squarely in Sheri's mouth as ribbing, and the text marks it as provocation ("His face was hot and he knew it"). The author keeps the male POV's desire offstage and indirect ("The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles"), then undercuts it. This is characterization and dialogue, not authorial gaze.
- "the curvy one" / "that backside" — same discount: female-voiced teasing, not narrator inventory.

### Rationale

The chapter's POV is male, so the test is whether the women are inventoried from outside, and they largely are not: Sheri's appearance gets one economical paragraph that resolves into her defiance, while her grief, appetite, friendship, and social intelligence are rendered from inside with lived mechanics (the shake, the fries, the "folder" she closes). The strongest signal is the handling of Sheri's estrangement and the emotional arithmetic of Vee's ultimatum — consequences and shame carried interpersonally rather than visually — plus the narrator's sensory interoception ("the kaiser gave under his teeth... the cold crunch of the lettuce") displacing erotic choreography entirely. The male-gaze candidate lines are all safely diegetic, spoken by a female character to needle the protagonist. This suggests a female or female-adjacent authorial sensibility, though a disciplined male writer could achieve it, so the score stays moderate rather than extreme.
