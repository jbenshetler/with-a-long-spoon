### Tells toward male-authored

- "with Pace she was a girl who had a body, not a body that happened to have a girl attached to it" — This is the only strong counter-signal: the female self defined entirely through a man's desire. But the framing ("a girl who had a body") reads as the character's own grateful self-narration, not authorial inventory.

### Tells toward female-authored

- "the whole use of the evening was in her at once, a deep worked soreness low down that she'd be aware of tomorrow and glad of" — Post-coital interoception rendered as bodily aftermath with emotional valuation, the female-authored register of sex written from inside the body rather than as spectacle.
- "it drew tight under his mouth and came up bright, with no say from her, spent as she was" — Arousal as involuntary physiological response the character notices happening to her, with a wry interiority ("Which rather spoiled her theory, and pleased her to no end").
- "the cold sane thought any girl had about a picture like this" — The lived mechanics of risk, reputation, and vulnerability around an intimate photo, taken as shared female knowledge; a male author reaching for this beat typically stages it as plot, not as inherited social reflex.
- "she brushed the wreck of her hair back into something, and put her face back on... until she'd found the version of herself she wanted him to keep" — The conscious, practiced self-composition for a male gaze, rendered from inside as labor and choice, including its own irony ("a girl who had decided to be looked at").
- "The flicker came before she could stop it, small and low and all her own, another woman at his table on a day meant for family" — Jealousy rendered as a fleeting, half-shameful bodily event, immediately managed and rationalized; this granular handling of female jealousy-as-sensation is a strong interiority tell.
- "she could take the thing apart on her tongue and tell each piece of it from the next" — Appetite and pleasure rendered from inside with sensory precision, and framed as a change in the self ("She had used to just eat") rather than as the man's skill displayed.

### POV notes

The male love interest is heavily idealized (skilled cook, tender, "grinned like a boy who'd gotten away with something"), which in a male-authored book would more likely be self-flattery; here he is observed and appraised by her, which cuts against a male author. The woman's body is described shining and arranged "like a woman in an old calendar," a candidate tell toward male gaze — but the text explicitly frames this as her own chosen fantasy ("She had wanted pinup, glamour, a girl on a calendar"), so it survives the POV defense as characterization.

### Rationale

The chapter is saturated with female-first interoception: soreness, involuntary arousal, thirst, taste, jealousy, and the social risk calculus of the photograph all arrive as felt events before they are described from outside. The one counter-signal — the protagonist's selfhood routed through Pace's desire — is voiced within her own consciousness and complicated by the text's awareness of that arrangement. The weight of independent, hard-to-fake tells (the photo anxiety, the self-arrangement ritual, the flicker of jealousy) points clearly female-ward, though not to absolute certainty.
