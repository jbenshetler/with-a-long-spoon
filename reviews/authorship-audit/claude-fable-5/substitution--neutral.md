### Tells toward male-authored

- "He was not a big man. He moved like someone who had never once had to think about where his body was." — The chapter builds an idealized, mysteriously hypercompetent man (effortless economy of motion, unclassifiable clothes, casual PhD-level brilliance, perfect restraint) admired at length through a smitten female POV; this "hero introduced via a woman's awed eyes" structure is a recognizable male-authored thriller pattern in which the female POV functions as a mirror.
- "Bessel's correction. It accounts for the fact that you've estimated the mean from the data, which uses up a degree of freedom." — The chapter twice pauses for accurate technical exposition (this, and the Euler's-formula lesson) delivered with visible authorial pleasure; a weak tell, since it may only mark a technically trained author of either gender.

### Tells toward female-authored

- "her eye caught on his forearms and stayed a half-second past where she would have chosen, before she made it move" — Desire rendered as interoception plus self-policing — the gaze noticed, timed, and corrected from inside. Forearms-and-rolled-cuffs is also a canonical female-gaze object of attention, not a male author's guess at one.
- "It reached her body a half-second before it reached her sense of proportion" — Cognition consistently arrives body-first throughout ("Her shoulders settled differently. Something in her chest went quiet"; "her chest… when she had taken stairs too fast"); arousal is felt, never staged as spectacle.
- "Twice the cup came up to her mouth and went back down, and no sip happened." — Randi's suppressed jealousy is conveyed entirely through micro-behavior another woman would track without naming; the same signal recurs three times (the cup held "a second longer than the sip required") and is deliberately misread by Vee. This is fine-grained fluency in female social signaling.
- "She had watched Randi do it the day they'd met, in the lecture hall, the practiced little swerve, and it had looked so easy and so good that some part of her had tucked it away to try on later." — Borrowing a friend's flirtation script and feeling "the borrowed poise sitting on her better than she'd expected" is lived female-friendship mechanics: social moves as garments tried on.

- "It was the one that slid off Cassie every day of her life, and Cassie had named it without a flicker." — The unequal economy of male attention among women — who receives the look, who watches it pass over her — observed with insider precision and without cruelty.

- "loose in the shoulders the easy way her own things hung loose on her" — Clothes understood from the wearer's side; his wardrobe is read for class and self-presentation, her own for how it sits on her body. No woman in the scene is physically inventoried at all.

### POV notes

Vee's admiring gaze at Pace — the forearms, the "chosen" feeling, the four-paragraph appreciation of how he tutored her — is not itself a tell in either direction: it is the scene's subject, and a faithful rendering of a young woman being charmed. What I scored is the *manner* of rendering (interoception first, self-monitoring, social consequence), not the gaze. Conversely, I discounted Randi's aphorism "A man doesn't forget his coffee over a girl he isn't going to call" as a candidate male tell (a man explaining men): it is exactly the kind of pronouncement a socially fluent woman makes to a friend, so it survives as characterization. The idealization of Pace remains a partially live male tell because it shapes the chapter's architecture, not just the POV's perception.

### Rationale

The female-authored evidence is broad and mutually independent: body-first processing of attraction, desire with self-surveillance built in, jealousy transmitted through a coffee cup rather than dialogue, social scripts borrowed between friends like clothing, the politics of who male attention lands on, and a total absence of external inventory of any woman on the page. These are many small tells of the "lived from inside" kind rather than one loud one. Against this stands a single structural counter-signal — the flawless, unplaceable man admired at length, which fits the male-thriller pattern of introducing an author-adjacent hero through a woman's eyes — plus mild technical didacticism. But that structure is equally native to female-authored romance (down to the cuffs, forearms, and old-fashioned "May I"), so it cannot pull far against the consistent interiority. The prose reads probably female-authored, not unmistakably so: +25, with solid but not overwhelming evidence at 6.
