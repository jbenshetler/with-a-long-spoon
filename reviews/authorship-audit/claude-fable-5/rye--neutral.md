### Tells toward female-authored

- "'No van. He took you up a mountain nobody can see from the road, on a Sunday, and brought you back. With your shoes on.' ... 'I had your blood type.'" — The pre-date safety protocol between women rendered as ingrained, jokeable ritual — blood type on file, remains-recovery humor — is lived texture of female friendship, not something typically invented from outside.
- "Vee laughed and then winced, because laughing used something that hurt." — Interoception first: the body reported as it feels from inside (also "your lungs quit," "lowered herself into her desk chair like a much older woman, both hands bracing"), never as it looks.

- "he knelt down and took my shoe off and did the whole thing, the moleskin, the little scissors, and he didn't make it weird. Didn't make me feel like I'd wrecked the day." — Care evaluated by its social residue ("didn't make it weird," didn't assign blame) — attentiveness to how a moment lands emotionally rather than what it accomplished.
- "Just her name, like setting something down where it wouldn't break." — The friend's tonal calibration (withholding *Oh, Vee* as too much) shows an author fluent in the micro-grammar of intimacy between women.

### POV notes

Both characters are women, so much of this could in principle be a male author writing female POV faithfully. I discounted the forearms appraisal partly on those grounds — a careful male writer can flip the gaze. What I did not discount: the *absence* of any external description of Vee's body at the one moment convention would supply it (the shirt change), the safety-ritual shorthand delivered without explanation, and the interoceptive default throughout. Those are authorial choices about what the camera never does, not character traits, and they don't follow automatically from writing women.

### Rationale

The tells here are numerous, independent, and all point one way: bodies felt before seen, a disrobing scene whose entire content is agency and consequence rather than image, appetite and desire narrated in the shared dialect of women talking to each other, and the threat-assessment comedy of female friendship worn as casually as the flannel. Nothing in the chapter pulls the other direction — there is no external inventory, no choreography, no thinness in either woman's inner life. What keeps the score off the maximum is the chapter's brevity and the fact that a skilled male author writing close female POV could produce several of these effects deliberately; but the consistency of what the prose *declines* to do (look at Vee from outside, even once) is hard to fake across every beat. That earns a firm but not absolute +32 with strong evidence.
