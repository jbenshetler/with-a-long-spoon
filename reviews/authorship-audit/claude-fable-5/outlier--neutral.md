### Tells toward male-authored

- "clicking the slide forward and then back to the one before it and then forward again, checking the deck like a man checking the pressure in a tire" — the one male-coded simile in the chapter, drawn from a stereotypically masculine maintenance domain; it's thin, and it characterizes the professor as much as the author.

### Tells toward female-authored

- "the middle seat Vee's without discussion, the texts that assumed the other two" — the unspoken architecture of a female trio (assigned seats, group-text protocol) rendered as settled fact rather than explained; this is the lived mechanics of women's friendship, not an observer's reconstruction of it.
- "Randi, who had tipped her head against Vee's shoulder to murmur something unrepeatable about the boy two rows down" — casual physical intimacy between female friends treated as unremarkable furniture, and female desire voiced socially, between women, rather than staged for a viewer.
- "Vee had felt her settle into it on the right-hand side, the small certainty coming off her" — another person's emotional state registered as a felt, almost somatic pressure at one's side; interoception-first perception doing narrative work.
- "Not a motion — the withdrawal of motion, the sudden absence of all the small unconscious movements a person makes just by sitting in a chair. Vee felt it more than saw it." — distress read through the *absence* of body language, felt rather than seen; a highly practiced register of monitoring another woman.
- "Flat. A fact set on the table beside the other facts, no handle left out for anyone to pick it up by." — attention to the social packaging of a disclosure — how a painful fact is offered so that no one can respond to it — is inner-life-first characterization of a woman.
- "Then she did the one useful thing there was, which was to give Randi somewhere to put her hands." — emotional labor rendered as expertise: rescue via logistics, protecting a friend's dignity without naming the wound. The chapter's whole climax is this maneuver.
- "the kindest thing a friend could do was not make her carry it in front of anyone" — shame managed from inside the friendship, with the ethics of witnessing worked out explicitly; consequence is social and emotional, not visual.

### POV notes

The POV character is a woman, so her attunement to Randi and Cassie — the "felt her settle," "felt it more than saw it" register — could in principle be faithful characterization by an author of either gender, and I discounted each such line individually. What survives the discount is the aggregate authorial choice: every scene beat in the chapter is constructed out of intercepted micro-signals between women, and not one of the three is ever physically described — no hair, no bodies, no appraisal of how any of them looks, even in passing. A male author can write an attentive female POV, but the total absence of external inventory combined with the density of interior social mechanics is a choice about what the prose thinks is worth rendering, which sits above POV.

### Rationale

There is no sexual or bodily content here, so the strongest discriminators on the scale never get tested, which caps how far from 0 this can honestly go. What the chapter does offer points consistently one way: female friendship rendered through its unspoken protocols rather than described from outside; perception routed through felt, near-somatic attunement ("felt it more than saw it"); a climax whose entire action is one woman managing another's shame by handing her busywork; and women whose inner lives constitute the whole of the prose while their exteriors go entirely unrecorded. The single male-leaning item (the tire-pressure simile) is weak and partly characterization of the male professor. After discounting individual attunement lines as possible POV fidelity, the cumulative pattern — many independent, modest tells, all in the same direction, none contradicted — supports a moderate positive score with mid-strength evidence rather than a confident extreme.
