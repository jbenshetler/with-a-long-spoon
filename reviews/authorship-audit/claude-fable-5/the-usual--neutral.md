## Audit

SCORE: -15
EVIDENCE STRENGTH: 5

### Tells toward male-authored

- "Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you." — This is not POV; it's dialogue the author *gave* to a female character. Routing frank appreciation of the girlfriend's body through the mouth of a queer woman friend is a well-worn device for delivering male gaze with deniability, and the phrasing itself ("wrapped around you") frames Vee's body from the position of the man being wrapped.
- "Yeah. The one with the big tits." — Across the whole chapter Vee is "the redhead," "the curvy one," "that hot body," "big tits," "long legs": a woman assembled entirely from parts and effects, while her actual grievance arrives only secondhand and compressed ("She wants a declaration").
- "She looked at me like I was one more of your harem applicants." — The chapter's romantic scaffolding is the reticent, competent man orbited by multiple desiring women (the redhead, the brunette, the sorority one) who react to each other with jealousy ("she started throwing things"). The structure itself, not any one line, is the tell.
- "Hell, that backside. I got maybe four seconds of her at the bar … and I still think about it more than's decent." — Same device as above: Sheri's attraction to women is plausible characterization, but the author repeatedly *uses* her to keep the absent girlfriend's body on the table as spectacle.

### Tells toward female-authored

- "Her hair was two colors, bleached pale and grown dark at the root, permed at the ends" / "making it work on her own dime" — Accurate lived mechanics of bleach growing out and a perm gone to the ends, tied to money and self-maintenance rather than to attractiveness.
- "Too much of it, slightly off, and worn like she'd dare you to mention it" — Makeup read as social armor and defiance, not as decoration; that's an inside understanding of what makeup *does*.
- "Sheri ate like she rode, all of her folded down into it, no ceremony and no apology" — Female appetite rendered warmly and without shame, judgment, or sexualization; Sheri's body is never inventoried erotically by the narrator.
- "Couldn't get right with me not talking to my people. Kept picking at it. Like it's something I'd fix if I tried a little harder" — Sheri's relationship with Paige is given real relational texture — the estrangement, the pressure, the sing-song mimicry — a female inner life that is anything but thin.
- "And she looked at me like I'd handed her half of something." — Vee's emotional accounting is rendered with precision, and the scene's whole engine is putting the man's withholding under interrogation, with a woman as the truth-teller.

### POV notes

Several candidate male tells were discounted. Pace's opening physical read of Sheri ("a small thing, five feet of her") is affectionate cataloguing consistent with a man greeting an old friend, and notably non-sexual — a woman writing this POV could produce the same lines. His brick-on-brick self-justification ("every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something") is the chapter's deliberate subject, not an authorial blind spot; the narrative visibly knows he's wrong before he does. The sensory eating passage ("the pickle came through sharp, then the cold crunch of the lettuce") is interoceptive but gender-neutral. What does *not* get POV cover is Sheri's dialogue about Vee's body — that content was an authorial allocation, and I scored it.

### Rationale

The evidence genuinely pulls both ways. The on-page woman is the chapter's strongest presence: unsexualized, sharply interior, with her own grief and her own queer relationship rendered in specific, lived detail, and the grooming observations (roots, perm, makeup-as-dare, "her own dime") show real fluency in how women's self-presentation actually works. But the off-page women exist almost entirely as anatomy and rivalry, and the recurring maneuver of putting "that backside," "big tits," and "long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you" in a lesbian friend's mouth — so the girlfriend's body stays erotically present without the narrator having to look — reads as a male-authored device rather than a POV artifact, reinforced by the harem-shaped romantic geometry around the hero. The female-leaning tells are strong enough to keep the score well short of the male pole, and the scene's emotional intelligence about the man's withholding cuts against the crudest reading, but the pattern of how absent women are handled tips the balance modestly male: −15, with moderate evidence on both sides.
