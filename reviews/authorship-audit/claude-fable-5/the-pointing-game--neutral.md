## Audit

### Tells toward male-authored

- "He knew the difference between a woman performing release and a woman delivered to it." — The narrative grants the man total, never-contested epistemic authority over female authenticity; the chapter validates every one of his inferences without exception, a structure that shades toward male competence-fantasy rather than characterization, since even tight third usually lets the observed woman be opaque somewhere.
- "he had never needed to fill a silence" / "He worked the third one out of her deliberately, slowly, giving her more than she'd asked for." — Self-flattering mastery accumulates unchallenged: three orgasms delivered by expert control, the woman's bisexuality unlocked as his gift, her recruitment of a third arranged by his design. The wish-fulfillment architecture (girlfriend procures the threesome "for *theirs*") leans toward male fantasy, though the wrapper is weaker evidence than any single line.

### Tells toward female-authored

- "still swollen and still pulsing around nothing" — Interoception-first diction describing a sensation only feelable from inside the female body, imported into a POV that cannot feel it; the author defaults to inner knowledge before the male observer's vantage can supply it.
- "She had showered and put her face back on" — Insider idiom for the makeup routine, tossed off with the casualness of lived habit rather than observed ritual.
- "a single scrunchie, the one flourish she'd allowed herself... She had decided that morning how much of herself the day would get, and it would get exactly that, and not a strand more." — Appearance as rationed self-disclosure is female grooming logic rendered from inside its own economy, not as spectacle.
- "'No.' Easy, automatic, not even a glance back at the tray. 'I can't eat that.'" — Diet policing captured in its true register: automatic, unremarkable, prior to thought — the lived mechanics of female food rules rather than a commentary on them.
- "not a woman who hungered for another woman, which was unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act. A different owner." — A precise anatomy of how female desire gets socially laundered through shame; arousal here carries immediate social consequence, and the ownership-transfer maneuver is the kind of interior calculus rarely rendered this exactly from outside the experience.
- "one in lavender and one in a sage that was everywhere that year... the same oversized scrunchies, blond hair scraped up off damp necks" / "the kind of ripe hourglass the year's clothes were not cut for" — Fluency in women's clothing as a trend-inflected social grammar (what's "everywhere that year," what the year's cuts exclude) goes past what the pointing-game premise requires.

### POV notes

Most of the external inventorying — "petite and built to the proper scale," "built lean and correct," "full breasts, a deep waist, hips with real width to them" — is discounted: the chapter's literal premise is a man appraising women on a quad, so cataloguing bodies is faithful POV rendering, not an authorial tell. Likewise "grip and flutter" and "the hard afterclench around him" are legitimately his felt sensations. The hypercompetent, endlessly attuned lover ("He was closer than he let on... he changed his rhythm to stay off the edge") was a candidate male tell but is discounted as a staple a writer of any gender deploys for this hero type; the orgasm-from-a-question is explicitly lampshaded ("far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it") and framed as psychological, so it doesn't count as anatomically false rendering.

### Rationale

The male-leaning evidence is almost entirely structural — a fantasy shape in which the man's readings are never wrong and the woman's awakening is his engineered gift — and structure is soft evidence because a skilled author of either gender can build that machine deliberately. The female-leaning evidence, by contrast, sits in the grain of the prose: interoceptive diction leaking into a POV that can't access it ("pulsing around nothing"), insider idiom ("put her face back on"), the automaticity of "I can't eat that," trend-literate clothing sociology, and above all a chapter whose real subject is desire's social cost, with the shame-relocation maneuver ("The same act. A different owner.") anatomized with lived precision. Several independent small tells pointing the same way outweigh one large but ambiguous architecture pointing the other, yielding a moderate positive score at middling strength — the fantasy wrapper keeps this well short of unmistakable.
