REACTION —

Oh, this one got me in exactly the tender, awful place where wanting becomes spending.

The first half I loved almost helplessly. Randi in the dorm room is such a charge because she makes Vee’s ordinary life visible by entering it. The cinderblock, the bunk beds, the Stevie Nicks poster suddenly looking smaller because Randi is there: yes. That is precisely the kind of social magic Randi has, and it’s also the danger of her. She doesn’t just admire Vee; she rearranges the room around what Vee might become.

And the dress. God. “He made it to you” is the cleanest, cruelest, most perfect thing Randi could have said. Of course she has the word for it. Of course she does. Pace made the thing, but Randi names it, and Vee needs both of those forms of attention so badly that I can feel myself getting nervous on her behalf. Randi understands the dress not as an object but as an address, a message written on Vee’s body. That’s intimate. Almost too intimate. And Vee receives the naming like a second fitting.

The cheap shoe store scene made me happy in a different way, because for a few pages they were just girls. Truly just girls. Laughing over ugly shoes, insulting each other’s feet, getting stupid in public. I needed that. I needed Vee to be funny, not only desirable. I needed Randi to crack into a real laugh that “costs her something to give.” That line matters to me because it lets Randi be more than a manipulator in good clothes. It’s why I can’t simply hate her, though I keep trying at strategic intervals.

But then the boutique. Oh, my stomach. The shoeing scene is hot, yes, absolutely, but it’s also so loaded I almost wanted to look away. Randi on one knee with Vee’s foot in her hands, the thumb across the plum toenail, Vee’s hand flying to Randi’s shoulder: that is not friendship. That is not even plausibly pretending to be friendship anymore, except Vee can still somehow pretend because the whole world keeps handing her softer names for what is happening. Randi knows. Randi knows exactly what she’s doing. I don’t know if she knows how much it will cost Vee, but she knows where her hand is.

And then the credit card. That was the part that actually scared me. Not because shoes are immoral, or because beauty should be denied, but because the book has made me feel Vee’s money all along, so I felt the floor go out when she put the debit card back. The line about there being no paying-it-back part yet: there it is. That’s the chapter’s knife. Randi can turn a want into a debt and make the debt feel like loyalty to the dress, to Pace, to the version of Vee who walks in late and stops the room. And Vee does it herself. That is the awful brilliance. Nobody takes the card out of her wallet. Every yes, every yes.

I’m still in, but I’m more uneasy than thrilled by the end. The sidewalk laughter is sweet, genuinely sweet, and I believe Vee when she thinks she’s never had a friend like this. But I also want to text Cassie a photograph of the receipt and say: please look up from whatever field you’re on and count.
