Oh, this one got me in the stomach in a way I half resent because it was so beautifully set up. I knew the dancing was going to do something to her, but I didn’t expect it to become such a clean little machine for all of her worst and best instincts: the needing to be led, the terror of being one of many, the instant shame when she realizes she has wanted something before she had permission to want it.

The lesson on the concrete pad was the part I loved most before it all went bad. “Being jelly doesn’t work.” Of course. Of course that is the whole chapter, maybe the whole book right now. She can’t collapse and call that trust; she also can’t hold herself three inches away and call that safety. The frame has rules, and the rules let her move. I felt that physically. The way she finally stops anticipating him and the dance comes through his body into hers — that was hot, yes, but more than hot. It made me understand why she keeps going back to him. He doesn’t just desire her. He gives her a structure in which she can feel herself doing the thing.

And then the bar. I was so happy for about thirty seconds. Pace having a whole room, a history, people who love him outside the warm house. That mattered to me. I want Vee’s world wider, and I want his world wider too; I don’t want him to exist only as this miraculous domestic-sex machine waiting behind his door. But the minute the women started hugging him I could feel Vee’s mother coming up the stairs inside her. The blonde was cruel in that terribly plausible way, not even necessarily lying, just pressing exactly where it would bruise. “Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to” is such a poisoned version of what Vee had just experienced as trust.

I hated watching Vee turn on him, but I believed every second of it. That’s the awful thing. The line about being “just next” is so nakedly her wound talking that I wanted to put the book down and also couldn’t. She has been special with him, unmistakably. But the fear that specialness is only a trick men do to girls is not stupid. It is wrong about Pace in this moment, I think, but not stupid. And because the book lets me know both things at once, I stayed with her even while I wanted to drag her outside by the shoulders and say, look at what you are doing.

Pace leaving instead of chasing her was quietly devastating. I think if he had followed, she would have had more to fight. He just took the hit, set the beer down, and left. There is a dignity in that that hurt me. And then the bartender, bless her, gives Vee the facts without petting her. “Wasn’t him they were jealous of, honey.” Whew. That line snapped the room into a different shape. Same numbers, different grouping — yes, Professor Simpson, I heard you. I love when this book repeats a lesson without underlining it to death.

The cab home made me feel young in the worst way. The bad back seat, the fare being more than she guessed, not calling Cassie because then it becomes real. I know that species of mistake: the one you are too ashamed to narrate while it is still warm. And then putting on his washed shirt, the one that no longer smells like him. That was mean. Perfectly mean. She wants the proof, but even the proof has gone neutral.

I am very keyed up by the fact that she’s going to Randi. Not Pace. Randi. That feels exactly right and also dangerous as hell, because Randi is the person who can translate Vee to herself, but Randi is not neutral ground. Randi already wants her, already wants “us,” already has her own jealousies and hungers braided through this. If Vee goes to her raw and ashamed and asking how to fix what she did to Pace, Randi can be generous, or she can steer, or both at once. That is what scares me: with Randi those are not opposites.
