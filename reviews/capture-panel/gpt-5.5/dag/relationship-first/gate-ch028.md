REACTION —

Oh, this one got under my skin in exactly the way I’m most vulnerable to: not the big scene, but the little mechanics of learning to trust a hand at your back. The dancing lesson is so obvious as metaphor that I should have resisted it, and I didn’t, because the book made it bodily first. “Being jelly doesn’t work.” “Close it.” “Don’t think. Just feel the lead.” I could feel Vee fighting not just the dance but the whole idea that she can be held inside a structure and still be choosing. That’s the central knot, isn’t it? The thing that thrills her is also the thing that could undo her.

And I loved Meg here. Briefly, practically, usefully. The book keeps giving Vee these small female economies — boots passed down the hall, shirts judged in mirrors, a friend who knows where ropers live. It matters so much that Vee’s life is not only Pace and Randi’s warm triangulation machine. Even two pages of Meg makes the campus real again.

The Rusty Nail scene made my stomach drop before Vee’s did. Not because I believed Pace had been secretly awful, exactly, but because I believed the room. Of course he has a world. Of course women know him. Of course the man who makes Vee feel singular has made other women feel held and safe and light. That is not betrayal, but to Vee, with her mother already loaded like a gun inside her, it feels like proof. “You’d be one of them” is such a cruel little sentence because it uses ordinary reality against her. A good man has a history. A generous man has been generous before. And shame can turn even generosity into evidence.

I hated watching her hurt him, but I believed every inch of it. The “service” line is awful in that exact way where you hear yourself say it and part of you is already wincing and another part is relieved to have finally drawn blood. And Pace leaving instead of chasing her felt right and painful. I’m glad he didn’t perform wounded nobility or force the correction. He just went. That restraint hurt worse.

The blonde worried me for half a second as a plot device, but the bartender saved it for me. Not because she exonerated Pace — though she did — but because she clarified the room’s social weather. “Wasn’t him they were jealous of, honey.” Delicious. Devastating. Vee thinks she has been exposed as ordinary, when the room has been reading her as the exception. I could have kissed that woman behind the bar for being so dry and so final.

And then the ending. God. Vee in his laundered shirt, smelling fabric softener instead of him, lying awake with both truths: she was wrong, and the thing she learned from him still lives in her body. That is the kind of aftermath I need. Not instant reconciliation, not a stormy apology scene immediately cashed in, but the long sick night of having been unfair to someone you love and knowing it.

Going to Randi’s is interesting. Not Cassie. Not Pace. Randi. That makes me nervous in the good way and the bad way. Randi can absolutely help her name what happened. Randi can also steer this, because Randi has an interest in every road that leads through her. I want the next chapter badly, but I am watching the hands on the wheel.
