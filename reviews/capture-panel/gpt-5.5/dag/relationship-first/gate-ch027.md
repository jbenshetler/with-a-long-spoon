Oh, this one got me in the quiet place. Not the flinching, hot, hand-over-mouth place, though there is still plenty of body in it. The deeper one. The place where two people have talked long enough that touching starts to feel like part of the conversation instead of its reward.

I loved this chapter. I loved the couch, the quilt, the cold against the glass, the tea going undrunk because talking has become the thing. This is exactly the kind of chapter I stay up for: food, family, names, old injuries brought out sideways, the erotic charge of being listened to properly. Pace’s hand on her breast while they talk could so easily have felt like authorial showing-off, but it didn’t. It felt like the physical grammar of their room now: he can hold her and not turn the whole scene toward taking. He can want her without interrupting her.

And Vee. God, Vee. The way she talks about food and Ohio and casseroles and unopened saffron made me ache with recognition, even though that is not my childhood exactly. She is funny here without performing. “Whatever was in it may have been old enough to vote” is the kind of line you say when you’re comfortable enough not to polish yourself. And Pace receives the affection under the joke before he receives the joke. That is why she keeps opening. That is why I believe her.

The “Peter” reveal was so small and so enormous. Pace suddenly having a plain name underneath the almost allegorical one made him feel younger to me, and more breakable. Peter is someone’s son. Peter got sent into the hall with worksheets. Peter was fifteen in a dormitory full of men. I felt Vee recalibrating him in real time, not losing desire, but adding tenderness and alarm to it.

Daphne. There it is, finally, and I am uncomfortable in exactly the way I want the book to make me uncomfortable. Because Pace tells it like a reasonable story about first love and timing and nobody doing anything wrong, and Vee hears the other story inside it. So did I. The chapter does not force the word onto the page, and I’m glad it doesn’t, because the more devastating thing is that he is still protecting the memory. “He had handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck” is the sentence I would text someone at 1 a.m. with no context and then throw the book facedown on my chest. That is the book knowing more than it says. That is the stuff.

The math passage worked on me more than I expected. I am usually wary when a novel makes the brilliant man explain the beautiful universe, but here it didn’t feel like TED-talk seduction. It felt like the first time he has been able to show her the room he actually lives in. And she does not have to understand the math to understand him. That matters. He turns knowledge toward her “like light,” and yes, fine, I am gone too.

The scar not being told yet was perfect. I wanted it, and I was relieved not to get it. “Not tonight” with her hand still held over it is much better than a reveal. It keeps the trust alive without pretending trust means total access. That felt adult and strangely erotic.

And then the last paragraph quietly breaks my heart because Vee is lying there feeling chosen by fortune, by accident, by the universe arranging itself kindly around her, and I am sitting here with the jacket in my lap knowing it was not accident. Randi was not just stumbled into. Pace did not just cross a coffee shop for math, or not only. The chapter lets Vee call it “one piece,” and I can feel the secret underneath that phrase like a fault line. This happiness is real. That is the problem. Every time the book makes me believe in the tenderness, it makes the plan worse.
