REACTION —

Oh, this one got under my skin in a quieter, more complicated way than the last one.

First: I loved Vee being late and not caring. I loved that stupid, floating, slept-with-and-adored happiness where the whole ordinary day cannot touch you. Her made bed tattling on her made me smile so hard, and Cassie catching it immediately felt exactly right. Cassie remains the person in this book I trust with a flashlight. She does not care about the dress, not really; she cares whether Vee is happy, whether the glow is safe, whether the thing that happened to her was good. That little “Was it a good night?” did more for me than a page of best-friend dialogue would have. Cassie is not dazzled, and thank God.

And then Randi. God. I am in such trouble with Randi, because when she is good she is so good. “He made it to you” is the kind of sentence that makes me forgive her for about ten minutes. She sees the exact emotional truth of the dress, not just that it is beautiful, not just that Pace is talented, but that it is an address, a love letter in silk. And Vee needs someone to name that for her. Pace makes the thing, Cassie checks the girl, Randi gives the feeling language. I understand completely why Vee feels like she has never had a friend like this.

But I could feel the old danger all through the shoe shopping. Not danger like “run,” exactly. More like the floor tilting. Randi is generous, attentive, funny, sensual, socially fluent, and she knows how to move a person from one room into another before they have admitted they wanted to go. The cheap shoe store scene was honestly adorable. Vee making Randi laugh for real made me melt. I wanted that for both of them. I loved Vee feeling funny and chosen and equal for a second. But then the boutique arrives, and suddenly Vee is standing inside money she does not have, swallowing the shape of her bank account, and Randi is kneeling in front of her with Vee’s foot in her hands.

That foot moment. Whew. That was not friendship, or not only friendship, and everybody in the room except maybe Vee’s conscious mind knows it. The thumb over the plum toenail? The “Mm”? Her hand shooting to Randi’s shoulder? That had charge. It was small, but it went straight through me. And because the book has trained me to care about consent and interiority, what matters is that Vee feels it. It is not just Randi performing glamour at her. Vee’s body answers. Still, Randi is leading her again, and this time into spending money she cannot afford. That is the part that made my stomach tighten.

I do not think Randi meant harm in a simple villain way. I think she believed the shoes were right. I think she was right, aesthetically and maybe emotionally. The tan heel would have been an apology. But Randi can afford for beauty to be mandatory. Vee cannot. Watching Randi buy shoes several times more expensive without looking while Vee puts hers on a credit card and hides the flinch was the first time in a while I felt genuinely protective in a bad way, not sexy-protective. The class difference is not decorative here. It has teeth.

And Simpson’s paradox sitting in the middle of all this is not subtle, but I did not mind it. Same numbers, different grouping, different truth. Vee has Cassie and Randi on either side and reads it as being loved from both directions. Maybe it is. Also maybe if you separate the moments, every moment is freely chosen and lovely: the brunch, the dress, the shoe, the hand on her foot, the credit card, the laughter. Put them together and something else appears. That is the problem. That has always been the problem.

I still want the induction desperately. I want Randi to see Vee in the full thing with the shoes. I want Pace to see it too, maybe from across a room, and I want him wrecked. But I am uneasy now in a way I was not after the dress chapter. Not because the heat failed. Because the seduction widened. It is not just Pace and Vee’s body anymore. It is money, friendship, taste, status, Randi’s hunger, Vee’s hunger to belong, all braided together. I am reading on, absolutely, but I am watching Randi’s hands.
