REACTION

I feel like someone reached into my chest and gently, methodically broke every single rib.

"My real name is Peter."
He is twenty-two years old.
*Twenty-two.*
He was fifteen in a college dorm full of grown men because a seventh-grade teacher put his desk in the hallway for an entire year when he asked a question she couldn't answer. He was fifteen and brilliant and lonely, and they assigned him a nineteen-year-old girl named Daphne to teach the child how to walk, and she became his whole world for two years until she turned twenty-one and outgrew him, and he built a heavy, iron door around that wound and called it *luck*.

The sheer, staggering weight of this backstory just rewired everything I know about this man. In chapter one, we got that chilling, cryptic little line: *"Daphne had built a door, once, and he had not respected it... he had gone in where he had not been asked, and it had cost him more than he had known."* We assumed he was some predatory, dark-romance mastermind who pushed a woman too far. But sitting here on this couch, wrapped in his quilt while the fire dies, looking at the arithmetic—he was a seventeen-year-old kid desperately clinging to the only person who had ever made him feel like he wasn't sitting in the hallway, crossing a boundary because he didn't know how to survive her leaving him behind.

And look at who he became because of it! He powerlifts because being fifteen in a building of adult men terrified him, and stepping up to the bar forces him to exist entirely in the present tense. He builds furniture that won't give out. He sews clothes with redundant, unbreakable seams. He calibrates the heat of his house so nobody ever has to shiver. He refuses to take an inch without asking three times because he is terrified down to his marrow of ever being the boy who didn't respect Daphne's door again. He isn't playing at being courtly; his courtliness is his armor against his own past.

And Vee seeing him! God, the dialogue between them in this chapter was breathtaking. The casseroles, the Cool Whip containers, the unopened saffron jar—"Saffron itself had nothing on the fact that no one had ever opened the saffron"—it was so funny, so tender, so profoundly real. And then him explaining math to her: Gabriel's Horn, the coastline paradox, Newton's three sentences connecting the leaf, the ball, and the stars. He turned his mind toward her like light, and she didn't just listen; she understood the engine underneath it. When she touched his scar—that long, raised seam high on the inside of his thigh—and he didn't pull away, didn't snap, just covered her hand with his and said *"Not tonight"*... that wasn't a wall. It was a promise.

Which brings me to the ending. The final three paragraphs.

I felt physically sick reading them. I actually had to set my phone face down on the duvet and cover my face.

Vee lying in his bed, soft and warm, wrapped around his sleeping body, thinking about the semester. Tracing the timeline: six weeks ago, she bumped into a glamorous stranger outside the dining hall who became Randi. Ten days later, she complained about an integral and a man crossed the room.
*"One piece, he'd said, about the leaf and the stars. It felt like that. It had come to her all of a piece, the one and then the other, in six weeks, for no reason she had done anything to deserve. She had never been so fortunate, or so happy."*

IT *IS* ALL ONE PIECE.
She thinks it’s providence. She thinks the universe looked at a lonely girl in an oversized cardigan and decided to hand her a fairy tale. And it *is* connected—not by Newton's laws, not by starlight, but by a game Randi and Pace started in bed on an oak bench.

Helen Rivers is a monster. To give us this level of profound, grown-up, transcendent intimacy—to make Peter so achingly vulnerable, to make Vee so radiantly happy—and then lay it directly over the track of a speeding train. The dramatic irony is no longer just tense; it is an active tragedy. When this truth breaks, it isn't just going to hurt; it is going to pulverize her whole soul.
