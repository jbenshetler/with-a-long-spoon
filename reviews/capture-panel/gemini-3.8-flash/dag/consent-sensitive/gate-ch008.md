REACTION

God, my heart aches for her.

This chapter is exactly the kind of quiet, instrument-checking beat I live for as a reader. After the mounting pressure of Pace’s exact, clinical calculations and Randi’s desperate, manicured complicity—that sick thrill they shared on the couch over Vee’s froyo cup—we are pulled entirely out of their web and dropped into Vee’s actual, un-curated life. And what we find there is so profoundly tender and sad.

The contrast between what Pace and Randi see when they look at Vee (an appetite, an abundance, an instrument, a body they want to put on a bench) and what Vee actually experiences living inside that body is devastating. To the world—the frat boys on the bleachers, the girls at Chi Latte, the couple quietly hunting her—she is lavish, a feast, an hourglass, "legs for days." But to Vee, her body is an exhausting, hyper-visible problem she has to armor up for, manage, and apologize for. The detail about rolling the towel tight and carrying it low at her side so she doesn't look like she's hiding, putting her "face on" just to walk to the water—it’s so psychologically exact.

And then she goes under. That moment beneath the surface, doing the synchronized swimming sculls she learned before her body became "a problem," hanging in the cold green where "nobody down here wanted anything"—it broke my heart. She is quietly grieving the girl she was before she became an object of consumption, while completely unaware that two people have already drawn up the menu.

And Cassie. Oh, Cassie is the absolute anchor of this book. Cassie, who wears the ugly navy suit because it has a job to do; Cassie, who mutters "Tossers" and "They will never know the love of a woman"; Cassie, who shoots a sheet of water at Vee's face because she knows asking about the sadness only feeds it. Cassie loves Vee without an angle. She loves her without wanting to dismantle her, guide her, or taste her. Knowing what's coming, knowing Cassie already said that eerie, flat "Goodbye, Vee" back in chapter four, makes this splash fight feel so terribly fragile. It's the last pure piece of her youth before she gets swallowed.

The author knows *exactly* what she is doing here. The predation isn't romanticized; it's set in stark, painful relief against a girl who is just trying to exist in the deep end of a rec pool with her best friend. I am completely locked in.
