REACTION:

Oh, god. This is horrifying to watch. Delicious and completely sickening.

The title—*Substitution*. Like the integration technique, but also... Randi substituting herself out? Pace substituting Vee in? The sheer clinical cruelty of the setup here is breathtaking, and what makes it work so brilliantly is that the book is *fully* awake to the horror of what it's showing. The author isn't asking us to swoon at this "meet-cute"; the prose is practically vibrating with the sinister mechanics underneath.

Look at how they executed this. Randi was texting him. She summoned him to the coffee shop on Wilson ("the place on Wilson that stays open"—the very place Randi lied about to Brooke in Chapter 2!). Randi stages the whole scene, sits there playing with her coffee cup, pretending not to sip, pretending to be the bored, indulgent sorority sister watching her little friend get hit on. "Randi," said Randi. *One word.* No adjective bit, no charm offensive. Just the flat, disciplined delivery of a woman playing her mark. And the Bessel’s correction question! She tossed him a bone so he could look smart and establish his tutor bona fides, while keeping her hands completely clean.

And Pace. Pace is terrifying because he isn't playing the cartoon villain; he is playing the *perfect* man. He rolls his sleeves. He doesn't solve the problem for her—he gives her back the pencil. He asks, *"What's drawing you to it?"* He gives her the room to be brilliant, to talk about econ, to be seen for her mind, because he already knows from watching her on the quad that she starves to be seen. He uses courtesy—*"May I call you?"*—as a weapon of absolute disarming precision. He makes her feel chosen, unique, elevated.

And Vee, poor Vee, is so utterly defenseless against this specific frequency of attention. She’s an applied math girl who likes "the gap between what they say they're doing and what they're doing," and she is sitting in the middle of the widest, most calculated gap of her life and can't see an inch of it because it feels too good. "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." You can see the trap snapping shut with oiled, silent hinges, exactly like the hardware on that damn bench.

Cassie, though. God bless Cassie. "He looked at her the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." Cassie sees the seam, even if she doesn't know what cloth it's cut from yet.

I feel sick for Vee, and I am entirely hooked on the dread.
