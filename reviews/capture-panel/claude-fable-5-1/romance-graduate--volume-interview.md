**T1**

Vee. The book leaves her in a hallway with her face doing the thing, saying "*please*" attached to nothing, and a man answering "This?" — and I'm the only one in that hallway who's been on the bench. That's where I am with her: standing behind her, unable to tap her on the shoulder. What I feel is closer to protectiveness than I usually let a heroine get out of me, and it's specifically because she's *good at things* — she reads rooms, she edits herself on the bunk and I can hear the edits, she takes the hook she can see in chapter twenty-eight and says the venomous thing because she has never once been the exception and doesn't know how to be one. I've done that. And now she's started to owe — a credit card, a pair of shoes, a scarf, a name she said once under a quilt — and she thinks she's being courted and she's being *collected*, and the worst of it is that the collecting is also, genuinely, the first time anyone's looked at her stomach without her mother's word in the room. Both are true. I feel sick with tenderness for her and I'd like to be less implicated in it than I am.

Pace. I'm split exactly down the line I drew at Journal 10 and never moved off: his hands, yes; his silence, no. When he's on his knees asking "would you be bothered if I was too hard," he isn't lying, and I believe the book that that's real. But "he took the body's answer for the true one, he usually did" is in chapter three and it's never left me, because it's the whole man — he's honest with whatever's in front of him and lets that stand in for honesty. He's had a fire, a quilt, and Daphne to say Randi's name and hasn't, and the book pulled the wounded-nineteen-year-old story out of him at the exact moment I needed a reason to keep liking him, and I know I was handled and I liked him anyway. Right now what I feel is — warm, and wary, and a little angry at *myself*, like I've been laughing at a man's dinosaur voice while he tells me about a patent and not asking the question I'd ask any man in real life. He ends the volume standing at Vee's door saying the truest thing he's said to her, and I'm not sure he knows which door he's at.

Randi. God. She's the one I'd read aloud and she's the one I feel the most for, which I did not expect at the bench. Because the book let me watch her lose. "She's not ready, you'll want to take your time" — she went *still* when he described what Vee does to a room, and that stillness told me more than any of her lines. She *leaves* in fourteen and she never leaves. She's on her knees under a hem in twenty-nine running the fitting chapter with the genders changed and Vee doesn't clock it, and my face was hot, and I thought: she's not spending Vee to get the face back anymore, she wants the face *and* Vee, and there's no version of the game where she gets to keep either. I don't trust her. I don't trust her one inch. And she's the person in this book who's going to get hurt in a way the book won't let me enjoy, and I'm already bracing.

And Cassie, since you didn't ask — "goodbye, Vee" is still the saddest thing in it, and she's the one who's going to be watching when it goes.

**T2**

The room. Obviously the room. Thirty-one chapters and the three of them have never been in one, and I said in my verdict that's either a held breath or a cliffhanger sale depending on my mood, and today's mood says: held breath, but I'm counting.

But that's the thing everybody's going to say, so here's what I actually feel owed. Randi from the inside. I've had Pace's head from the bench, I've had Vee's whole head since chapter four — she's got more interiority than the last forty heroines I read combined — and Randi I get through Pace's body and Vee's lunches and one voice a beat late. The book keeps letting me *hear the seam* in her, which is a gorgeous trick, but I've now watched her go still on his chest and I want to know what the stillness was in *her* words. She got seen on that bench and she's spent an entire volume trying to buy the face back and I don't know what she saw. That's a debt.

The mother. Her word runs under every hot scene in this book — it's the fuel, it's the thing nobody writes honestly and she wrote it — and the woman never once walks in. I don't need a confrontation scene, I've read a hundred of those. I want one phone call. I want to hear the voice the word came from.

Smaller: I wanted the book to decide about the two-page chapters. Chapter thirty was a technique — somebody outside the triangle saying "two of them at once" plainly, a beat, in and out. Chapter thirteen was a shortcut, a landing I didn't need after the best Vee chapter yet. Same length, same bunk, and one of them earned it and one didn't, and by the end I still can't tell you what the *rule* is. Maybe there isn't one. That bugs me more than it should.

And the honest one, the one I'd only say to you: I was dreading that the book knew how good the fitting chapter was and wouldn't top it. Thirty-one came very close — closer than I thought it could — so the book doesn't owe me that anymore. What it owes me is *cost*. Vee sabotaged herself in twenty-eight and it cost her a night in a shirt that smelled of no one. Pace has been sabotaging for thirty-one chapters and so far it's cost him one closed door he didn't understand. I want the ledger to balance. I'll wait for it. I want it on the record that I'm waiting.

**T3**

It sits badly, and I think badly is the correct way for it to sit, and I want to push on your framing a little before I agree with you.

"Deceives Vee the entire volume and ends loved, trusted, unexposed" — yes. All true. But "nothing lands on him" isn't quite right, and the difference matters to me. Something's landed; he just doesn't know what it is. Randi walks out in chapter fourteen and he stands in the door not knowing why, and I know why, and it's awful — that's the book landing something on him and choosing not to let him feel it yet. And the last page: "You're always beautiful," to a woman who doesn't know the address of the bench she's standing on. He's the only one in that hallway who knows it's a rerun. The book handed him the truest moment he's had with Vee and made it a repeat. If that's not a cost, it's a bill with the date left blank.

Also — and I'd say this gently — your version gives Pace all the agency, and that's not the book I read. I wrote "I don't know who's running whom anymore" at twenty-six and I meant it. Randi built Vee open one *yeah* at a time at those lunches. And here's the part I have to own: *I leaned in with her.* Every lunch. I hated myself and leaned in. So when you say Pace ends the book loved and trusted and unexposed, my honest answer is: so do I. I'm the reader who wants the lunch to happen again. If I'm going to be angry at him for enjoying Vee under false pretenses, I have to look at what I was doing while I enjoyed her.

Now, where you're right. I've read — no exaggeration — a hundred KU heroes who lie for eighty percent of the book and get exposed in chapter thirty and grovel in thirty-two, and those books "land" the lie on him with a speech, and the speech is worthless, and I've come to skim those chapters the way I skim retellings. This book refused the speech. And the question you're actually asking is whether the refusal is nerve or a sales tactic, and I can't tell you from inside Volume One. Nobody can. That's what a cliffhanger *is* — it's the book asking for credit. In Journal 10 I wrote that Pace "knows he owes Vee the truth and has decided that's a Book Two problem," and I was a little sour about that at chapter ten and I'm a little sour about it now, and I bought it anyway. So the honest thing is: it changes what I'm buying, not whether. I'm not buying Book Two for more of Vee in a hallway saying please, though I want that too. I'm buying it as a *bet* — that a book with the nerve to write "grieved, a little" and not explain itself, to put the fitting chapter on the page without a single thing coming off below the waist, to let Randi go still and not tell me why — that that book has the nerve to make the exposure cost him something real, and not sand it down into a misunderstanding and a kiss.

If it sands it down, I'll be genuinely angry, and I'll tell you so, and I'll feel like the volume was a long con on *me*, not Vee. If it doesn't — if the room, when it finally happens, is as psychologically merciless as the jacket promised and chapter nineteen delivered — then everything you're calling a failure of Volume One was the book holding its breath on purpose, and I'll have been right to trust it.

Full price, tonight. That stands. But you asked how it sits, and it sits like something I'm owed, not something I've been paid. The difference between those is Book Two's entire job.
