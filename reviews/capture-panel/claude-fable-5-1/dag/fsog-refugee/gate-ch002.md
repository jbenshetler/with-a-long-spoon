Okay, so first — I need to say this because it's been sitting on me since last night's chapter — I bought this book because the jacket promised me Vee, and I've spent all of chapter one in bed with *Randi*. The glamorous friend. The one who's supposedly going to steer the girl. And I didn't mind, because that bench scene was the thing I've been hunting for since I quit the mafia books: a man who built the thing with his own hands, who asked for her word *before* he touched her and said "I won't be upset," who stopped the whole scene to unbuckle her shoes, who sat on the bed outside a locked bathroom door and *did not knock*. I've read a hundred heroes who'd have kicked that door in and called it love. Pace sat down. I actually put the book against my chest for a second.

So I came into chapter two wanting him and I got — a sorority common room. And Brooke. God, Brooke. I know that woman. Everyone knows that woman. The warmth that's a weapon, the not-standing-up, "Engineering, isn't it," "he doesn't *teach*, does he," and then releasing her before she can use the lines she's been building — "Go." That was so precisely cruel I felt my own shoulders go up. And I hated, a little, watching Randi fold. "He's cute. Yeah." That man built you a bench and held you while you leaked on his shoulder and you let a girl with a cereal bowl file him as *short-cute* and said nothing. He wasn't in the room to be defended and she didn't defend him. That's the thing I'm going to be tracking now — whether she ever does.

The line that stopped me: *He's hard to say no to*, heard "in a different key." For about half a page my stomach went cold, because I've been burned by this exact move — the book letting the heroine quietly reclassify consent after the fact so the next thing he does can be framed as something done to her. And then I thought about it properly. He asked for her word. He checked her hands and shoulders. He waited outside the door. The thing that's hard to say no to isn't *him*, it's her own yes — she begged, and it was real, and she can't make it un-real, and that's what she can't sing in. That's exactly what the jacket meant. "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." I believe the book knows the difference. But I'm watching.

What actually got me was the duvet. "It was not — it was a duvet." She can't even finish the sentence about what it isn't. She's lying under cheap synthetic in a cold room in last night's clothes with her shoes on, and her body knows exactly where the warm house is, and the man who'd have had water waiting. And the "no door" paragraph — not holding the crying back, just there being nothing to hold — after the bottle cap under her thumbnail last night? I'm genuinely a little frightened for her. Not of Pace. Of what she's going to do so she never has to be seen like that again.

Because here's my 1 a.m. text: the jacket says Randi and Pace cook up Vee together as a *game*. And Randi just walked out of his house having decided, deadbolt-quiet, that the real her is "not coming out again." So is Vee what she does about it? Does she hand him a girl so he'll look at someone else that way and leave her face alone? That would be the coldest, saddest thing, and I think the book might be building exactly that, and I don't know yet whether I'll forgive her for it. I know I'll keep reading to find out.

The title — Standards. She *sits on Standards*. She judges other girls for a living and just got judged in her own common room while smelling of him. Fine, Helen Rivers. Fine.
