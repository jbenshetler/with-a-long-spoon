He lifted her off the ground. A week, and the door came open before she got to it and her feet left the porch, and the sound she made — "a bright stupid sound she'd have died of anywhere else" — I made it too, on my couch, at whatever hour this is. That's the thing I read for and I've never once gotten it clean: a man so glad to see a woman he can't let her walk the last three steps. Christian would have had her against the wall with his jaw set. Pace swung her around under the porch light and let her slide down the front of him. Then coats on the floor, everything on the floor, a week of it, and even then he's *slow*. "It always came around to her first." She hadn't known a man would want to. Oh, honey. Neither did I, for a lot of years and a lot of books.

And then he cooked, and she ate like a field hand, and her bras are riding higher. I sat with that one. Her mother's arithmetic is gone and in its place is a body quietly burning down whole weekends of him, and she didn't ask it to, and she's not pushing the plate away half done anymore — she's eating *everything*, like Randi in his undershirt — and the book just slid that past me like a plate. And underneath it, the racks that never believed her build and the tailor she can't afford, and I'm back on the credit card for a second, and then the Greek wine takes me off it again. He comes back to her an hour later on the couch like she's something he's been turning over the whole time the salmon cooked. There can be an *after*. God.

Then she says it. In her own chest, where she can't take it back. And here's the 1 a.m. text: she loves him, and he has never once said it, and she counts the door and the spin and the salmon and the warm tomatoes and decides she can live inside the everything-he-did without the saying. I believe her. I also hear it. This is the man who lifted his mother out of a sentence and kept a shelf of things he sets down and never picks up, and she's learning to be fed on what he does and not need what he says — and then she puts her mouth to his ear and gives him *Peter* while he's asleep. Barely the breath of it. To a man who can't hear it. She's practicing. She's keeping it for him the way she's keeping the scar, "a thing about a person you've decided to keep," and I don't know yet whether she's learning tenderness or learning his dialect so well it's become hers. Both, probably. That's the whole book. And I still don't know if Randi's had that name for a year.

The mirror undid me a different way. His arms under hers, his mouth on her neck, and she *watches his eyes leave her face* in the glass and go down to the counter — the brush, the pins, the open cream, all of her thrown across the one bare surface in a clean man's house — and hold there. I held my breath with her. I've been that guest. Every Ohio girl who learned not to take up a square foot more than she was given has been that guest. He smiles the small one and puts his mouth back. Not one word. And she carries out "had he minded? He never said. He never *said*" — and I wanted to shake him, again, because the smile isn't an answer and she knows it isn't and she's teaching herself to live on smiles.

Then Friday, the scrunchies gone, and the bottom fell out of my morning too. He'd minded. He'd gathered her up and put her out of sight the way he does everything, quietly, and now she'd have to learn where too-much goes. And I thought: yes. This is where he does it. This is the shelf.

The drawer. The *top* drawer. Her form of green, her pins gathered into a dish, her cream stood upright, room to spare — "room for more of her" — and everything of his pressed down too tight into the one below, a whole careful man folded into half the space he built for himself, and no note, there's never a note. I cried. I'm not embarrassed. He didn't tell her it didn't matter. He made her a place. That's the man I asked for in chapter one — the one whose control makes room for her instead of erasing her — and here it is, literally, a drawer, with his own things crowded down to give her the one your hand goes to first.

And I can hear the jacket anyway. "She believes she pulled out the chair herself." She opened the drawer herself. She found it herself. Nothing was handed to her she didn't reach for. That's either the loveliest thing he's ever done or it's exactly how he does everything, and tonight I genuinely cannot make myself care which, because she took a scrunchie out of *her* drawer and went to find him, and I'd follow her down that hall.

What I noticed only after: no Randi. Not one line. The first chapter in I don't know how long with just the two of them, three whole sections of it, and after the glaze on her lip at the curb I felt that absence like a held breath. The quiet is so warm. The quiet is so warm it's suspicious. He gave her the top drawer. He hasn't given her a word. And somewhere across town a woman with sky-blue toenails is sitting on a story she hasn't told.
