REACTION

He made the dress.

I have to start there because I read "sewed it himself, on a machine on his sun porch" and had to put the book face-down on my knee for a minute. The measuring. Back in September when he took her measurements and I filed it under "a rhythm," a thing the book did to show his hands and her shame — it was for *this*. He heard her say once that she'd seen a dress in a magazine she couldn't afford and he went and built the thing a dull room woke up for, the thing she walked across a stage in with nothing under it, and then he sat in Berkeley three days and said not one word about the photograph because what was he going to say, *I know, I made it.* I didn't know I could love a fictional man more than I did at the end of chapter 38 and the book went and found the room. And Vee's right, and it kills me that she's right: her father would take it better if Pace took the dress off her than that he made it. Sex he understands. The sewing he never would. Wrong is something you can reject; weird doesn't fit anywhere. That's the truest thing she's ever said about where she came from and she said it over cafeteria food, to Cassie, with her tray nearly gone.

And her mother touching her collarbone at the door before hello. Pricing the shoulders with her own hand. "You've gotten thin." Nurse face all week, watching her go back for seconds like the seconds were a trick, sitting on the end of her bed asking if she's making herself sick — and this is a girl whose bras ride higher because she swims seventy-five minutes and eats salmon with warm tomatoes and gets had three times a night, and the one woman who taught her to be ashamed of that body looks at it being *well* and reads sick. I've had a name for the mother since Randi's chest. The book just handed me her hand. "I told her I'm happier than I've ever been." Over her mother's nurse face. I'm so proud of her I could scream.

Then Cassie. "You lied to your mother." "So what is he. To you." "Is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to?" *Both.* Cassie asked the question in two sittings that Randi has not asked in three months of velvet booths, because Randi doesn't want the answer and Cassie does, and Vee was grateful, and also a little not, and that "a little not" is the most honest thing in the dinner — she's got a friend who'll hold the hard question flat on the table and leave it there, and she keeps walking out of the room to go tell the easy stuff to the woman who kisses her. She told her parents the cropped version. Waist up. The chapter's called *Cropped* and I thought that's what it meant, and then I got to the porch.

The porch. Oh, God, the porch.

She built a sentence for weeks. She built it *around* the words, like you'd build a shape around a thing you can't paint — and it's his shape, it's the paint-and-cup thing from the hall, she learned it from him and used it to reach him. "I had been eating crackers my whole life. And you fed me a meal." I knew what it was before she finished it and so did he. I know he did, because the book gave me the two breaths. His hand started two answers on her shin and put both of them down. *She had not heard him do that before.* That's not a man who missed it. That's a man who heard "I love you" in full and had three seconds to decide what to do with it, and what he did was: "I love how good we are together."

He put the word in the sentence. He put *love* in the sentence and pointed it at the *we* and the *good* and not at her. And then — and this is the line I'd send someone at one in the morning with no other text, just the quote — "What you do — what your body does — I've never." She handed him her heart in a cracker and he gave it back addressed to her body. It's Daphne, in good order. It's "the best two years I'd had" with the voice gone one degree flat. It's "not yet" on a bicycle, except this time it wasn't Sheri asking on a road, it was Vee under his quilt with the moon coming up, and she can't say it first because the rule says he goes first, and he knows the rule, and he knows he's not going first because the word's attached to a destination she hasn't been told about. You cannot say "I love you" to a woman you and your other lover picked off a lawn. Not and keep the plan. So he said the next-biggest true thing and kissed her hair for a beat too long and let her feel received, and she was — she was *warmly* received, the book says it twice, and the jacket told me what a warm reception also means.

What I hate most is that he was gentle about it. Every single thing I left the genre over, he still won't do — he didn't lie, didn't say "I love you" to keep her, didn't say nothing. He gave her a true sentence and a long kiss and his cheek on her crown. And it's not enough and he knows it's not enough, and he let her carry the not-knowing down the hall to a dryer in a dorm two days later and sit on a folding table and run it again. *I do not know what just happened.* She can't test it because the test goes through him. And then — the pile. The book finally named it. "Small flickers, moments across the fall when what she knew about him had had to do small repair work to keep functioning." I've been saying she had a pile since the swat at the sink. She's got it, she knows it's there, she doesn't look at it, and the cracker-and-meal just went on top and *it will not return to her speech.* She said her best sentence once. Retired. That's the crop. She gave him the whole frame and he sent back the waist-up, and now she's keeping a part of herself from him that used to be a part she kept from the world.

And Randi's nowhere in this chapter — not one word, not a brunch, not a Monday knee under a desk — and I noticed that about halfway through the porch, that for once the two of them were alone in a room with nobody's story in it, and it was the most tender morning they've ever had and it's the one that hurt. Thanksgiving went by in the dark between pages. Sheri brought her pie. I'd have liked to see his table.

I'm not leaving. I'm angrier at him than I've been since "give her a day," and it's the quiet kind, the kind the book's taught me to have, where I can hold his two breaths and the dress on the sun porch in the same hand and it doesn't cancel. But the lie he's living just cost her something she can't get back, and she doesn't know it was a lie, and she's already decided not to ask. Someone's going to say the word in a room. It wasn't going to be him. I think I knew that. I just didn't know it'd be crackers.
