REACTION

I read this one with my hand over my mouth, and not for the reason the book would have gotten out of me a week ago.

Because here's what happened. Two chapters back I sat on a couch with the two of them and heard "she consents — every time — or I'm done," and heard "somebody has to get her used to my mouth," and heard him say "I am" to *aren't you*, and then I turned the page and got a glass room and a father's name, and then I turned the page again and got THIS — the door open before she reached it, the cold cheeks in the warm hands, the bag she never heard land. And it's the best hallway in the book. It's the thing I came for, it's the thing I left a whole shelf to find: a man who stops her at the bathroom door with one hand and no effort and doesn't take over, who holds the back of her bra and *doesn't pull*, who lets her unwind herself out of it, who stands there not helping with the socks because her laughing is better than getting to the bed faster. "You're not helping." "I'm helping." I'd have given that hallway a ten with my whole chest. I still feel it in my body. And I can't have it clean, and I'm so angry about that I could throw the book across the room — not at him. At the book. At myself for still being in it.

"Take the time you need, I'll be here." She wanted him to fight for the nights and he didn't and she called it grace. I called it grace too, for about a second, and then I counted. Five nights. Monday was Randi on his couch in dark red silk out of *her* bag, telling him about the drawer he gave Vee on Friday. He didn't need to fight for Vee's weeknights. He's never had an empty weeknight. The plain grace of it cost her more than a fight would have — honey, it cost him nothing, that's why it looked like grace.

And then he does the thing I used to love him for: "He decided not to ask. He was good at not asking things." Two chapters ago that sentence would have been the whole man — the man who turned off the tap, who said "Don't" and had the hand gone. Tonight it read like the book leaning over and telling me what he's for. He's good at not asking. He's good at not being asked. He said "I don't go back" at the table and shut a door and I was proud of her for not pushing on it, and now I think every closed door in that house has somebody behind it.

The shirt. God, the shirt. "It stopped smelling like you." I need you to wear it for me, all weekend, give it back Sunday. That's the most *her* thing she's ever done — more than the second step long, more than the recliner — because there's no frame on it, no Randi script, nobody to retell it to, just a girl who slept in a dying shirt for weeks and washed it and drove it out there and shoved it into his hands before her nerve went. And he heard all of it. He put it on over the shirt he had on. He stood in his kitchen in two shirts because she said so. And I cried, I actually did, and then the 1 a.m. text I'd send is: what else does that house smell like. His couch smelled of her perfume gone hours old. The room smelled of *what they'd been doing all day*. Vee's going to carry that flannel home Sunday and press her face into it every night that isn't his and breathe him in, and some of what she's breathing is going to be bitter orange, and she won't know what she's smelling because she's only ever smelled it on a goodbye hug on a sidewalk. That's the sentence I can't get out of my head. The book put it there. I don't think it's an accident.

Small things that aren't small. No porch light tonight. "The long, unusually proportioned table that stood in the middle of his bedroom" — her thong lands on it and she never looks to see where it went, and I looked, because why does a bedroom table get an adjective like that in a chapter that's otherwise all skin and dough? The book is furnishing a room for something. "He'd have made the pizza exactly like this for an empty house" — she means it as the highest praise, that the care isn't a performance, and I read it and thought: his house isn't empty when she's not in it. Her face "told him something he'd wanted to know" over the pizza — he's checking the gauge. Every private smile is a reading now. I can't un-see it and I'm not sure the book wants me to.

And still — still — her desire in this chapter is hers, all the way down, and that's the only reason I'm not out. The tally her body kept. The sound she couldn't help, too loud, too soon, "her body having just published the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private." Nobody wrote that for her. Nobody's hand was on her face while she read it off. If the book had let him *take* anything tonight I'd be gone; instead he let her lose a wrestling match with her own bra and then let her put her mouth back on him anyway, and she did, and it was hers. What's being done to her isn't being done in that hallway. It's being done on the sidewalk and on the couch on Monday, with her out of the room — and she's the one who gave Randi the uncropped photograph after he promised never to share it, and I don't know whether that's the book's cruelty or its mercy, that the two piles are the same height and neither of them can see the other's.

What I keep circling: he asked Randi "Does she want it?" the way he asks things, and Randi dodged it three times, and he let her come instead of making her answer. He asked Vee nothing tonight. He never has to. She brings it to him in a bag.

I'm not reading for him anymore. I'm reading for her, and for the girl under the bunk with the window cracked two inches, and I'm reading because I need to be in the room when the flannel comes back smelling of two people. And I'm scared the book is going to let her be "ready" before she knows what she's being gotten ready for, and I'm going to have to decide then.
