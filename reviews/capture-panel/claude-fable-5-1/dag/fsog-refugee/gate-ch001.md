REACTION

Okay. Okay. I need a minute.

I bought this book on a blurb that promised me a girl named Vee, and the first thing it does is hand me a chapter about a woman I'm only told is *Randi* in the very last beat before the bathroom — and I actually said "oh" out loud. Because I'd been reading the whole thing as the man-wholly-focused-on-one-woman thing I came for, and then the name lands and the jacket reshuffles in my head: this is the glamorous friend. This is one of the two people with a plan. And I've just watched her get taken apart on a bench, by the other one. The book told me I'd be ahead of Vee from the start and I did not expect it to put me *this* far ahead, this fast, this naked.

But the bench itself. God. I've read a hundred of these scenes and the thing I never get is the waiting — the man who puts his hand on her back and says *breathe, just breathe a minute* because he can feel in her shoulders that every man before him came in fast. "Tell me your word." "Tell me again if you forget. I won't be upset." He takes her heels off *mid-scene*, holds her foot, goes back to her face and says *okay?* and she says *okay* — and only then. That's the whole thing I left the genre looking for, right there, and it isn't played as a softness that undercuts the dominance. It's the dominance. He's more in control because he keeps asking. I was wet reading it and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

The one place I bristled: "It was still her *please*." He decides which begging counts. For about a paragraph I thought — no, you don't get to grade her. And then I realized she'd been told *red, anytime,* and she doesn't say it, and what he's doing isn't withholding to punish, it's waiting for the performance to run out of road. The chapter earned it when the voice changed and he heard it — "almost ugly," "*hers*" — and said *good girl* like he was handing something back, not taking it. And then *there you are*. I had to put the phone down. The aftercare — the blanket she brought herself, the water he knew to leave, *his job for the next twenty minutes was to be the thing she sat against* — that's the warmth. That's the sex meaning something between two particular people. And he's still hard and he just... sets it down. *That's not what this night was for.* I would text that line to three friends.

And then the book does something merciless. The bottle cap under her thumbnail. The flinch at *you're beautiful*. The lock on the bathroom door — and him not knocking, because of Daphne, because he already learned once that hearing through a door isn't permission. I have never read a man *not* going in after her and felt it as the most romantic thing on the page, but that's what it was. Meanwhile she's at the mirror putting the face back on, deciding — *quiet as a deadbolt going over* — that he found her once and he's never getting her again. She walks back out smiling and he smiles back, *unguarded*, deciding whether to ask about dinner, and I wanted to scream through the page at him. He thinks it was a remarkably good evening. It was. It was also the night he lost her and he doesn't know it.

So here's what I'm suspicious of, and it's 1 a.m. logic: if she can't be the one he sees, she's going to go find him someone else to see. That's what the jacket is. The "game" is Randi solving her own terror by handing him Vee. And the chapter made me *understand* that — the apple sliced thin, the mother on egg whites, twenty-one years of making sure the face was all there was to find — without once excusing it. I'm frightened for Vee and I haven't met her yet. I'm frightened for Pace, who's going to do everything right and get played anyway. And I'm a little frightened for Randi, honestly, because the eyes "had been somewhere they had not been before and she could not get them back," and that hum in her ribs is going to go somewhere.

Door closed. Earned. Give me the next one.
