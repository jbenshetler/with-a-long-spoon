REACTION

Oh, this one got me in the chest in a way the last one didn't, and I think it's because I'd just come from Randi's chapter. I read those two texts land in Gstaad — "every word of it true, none of it the thing" — and now I've read them *sent*, from a twin bed with the knees bent because her legs outgrew it in eighth grade. And Vee read it twice for anything that gave her away. They're both doing it. Both of them, same night, same two texts, each one scrubbing the real thing out before hitting send, and each one reading the other's as proof she's fine. Randi's looking at the chalet and seeing Ohio as the warm kitchen with the parents who love Vee out loud. Vee's looking at the mountains and seeing a billionaire and a wild weekend. Neither one knows the other is lying in the cold next to something that isn't warming her. I wanted to reach into the book and knock their heads together. I'd have texted a friend at 1 a.m.: *they're both in cold rooms and they're both looking at each other's window.*

The room that "had known Vivienne, in high resolution, and loved her" — God. The little gold swimmers, the clarinet nobody's opened. The ceiling coming down close. I know that room. Everyone who's left home knows that room, and the book did it in two paragraphs without once saying the word *grief*. And the cropped photo, again — but this time *she* was the one who cut it. The same picture, one house, and she's the one who divided it. The book won't let her off the hook for anything, and I keep loving it for that even when it stings.

What scared me was the fra diavolo. That's the night. That's *her* night, the first one, wrapped in the sheet at his table with the oil going — and it turned while she was inside it, the hair went gold and the leg went tan and she couldn't keep it. That's the most frightening thing a book like this can do to me, honestly, worse than any threat: taking the one memory she goes to for warmth and letting it rot in her hands. And then the bartender's lines and Randi's "he leaves you the room" — all the things that held her up — going straight through her hands because they only ever worked while she *knew* she was the one. "The floor was gone." I believed it. And I noticed what she did with "he always left you the room" — appreciated it once, hates it now. That's the whole book in one line. The thing I came to this genre looking for, the man who lets her leave with her no intact, and she's lying in the dark hating him for it because what she needed was for him to pick up a phone. I don't have an answer. I don't think she does either.

The blonde she built from spite: "a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's." Oof. She said it about herself. That's what midnight does, and the book knew exactly what midnight does.

And here's what I'm sitting with. She can circle a day for Randi. She can't circle anything for Pace. "A day she could circle for the one and nothing at all for the other." The book just put that on the table flat, and I've been waiting for the Corolla to point somewhere and now I think I know. Not the Mercedes, not yet — a seven a.m. anthro classroom, and a face that lights up and says *gorgeous* and means it, and I know from last chapter exactly how much it means and exactly how much it isn't saying. "Let Vee arrive at it on her own." She's arriving. Randi's going to get what she set up and I don't think it's going to feel like winning.

What turned me off: nothing, really, except the ache. This is the fourth chapter in a row with nobody in a room together and I feel the gap in my teeth. Two people alone in two cold beds on opposite sides of the ocean is beautifully done and I don't want it twice more. He's been silent across the whole break and the book hasn't let me inside him once since the couch — I need to know if "Vee," once, at the door, is still costing him or if he's just cooking for Sheri and sleeping easy.
