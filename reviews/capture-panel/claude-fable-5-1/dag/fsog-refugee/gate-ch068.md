REACTION

Okay. I had to put the book face-down on my chest for a minute after "Don't move."

Let me start with the part that got me, because it's the part this whole genre keeps promising and almost never delivers: the wrong hand. A stranger's palm flat on her thigh pressing her back to the table, wanting nothing, and her body *knowing that grammar* — "had learned that grammar in a warm dark room from a man who meant it." That's it. That's the difference between this book and every captive-bride thing I quit. The shape of being held down is identical, and what makes one of them hers and the other one a service is whether the hand wants her. She lay under the wrong one and ached for the right one and kept "the whole lit ruin of herself back behind her teeth where it belonged to him." I read that twice. That is desire from inside, and it's hers, and I felt it in my own thighs, and it's a chapter where he never appears.

And then the end — alone, nobody's hands on her, her own fingers on herself and "the sound she made was not one of pain," and she's not even thinking about her own hand, she's thinking about his, learning by touch what she went and did for him. I'd read a whole chapter of just that moment, the weekend, his hand finding out. I want it so much it's embarrassing.

Now the rest of it, because I'm not stupid and neither is this book.

He asked. "Will you?" — looking straight at her, waiting. I clocked it, I'm grateful for it, it's the thing I read him for. But listen to what she says about it: "He never told her why. He asked for the thing itself, clean, and left the place where the reason should be for her to fill." I have been keeping a tally in my notes of how many times Randi stops a sentence so Vee will finish it — I was at five — and here's Pace doing his version. Different grammar, same machine. He leaves the reason blank; Randi leaves the predicate blank; and this girl fills every gap with yes before her mind catches up, "while she was still somewhere back down the sentence going *bare?*, going *the weekend?*, going *why?*" The fear arrives after, in private, once the yes is spent. And the book *says so* — "that was nearly the point of it." I don't know if that's Vee's thought or the narrator's, and it's keeping me up. "By the weekend" is also the first deadline he's ever set her. He doesn't set deadlines. Something's on for the weekend, and I'm betting it was decided in a bed in chapter 64 with Randi's hands on his wrists.

Which brings me to "barely stings." Randi. *Randi.* A woman who's had every inch of herself done by Sondra on Draper Street, who knows precisely what "the one service" includes, sat across a white cloth turning Vee's fingers over one at a time and said *barely stings* and *so worth it, baby* and not one word about rolling over. She lied about John so Vee would laugh. She lied here so Vee would go. And I keep turning it to see if it's a blind spot — Randi's always one road further along, maybe she forgot what's a first for anyone — and I can't make it one, because "Oh, you're going to *love* it. God, the first time" is a woman who has thought about Vee's first time. "Did he?" with her chin coming up an inch. She knew the ask was coming. I'd put money she made it.

And here's the beat I almost left on. Face-down, "she could say no. The word was right there... and she did not." Not because Sondra pushed, not because anyone winked — because "another line seemed like a joke, a thing for a girl who had any lines left." I flinched hard at that. That's a girl talking herself out of her own no with momentum, and I have *read* that sentence in books that wanted me to find it hot. The difference, and it's the only difference, is that this book didn't. It took the wanting out of the room on purpose — "No wanting in this one. Nothing lit, nothing for him, nothing to keep back" — and let the mother's kitchen voice name it, and let her hold still with nobody telling her to. That's not the book winking. That's the book watching a yes degrade into compliance and refusing to score it. The jacket promised me nothing's taken from Vee she doesn't hand over gladly. The front half of that table was gladly. The back half wasn't, and the book knows the difference, and I need somebody *in the book* to know it too — I need Vee to say to Randi on Thursday "you didn't tell me," and I'm afraid she'll walk in with the win in both hands like she did with Sheri and never say it.

Small things I'd text you: the two girls on the couch, one come to be looked at and one come to be touched, and Vee knowing which one she was. The underwear folded inside the jeans. "*Fuck—*" from a girl who says sorry to furniture. The nod you'd give a coffee order — that the sentence that cost her everything cost Sondra nothing. All of that is her, high resolution, and it's why I'm not angry at the book even where I'm angry at the people.

And the last line. "The most expensive thing in the room." Randi's phrase, Randi's register — the woman with the bracelet locked on her wrist who knows what every object costs. Vee walks out wearing it like a compliment. I heard a price tag. I think the book wants me to hear both.
