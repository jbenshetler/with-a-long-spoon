Oh. Oh, *Randi*.

I did not expect the book to give me this chapter and I did not expect it to be the one that got me. I went in braced — Gstaad, a chalet, her father with the light at his back and the knee that's "giving him trouble," the mother who's all edges and points and a tennis bracelet in the window light — and I thought, fine, here's the money, here's the glass room from the other side, here's why she needs the hand on his arm with both of hers. And it was that. "Eating her revulsion." The invented boys, safer than the real ones, "they always had been, at this table." Her own bracelet, the one that never comes off, kept out of the light. I noticed that and I noticed her noticing it, and I thought: she's locked. She's carrying the lock on her wrist into the breakfast room and hiding it from her mother.

And then John. God help me, I liked John. "Play along." "And you're already in it. You're whispering." Three children named on a lift line and neither of them missing a beat. He had the run to spare and spent none of it proving it — that's a line I've read about Pace in other words, and I felt the book put it there on purpose, the quiet skier, everything in reserve. The hütte, the table by the window she didn't see him get. The dress — I was *with* her buying the dress, for the daughter she doesn't have yet, "the year I met your father, the ball, the snow." She ran the whole night to the end an hour early in front of the mirror. She can always run a scenario to the end. The book told me that twice and I didn't hear it the first time.

The stairs. Both hands crossed over his heart. "She walks in beauty," and couldn't finish. The venison and the sour cherry and the white truffle turning heads. The waltz — "there was nothing for her to do but let him," and her body going where it was sent and coming back, and *easier was better*, and "tonight, gliding, she thought she was done with the work." I read that and I knew. Not because of the sleigh, not because of the midnight — because she was comparing. "He was a better dancer than Pace." Nobody who's done with a man measures the new one's hand against his. Pace's lead was "a thing she'd had to be listening for and good enough to catch, and it left the rest of the way to her." That's the kindest thing anyone has said about Pace in ten chapters and it came out of Randi's head on a marble floor while another man steered her.

Then the hotel steps. "It was only there, at the door, that she understood he had never asked her where the night was going." And her little line, hand to her chest, *what kind of girl do you think I am*, "light and young and unlike herself." And this: "Pace had never assumed anything in his life. Pace made her say it, out loud, every time. She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked." I sat with that for a long time. Because I know exactly the shelf she's standing on. I stood on it. *Easier was better. I liked not being asked.* The book let her have that, let her go up the stairs on it, didn't stop her, didn't punish her — and then gave her the room. Warm along one seam. Cold everywhere else, "and the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers." The ring of lipstick on him where she'd "felt her own mouth do it from a long way off." The eyeliner held. "Nothing had happened to her eyes."

That's the whole argument of the genre I left, made in one hotel room, and the book didn't wink once. Being taken, not asked, done to and done well by a faultless man — "the best the acceptable world could do for her" — and it was a sandwich. A good one. Gone before the plate was cleared. I have read a hundred books where that scene is the hot one and the asking is the boring one, and here is a woman lying in the fairytale, in the sleigh, in the gold, finding out that the asking *was* the heat. "Pace went straight to her. That was the whole terror of him, that he found the person she kept hidden and did not look away." I've been so angry at that man for chapters and the book just handed me, through the woman I trust least, the reason I ever loved him.

And then Vee. "Something came off Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday that this room could not counterfeit one thread of, and her whole body knew it, and she knew exactly what it was." She *knows*. She's not confused, she's not calling it work tonight. "Vee was her gorgeous." And she took the thought out — telling her — and couldn't picture Vee's face. The one scenario she can't run. I believe that more than anything she's said in the book. And then she put it back, and the reason is the worst reason and the truest one: "She was not brave enough to lose her. She had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be." So the plan is — keep the cover on. "Let the thing keep the shape it was already taking. Let Vee arrive at it on her own." Which is the lawn in September again, the thing chosen, and now I know it's not just Pace doing it. It's her too, and she's doing it out of fear, and that's not better. It might be worse, because I can't hate her for it the way I could if it were appetite.

The text. Vee, from a warm Ohio kitchen with parents who love her out loud, with minutes left in the year — *i'm here if you want to talk. or not.* After the front hall. After walking out on him in the cold. She's the one who just got her heart folded-arms'd and she's the one texting *are you okay* to the girl in the gold. And Randi gave her the fairytale — "every word of it true. None of it the thing" — and one real line, *miss your face most though*, and turned the phone facedown. On the nightstand. Vee did the same thing on the bunk rail five chapters ago, with his number. Two women, two phones facedown, both of them keeping the other one from the real sentence. I want to scream at both of them and I want to hold both of them.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *she went back and lay down against him.* In the warmth she could borrow and couldn't make. *And stayed.* That's the last line and it's a door closing and it's not the door I thought this book was going to close on. And I notice — I notice — that the two of *them*, Vee and Pace, haven't been in a room since the hall, and this chapter was a whole mountain away from either of them, and I didn't mind for one page, and I'm minding now, hard, because the next thing has to be Vee's kitchen or his empty house on New Year's Eve, and I need to know what he did with the night. Did he have anyone. Did he have *nobody*, for once. That would be the first empty night of his I've seen.
