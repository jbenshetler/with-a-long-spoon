REACTION

Oh, that's the chapter where I'd put the book face-down on my chest and look at the ceiling for a while.

Nothing happens. A girl in a top bunk takes off a shirt. And I felt it in my sternum harder than the counter or the bed or the back of the couch, because I have *done* this — not the shirt, but the thumb over the empty field. His name near the top where it stays. Waiting for the words and knowing before you start that every text you could send is small and will come back small and warm and leave you exactly where you are. She put it facedown on the rail and I actually said "yeah" out loud to no one.

The layering got me first. His flannel over her own shirt — for the hall, for the bathroom walk, so Cassie wouldn't have to look at a girl too far gone in a boy and not say anything. She's hiding the shirt from Cassie the way she hid the recliner night from Cassie and gave it to Randi. She hides everything from the one person who'd hold it. And then she can't stand the cloth not touching her and pulls both off and puts his back on bare, and for a page it works, and I was so relieved I didn't see the knife coming. "The heat in the shirt was her own, given back." He isn't in it. She's holding a smell in the dark and warming it herself. I had to stop. Two chapters ago he stood in his kitchen in two shirts because she asked him to and I cried, and the book has taken that exact shirt and shown me what it is at three in the morning when he's not in the room: a thing she fills with her own body and then breathes back in and calls him.

And she did the Vee thing — she took it off, and the cold was worse, and she stayed in it. Didn't put it back on. That's the girl in the stall who wouldn't give herself a yes over a story. She will not lie to herself with a shirt. She'd rather be bare and cold and honest than warm on her own heat pretending it's his. I love her for it and it's going to cost her everything, and the shirt in a heap — *not folded, not set back on the pillow* — in a book about a man who folds the undershirt on the end of the bed for her to find. She dropped it like a dropped thing. That's a door, or the hand on the knob of one, and I've been through enough Volume Ones to know the shape when I see it.

Here's what's turning over in me, though, and it's not kind. "He was across town in his own bed, gone down easy as he always went, easy as a man with nothing grinding in him." She believes that. I don't. I've seen him take two breaths on the porch when she said crackers and a meal. I've seen the fourth one hurt him over the back of a couch. I've seen what's *in* that bed on the nights it isn't her — black hair and all that red "together on his pillows," a woman saying *God, I want her* into the wood and him saying "I am." Vee thinks the bed is empty and easy. I know it's neither, and I can't tell which of us is more wrong about him. And she said it herself, naming the parts of him in the cloth — "the plate he set down once he'd built the hunger in her" — and didn't hear it. Built. She said *built* and breathed it in like a compliment. The jacket's whole first line is in that sentence and she's using it to comfort herself.

I'm not angry at the book. The book isn't asking me to find his silence romantic — Vee is bleeding from it on the page and the book's letting her, no wink, no "but he showed her with his hands so it counts." That's the honesty I've been reading this genre looking for. But I'm three chapters deep in her lying awake now — the dryer, the dark after Chili's, the bunk — and I'd like, badly, for one person in this book to *say one true sentence to her face*. Him, Cassie, I'd even take Randi dropping the gloss for a line. Cassie is right there under her, two inches of cold window, asleep. If she'd been awake she'd have said "take it off, streetlight, you're going to cook in there" and it would have been the kindest thing anyone did all chapter.

I'd text a friend: *she took off his shirt because the warm in it was hers.* And then nothing after. There isn't an after.
