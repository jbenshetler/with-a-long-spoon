REACTION

Six chapters. I counted. And the book finally gives me Randi's own head and it does it in Gstaad, in a metallic blue dress, with a man named John who has the run to spare and spends none of it proving it, and I read the whole thing with my jaw a little tight because I knew — I *knew* — where it was going from the moment she said "this is the one" on a marble floor. You don't get to think that sentence in a book like this and keep it.

First, the thing I'd have texted at 1 a.m. before I even got to the ski lift: *plain white gold, locked on her wrist, and kept it out of the light.* Locked. Never comes off. She hides it from her mother's tennis bracelet and it catches on the heel of her hand when John turns her out at the end of his arm. That's not jewelry, that's a collar, and I think it's his, and the book just set it on the table next to the thong and the bench and walked away whistling. And the breakfast — "eating her revulsion," inventing boys for her father because the invented ones were safer *at this table*, "keep your eye out for an eligible bachelor" and her laugh hoping it doesn't sound as brittle as it felt. Central Florida, not mentioned. Smoked salmon, no butter. The girl who never opens the menu has a whole mother's worth of reasons not to, and I got them in four sentences.

John is good. That's the trick of it. The book didn't give her a creep or a bore; it gave her a man who skis quiet and leads firm and conjures a sleigh out of a side street, and I liked him, and she liked him, and I felt the "he was a better dancer than Pace" land like something true, because it is true. *Easier was better. She thought she was done with the work.* I believed her for a page. And then the midnight kiss and "for one second, from nowhere, Pace," and I knew the count was running.

Here's my gripe, and it's a real one: *he drew her in, and she went. Afterward.* That's a door closing on me, and I don't forgive doors. I almost put it down right there — that's the move I quit books over. What saved it is that the book's more explicit about the *after* than most books are about the act: the ring of her lipstick on him where she took him in her mouth and felt her own mouth do it from a long way off, the body spent and the woman unmoved, waiting to see what the holding would develop into and nothing developing. A good sandwich eaten fast. (A sandwich — the book's whole food system, crackers and meals and now this, and it never once winks about it.) I understand why the sex is a cut; it's a cut because it was nothing. I still wanted to be *shown* nothing, not skipped past it. Grumbling, but I stayed.

And then the mirror. Warm only where she touched him, cold everywhere else, and the cold is hers, she brought it in. I sat up. Vee in the flannel three chapters ago, finding the warmth was her own, taking it off, lying bare in the cold rather than wear a lie. Randi finds the cold is her own and *lies back down in the borrowed warmth and stays.* Same test, two women, opposite answers, and nobody underlined it. That's the book I'm paying for.

"Pace went straight to her. That was the whole terror of him." Yes. That's what the couch was, that's "it makes me feel real and some nights it frightens me," and this is the chapter that tells me what she's frightened of: not him, but that he'll never sit at the good end of her mother's table, and she's built a whole life to keep herself hidden and he's the one person who won't let her. I'll admit the last third leans on me a little — "the flaw was hers," "he was safe, she'd put nothing on the table and won exactly that," I got it the first time. But she's a woman who runs scenarios to the end; it reads like her doing inventory, not the author doing it for me. Mostly.

And Vee. "Something came off Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday that this room could not counterfeit one thread of." *Vee heard it one way; Randi meant it two ways* — the I-love-yous over brunch, and I went back and reread the note where she said it with sugar on her mouth and felt sick in the good way. She can run every scenario but the one where she has to look at Vee's face. She's not brave enough to lose her. *Vee was her gorgeous.* I had to put the phone down for a second. And her decision — don't tell, let it keep the shape it's taking, keep the brunches exactly as they are, *let Vee arrive at it on her own* — is the most Randi thing imaginable: it's the lawn all over again, it's picking her and arranging it so she thinks she pulled out the chair herself. She just did it to herself, consciously, about her own heart, and called it kindness. I don't know if I'm furious or if I'd do the same.

Then the phone. *thinking about you 💛 i'm here if you want to talk. or not.* From Ohio. From the girl who got told words are cheap on a doormat and drove off crying, who I've been waiting six chapters to find out where she drove, and the book skipped the car and the whole of break and here she is, reaching for Randi with *or not* on the end of it like someone who's learned the hard way not to need an answer. And Randi gives her the fairytale. Every word true, none of it the thing. Then the one line she means — *miss your face most though, gorgeous* — and face down. Vee doesn't know Randi's in bed with a stranger feeling nothing. Randi doesn't know Vee's holding MIRANDA. Neither of them has said a word about the man they share, and the text doesn't mention him once. Does Randi even know about the mat? Did he tell her? Has Vee been sitting in that warm kitchen for two weeks with the parents who love her out loud, saying nothing to anyone, and texting the one person she *can't* say it to?

I didn't get my car. I'm annoyed about that and I don't care, which is the book's trick with me now. I got Randi cracked all the way open and lying back down in it anyway, and that's worse than crying in a car. She stayed. She fixed her hair and she *stayed.*
