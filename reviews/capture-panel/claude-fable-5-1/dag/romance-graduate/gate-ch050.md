She left the bag in the car. That's the chapter, for me, before anyone says a word — she sat in the drive with the engine ticking, let the heat he'd turned up for her go straight through her guard, *hated* that it did, and then walked in carrying nothing and with her coat buttoned to the throat. She left before she arrived. I've been watching this girl learn to read him by what he moves — the lamp one click, the drawer, the two shirts — and tonight she walked into his house having finally learned to do it back. No bag on the chair. No mouth turned up. Let him arrive at the fact of it. I was so proud of her I could have thrown the Kindle.

And then he gave her the evening. "I've got the chicken going. We eat, and then if you're warm enough we sit out on the porch." God. He answered *what are we doing* with the itinerary, and it isn't a dodge, that's the thing that kills me — it's the truest thing he's got. That's what he thinks they're doing. "The most important person in my life" — and I believe him, I actually do, and Randi still has Saturdays, and he said it looking at a girl who can't bring her boyfriend home with a question mark on it. Then the math-guy move, "do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have" — a comparison, a ranking, anything with a shape he can hold, instead of the one word with no shape at all. She saw it. "That's not what I asked." She's not letting him solve it. She's done being the problem he's glad to be handed.

"Words are cheap. Believe what I do." I've been writing that sentence *about* him for forty chapters — he never says, he gave her a drawer instead of a sentence — and here it is in his own mouth, as a creed. It's a beautiful creed for a man who cooks. It's also the perfect cover for a man with a bench and a plan and a table three feet from her pillow, because *what he does* is exactly the part she's never been shown. And she turned it on him — "words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me." The girl who reads the right-hand column first, who's been paying for the cheapest yes on the card all book, finally priced him. Best line she's had. I said it out loud.

Then he folded his arms. "Holding a line at cost." *What line.* For whom. That's the moment I stopped being sad for her and got cold toward him — because a man who's simply bad at words doesn't fold his arms and shut. A man holding something does. Something went across his face and closed, and I have a drawer full of guesses about what's behind it and all of them are small and dark and smell like bitter orange. Three runs at it. This was the fourth and she stopped accepting the hand. That isn't one unsent text. That's earned.
