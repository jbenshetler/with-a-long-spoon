REACTION

Okay, so I said it out loud in my own notes — "if it's another Vee-alone-in-the-dark I'll feel it in my thumb" — and the book went and did it anyway, and my thumb felt it. Second quiet chapter in a row. Fifty ended on a doormat with her bag in the car and I've now been given a ski chalet and a twin bed and not one body in a room with another body who matters. I know what a bridge is. This is two bridges end to end and I'm starting to look for the far bank.

That said. The trick it's pulling is good enough that I'm annoyed at how well it worked on me. I watched that text arrive on Randi's nightstand last chapter — *thinking about you 💛* — with a perfect nothing asleep behind her, and now I'm in the bed it got written in, watching Vee read it twice for anything that gave her away. And then Vee opens the reply and builds a *devastatingly handsome billionaire* and *a weekend so wild Vee wouldn't recognize half of what they did* — honey, I've got that man on my Kindle, forty of him, and the real one's asleep with a lipstick ring on him and a woman lying cold against his side thinking about *you*. Two women, two beds, same midnight, both cold, and the one in the gold thinks the one in Ohio has the warm kitchen, and the one in Ohio thinks the one in the gold has the fairytale. And the only true line in the whole exchange — *miss your face most though* — is the one Vee reads as a nice girl being polite. She reads everyone backwards the second they're out of the room. The fork, Randi's text, and now the blonde. Pattern. The book knows it's a pattern.

The blonde got me, actually. Not the spite-building — that's every girl at midnight, I've done it — but the fra diavolo turning while she's inside it. She goes back to the one night she's got, the sheet slipping, him seeing, her not fixing it, and the hair goes gold and the leg goes tan in the wine light and *she could not keep her own night*. That's the sentence. I felt that one under the ribs. He got into her memories well enough that she can't even run them without him at the stove, and now she can't run them without somebody else at the table. And "a body that had never in its life needed building for" — she thinks the building was charity. She's wrong, I was on the couch, I know what the building *is* to him, and I'm sick that she thinks it's the thing she'd lose first.

What's actually making me angry is a half-sentence she slides past: *nothing since, not a word across the whole break*. He hasn't texted. Not once. She cried on his mat and drove off in the dark and he's given her the whole room, every inch of it, for two weeks, and the book has her say the exact thing I'd say — *a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now* — and I want to drive to that house and bang on the door myself. I understand him. That's the problem. "Leaves you the room" is his whole method and this is the first time it's cruelty, and he'd call it respect, and Randi told Vee it was respect, and Randi's in Gstaad learning the hard way what a man who *doesn't* ask feels like. Everybody's holding exactly the lesson the other one needs.

Small things I'm keeping: the room that *kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed*. The little gold swimmers with their toes pointed over a girl who sent her toes to Randi. The heel *Randi had knelt and buckled to her foot* — knelt, and I hadn't filed that. "Where Vee's was a stripper's" — she said it about herself, flat, and it's the most honest she's been about her own body in fifty-two chapters. And her mother's question in her own mouth — *what am I to you* — yeah, I'd put that together too late at twenty-one and it would have wrecked me.

Where I almost left: the beige paragraph. The tree, the wrapping, Wheel of Fortune, the casserole-that-was-love-but-not-food. I know that house, I grew up in it, I don't need a full paragraph of it to believe she's suffocating; I was skimming for the phone by the second sentence. The book earned it back with the text, but I was gone for a minute.

Here's my deal with it. Next chapter has to have a door. She said it herself — the term starts, a face that lights up and says *gorgeous* and means it, and I know from the chalet exactly what *gorgeous* costs the woman saying it. That's a scene I'll read standing up. If the book gives me that, I forgive two bridges. If it gives me a third quiet one, Pace at a stove alone or Randi on a plane, I'm going to feel the whole weight of my to-read pile at once.
