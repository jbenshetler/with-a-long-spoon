REACTION

Okay. So the book did the thing where it closed a door in my face — her out in the cold with no bag, his hand lifting — and then opened the next page in *Gstaad*, five weeks later, through *Randi*. I actually said "oh you did not" out loud. Thanksgiving is gone. Sheri's pie is gone. Whether the flannel went home with Vee is gone. Whatever he did with the cold — drove after her, called Randi, nothing — I don't get to see it, I get to find out that by New Year's Vee is in a warm kitchen in Ohio texting Randi a yellow heart. That's a lot of road to skip and I felt every mile of it as withheld. And then the chapter was good enough that I stopped counting the miles, which is the most annoying thing a book can do to me.

Because this is the Randi chapter I've been waiting since ch43 for, and it's not the one I expected. I thought I'd get her reading Vee across a booth. Instead I get her at breakfast with the cheating bastard himself, "eating her revulsion," hugging him with the sorority smile, and the bracelet — the one I never named, the one that clicked against his couch — kept *out of the light* next to the mother's tennis bracelet. She makes up boys for her father. "The invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table." Oh. Oh, that's where she learned it. The two-readings thing, the currency thing, the whole machine — it was built at that table, for that man, and MIRA just told her what the table was paid for with.

And then John. I'll be honest, the lift-line bit charmed me against my will — "you're already in it, you're whispering" — three kids named and numbered before the chair came round, that's a meet-cute written by someone who's read as many of these as I have and knows exactly what button it is. He's good on the black. He has run to spare and spends none of it proving it. He's cotillion-good at the waltz. He conjures a *sleigh*. The book builds the fairytale with real materials, the venison and the truffle and the silver coming down at midnight, and it lets her have it all the way — "this is the one" — and I knew, I knew from "he was a better dancer than Pace" where it was going, and I read it anyway with my stomach down.

"Pace's lead was the lightest pressure, a thing she'd had to be listening for." That's the whole book in a dance lesson and it's Randi who says it, not Vee. And then the kicker, the one I didn't see coming: "Pace had never assumed anything in his life. Pace made her say it, out loud, every time. She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked." *She consents or I'm done* — I've been treating that as his one clean rule, and here's the woman it's practiced on saying the rule is work, the rule is the thing that makes her present, and the fairytale is the man who never makes her show up. That's a knife in both directions and the book didn't explain it, it just let her enjoy the sleigh.

The sex is a fade and I noticed the fade, and I'm letting it go, because the fade *is* the sex: nothing happened to her. Then it comes back after, in the lipstick ring, and that's the most explicit the chapter gets and it's a crime scene — "where she had taken him in her mouth and felt her own mouth do it from a long way off." That's the sentence. That's her at the rail with her face to the wood. "A good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon" is funnier than it has any right to be and I hate that I laughed. And then "the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers." Same cold Vee took the shirt off in. The book's running both women in the same register now — warm only where they touch him, where they touch *each other*, and carrying the cold in and out themselves — and I don't think either of them knows the other one has it.

Here's what I'm not over. "Vee was her gorgeous." The word she's been throwing across booths all fall and it was the real one. And she ran the scenario and the only frame she can't run is Vee's *face*, so she decides — *decides*, in a faultless room next to a faultless man — to keep the cover on the word and let Vee "arrive at it on her own." Keep the brunches exactly as they were. That's not "for us, when she's ready" anymore. That's her, alone, choosing the slow play for herself and calling it kindness. She says she's never had to be brave and arranged her whole life so she wouldn't have to. I believe her. I also think she just told me she's not going to tell Vee, ever, and I'm supposed to grieve that and I do.

And then the text. God, the text. "i'm here if you want to talk. or not." Randi reads it as Vee seeing her "up here in all the gold," and sends back the fairytale — every word true, none of it the thing, the exact move she makes with everyone — and turns the phone *facedown*. But I know what that text is. That's not fairytale-envy. That's the girl who saw her go white in the glass room and slid the laptop over and never said the name, texting the daughter at minutes to midnight on her first New Year's knowing what her father is. Vee's offering her the thing about MIRA. Randi can't see it because she doesn't know she was looked at. So Vee sends mercy and gets a chalet, and Randi gets mercy and reads it as admiration. Two people each wrong about which woman the other one is. That's the book, that's always been the book, and it did it in two text bubbles.

What I don't have: Vee. Five weeks of her. Who's she texting at midnight — Randi first, apparently, "thinking about you," and I don't know if that's because Pace is over or because he's in the room with her. I don't know if she went back in. I don't know if the walkout held. This reads like a last chapter — the title, the midnight, the snow, the two women lying down in borrowed warmth — and if it is, I'm furious and I'm buying the next one tonight. If it isn't, somebody had better walk into that kitchen in Ohio on the next page.
