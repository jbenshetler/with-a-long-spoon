REACTION

Two breaths. I'm going to be thinking about those two breaths all week. He's never taken two breaths before anything in forty-six chapters — he takes one pause, the "answer it properly" pause, and then sets the words down clean. This time his hand started two different sentences on her shin and put both of them down, and he breathed twice, and what came out was "I love how good we are together." He *heard* her. That's the thing I can't get past. The book is being very careful to let me know he heard it — the stillness one beat too long, the press, the stop — and then he aimed the word at the wrong noun. She said *love* with crackers and a meal around it so he couldn't be cornered by it, and he said *love* with *how good we are* around it so she couldn't be either. They each built a little box for the word and handed it across. His box was about her body. Hers was about her life.

And I've been saying for ten chapters *he never says*. I had it in my notes as a character trait, almost a charm — the drawer instead of the sentence, the shirt pulled on over the other shirt. This is the first time the not-saying cost her something I could watch. I wrote that it'd break her when she found out how much he's moved. It didn't occur to me the other way — that it'd break her a little when he *doesn't*. "I don't go back" last week, each word set apart, and now this, and I'm starting to think the wall I've been side-eyeing isn't the plan at all. The plan is Randi's. The wall is his, and older, and she just put her hand on it in the dark and felt it not give.

Her sentence, though. God. "I'd been eating crackers my whole life." I winced and I loved her for it in the same second — it's the book's whole thesis put in her mouth like a thing she thought of in the laundry room, and it's a little too tidy, and it's also exactly what a twenty-one-year-old who's been rehearsing a poem for three weeks would say to the moon instead of to his face. She looked at the moon. She didn't breathe. She said it once and retired it. That's the saddest line in the chapter and it's not even in the porch scene — it's "the cracker-and-meal would not return to her speech." She's folding the sentence and putting it in the drawer with everything else she can't say, and the drawer in that house is *his*.

The pile. "The place was not empty." I've been calling it a shelf since the practice room — *please* unclaimed, the hand on her own knee, the inch she didn't take — and now the book has looked straight at it and told me there's a pile and she doesn't look at it. Which is the one place this chapter leaned too hard. I had the whole thing by the time she sat on the folding table; I didn't need "she had said her best sentence, he had not said the sentence back" spelled out in italics like a caption. And that *wait* that "went into the body where small waits go" — the chapter does the breastbone thing three times when once would've put me on the floor. It's the second quiet one in a row after Cassie's cafeteria, and I'm not resenting that, because this is the one the quiet was for. But I'm noticing the prose narrating her not-thinking at length, and that's the closest this book has come to explaining its own subtext to me.

What I keep coming back to at 1 a.m.: he said "what your body does." She told him he changed her *life* and he told her she's great in bed. Randi would have heard it. Randi, in that booth, went bright-eyed and unfixed over "like he wants *me*, with the body attached" — and *Pace* just told her, warmly, holding her, that what he loves is the body. The one person who could hear what Vee's sentence meant and tell her so is the one who's been off the page since the glaze, and I think that's where this is going, and I don't like how much I want it.

Also — he's wearing her flannel. He's wearing it for her, all weekend, two shirts in his own kitchen, and he can't say the word. Those are the same man. That's the thing the book's been telling me since the scrunchies, and I believed it was enough, and this morning on the porch she found out it might not be.
