REACTION

Two towels. He put it in the title and then walked her right past it — "a bathroom, plain, clean, two towels on the rod" — and she didn't blink, and I put the book down on my chest for a second because I know whose the second one is. I've *been* in that bathroom. I watched Randi lock it and decide in that mirror. Vee looked at the towel rod for one clause and moved on to the office, and that's the whole book in four words: she's standing in the evidence and it reads as housekeeping.

And the heat — she almost says *did you leave the heat on?* and doesn't. Randi's complaint, verbatim, from her side of the house. Vee filed it "under the small mysteries of him." Girl. That's not a mystery. That's a woman who's been cold in this house before you.

The kiss on the stoop got me before any of that did. She drove the paved road deciding to take it, she took it, it was everything — and *he* stopped. And her whole body went still and started paying attention, because no boy had ever once been the one to stop, it had always been her job. I felt that in my own chest as pure want, the good kind, the kind this genre has spent a decade making me numb to and this book just handed back to me in one sentence. And then, half a second behind it, I felt the other thing, which is that he stopped because the stoop isn't where the scene is. He's pacing her. He's got a room with a wall of glass at the end of this hallway and he's not going to spend her on a doorstep. Courtly, exact, patient. I'm turned on and I'm slightly sick and I've stopped pretending those are two feelings.

The closed door. She *leaned.* She'd put satin on under everything for that door and she leaned toward it before deciding whether to let him see her lean, and he said "my bedroom" like he'd said "the kitchen" and touched her elbow and turned her back to the light. Something in her dropped. Something in me went the other way, because I know what's behind that door — I know what the long table is, I know the bench — and he's just decided she doesn't get it today. And then, and this is the part that made me actually put my hand over my mouth: she crouches in the den and puts her fingers on a joint in a long low oak table and says *where'd you get these,* and he says *that joint you've got your fingers on, there's no screws in it. It's cut to lock, and glued, so it stays.* She's touching the same joinery. Same hands, same wood, same pale white oak. She's got her fingers on the bench's sister and she's admiring the craftsmanship.

The dress. Okay. I have read a hundred rich-man-buys-her-a-gown scenes and I've never once felt anything but the credit card. This isn't that. It's not the shade she said. Her first beat was disappointment — *oh, it's not the right* — and then he held it up beside her face in the mirror and it wasn't the dress from the magazine, it was *her,* mixed for her, and the disappointment turned over. She threw that dress away on a rock with a cherry in her hand and he picked it up off the ground and kept it. And then — *you made the shirt.* The no-tag thing paying off. (It's been a week and she's "worn it into the ground" — fine, she's been sleeping in it, I'll allow it, I would too.) Her eyes went hot and didn't spill and mine did a little, and I'm not proud of it, and I'd do it again.

And then the ask. Which is the most beautifully built ask in the book, and I say that having watched him ask Randi for her word. *To do it right I'd measure you first — down to what you'd wear under it, in good light.* In the glass room. And then he gives it back to her: *if you want it.* He has constructed the single circumstance on earth in which Vivienne Thorne — cardigan buttoned to the collarbone, *floozies* in her mother's voice — will stand in her underwear in front of a wall of windows, glad, grateful, crying a little, and call it a gift. He built the reason. The only price is being looked at, and she's been dying to be looked at since the truck. *Yes — of course, yes. No hedge in it.* The jacket said "every yes was freely given, that was the problem," and I have never seen a blurb line land so exactly on a page. I'd have said yes. I'd have said yes faster.

Kayla called it, by the way. There's no meal at two.

The patent, the MRI, the kid who can't lie still — I liked him more in that paragraph than I've liked him anywhere, and then "are your parents rich?" and a door easing shut two rooms away, and I've got a new one to carry beside Daphne. And "say it more than once so the noise can't kill it" — I noticed that. I noticed it's about more than codes. The book didn't explain it to me, which is the only reason I'm not annoyed that I noticed.

One thing my thumb flagged: he "lit up like a six-year-old who'd just found out everything there was to know about dinosaurs" — that's Vee's exact line from the smoothie place, word for word, and it came back in narration like the book forgot it had already spent it. Small. I'd forgive it three more times and then I'd stop forgiving it. Also I'll say this: after three chapters running of Vee-tells-a-friend (Randi, Cassie, then Meg and Kayla), I was starting to feel the shape, and this chapter broke it clean. And it's long, and it earns long — which makes the two-page ones look, in hindsight, like breaths between the real ones. I still don't know if that's a technique. I'm less suspicious than I was.

What I want now, in order: the measuring. Whether Randi's the good light. Whether he says the thing he said to Randi — *can we leave them up* — or whether Vee's version gets a different sentence. And whether Vee gets that bedroom door before the book gives it to her.
