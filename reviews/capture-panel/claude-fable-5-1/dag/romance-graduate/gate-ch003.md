The jacket told me there was a game and I've spent two chapters wondering when the book would let me see the mechanism, and it turns out the mechanism is a man's mouth at a woman's ear on a Tuesday afternoon saying *what about her.* I put the Kindle down on my chest for a second after "Her name is Vee." I've been ahead of the girl since the blurb and I just watched them pick her off a lawn like fruit. And I liked it. That's the part I'd be texting about at 1 a.m. — not "is this messed up," I know it's messed up, the book *knows* it's messed up, it's on the cover — but that I was leaning forward with Randi through the whole catalog going *no, not the ponytail, not the matched pair, come on, give her the one she actually wants.* I was shopping too. Merciless is right.

The bed first, though, because I need to say this out loud: this book does not flinch. He goes down on her, comes up before she's done, slides into her while she's still pulsing "around nothing" — that phrase, God — and then asks the question *inside* her and she comes on the question. Not on what he's doing. On the question. I've read that setup a hundred times as a fantasy the author is too shy to commit to and here it's the plot. Her body answers before she can dress it, and then twenty minutes later, in the ordinary light, he asks it again and she *flinches* and reaches for the kitten voice and hands the whole thing back to him as a gift. "Only in a threesome. With you." And he sees exactly what she did — "the same act, a different owner" — and calls it beautiful. That's the most emotionally intelligent paragraph I've read in a romance in a year and it also made the hair on my arms stand up, because I was in that bathroom with her in chapter one. I know she decided he gets the face and nothing else, *not ever.* So this isn't a woman opening up. This is the face finding a workaround. She's going to let herself want a woman by routing it through him, and he thinks that's her coming forward, and I'm the only one who knows both halves. That's the book's whole trick and it's working on me.

What I'm suspicious of — and I'm holding it, not dropping the book over it — is Pace. "He took the body's answer for the true one. He usually did." Sir. That's a lovely sentence and it's also a man deciding that an orgasm is testimony. Everything he does is courtly and slow and he asks and asks and asks, and it's genuinely hot, the "I point, you pick" is hot, the kiss on the quad with her own taste still on his mouth so she catches it mid-*maybe* is *filthy* in the best way — and underneath every gentle thing there's this hum of a man who's very sure he can read a woman better than she can read herself. He was right about her on the bench. He might be wrong about what she locked away in that bathroom. I want to watch him be wrong. The Daphne line from chapter one is sitting in my back pocket. He learned not to open a door he can hear through. He hasn't learned yet that she's built a door he *can't* hear through.

The catalog of women went a beat long for me in the middle — the two gym girls, the beanie girl, the blazer girl — and the quad description with the "men who wanted the eye to travel exactly this way" was the book clearing its throat prettily for a paragraph while I waited for the next point. But I forgive it because the catalog is the character work: every *no* is Randi telling us her own taste, and her taste is nobody who looks like her. And then Vee comes through the doors laughing with her whole throat bared, "clothes chosen to cover, and failing at an impossible task," and Randi — the girl who counts apple slices, who "can't eat that" cheeseburger — stops walking and *can't look away.* That's the appetite thread landing. He's found her hunger again, and it's shaped like a redhead who's never trimmed herself to a scale in her life. I got that in my stomach.

And then she engineers the collision so cleanly that the other girl apologizes. Ten years of dance. I laughed out loud and then I didn't feel great about laughing, which is, I think, exactly what the book wants from me. "Do you think she'll like the bench?" is the best last line I've hit in months — it's a joke, it's not a joke, it's a woman who cried facedown on that thing three days ago already picturing someone else on it, and I can't tell if that's her running from what it did to her or toward it.
