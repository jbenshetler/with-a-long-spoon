REACTION

Okay. So that's two in a row. School Nights was a bridge and I didn't mind it because Cassie was in it and Cassie asking "Do you love him" flat with her book on her chest was worth the whole chapter. This one is Vee alone in a pool for seventy-five minutes and nobody says a word to anyone, and I felt my thumb start to move around "Four hundred easy to start. Then the long middle." I know what a lap chapter is. I've read the hockey version of it a hundred times, the guy on the ice at 5 a.m. with the puck and his feelings. I did not expect this book to hand me one.

But I didn't skim, and here's why. "Her body, which had opinions all day long every day of her life, had none at all down here. It only worked." That's the sentence. That's the whole book's engine turned off for one chapter, on purpose, so I'd hear how loud it's been. Every other page of this thing somebody is looking at her — Pace, Randi on one knee, Cassie's forty seconds in stats, a room full of parents, a mirror — and her body has an opinion about being looked at every single time. Here nobody's looking. The butterfly man and the flip-turn woman don't even know her name. The green-and-white suit "had nowhere left to go on her and hadn't for years" and she *looked at the water instead* — that's the only moment of the old shame in the chapter and she just declines it. I didn't know she swam. Since twelve. Hymns and laps. Ohio girl. The book's been holding that back and it's the first thing she's had that isn't in Randi's folder and isn't on Pace's counter. I want to keep it for her.

And then the four strokes. "Whether he was that good or she had made him that good, out of wanting, out of two months of being looked at like that." *Thank you.* I've been sitting here for thirty chapters saying "I believe him and I'm furious at how much," and nobody in the book has had that thought — not Randi, who's too busy wanting him, not Cassie, who's never been told enough to have it. Vee had it, underwater, for four strokes, and then the wall came and she let it go, and I think that's exactly how long a person lets herself have that thought when she's happy. It didn't resolve. Good. But it's in her now. And "did he mind" going to nothing by the fourth piece while "do you love him" stayed a pressure with dread riding it — that's honest. The counter was never the problem. The *maybe* is the problem. She had the answer "sitting plainly in her" and said maybe to Cassie anyway, and she doesn't know why, and I do: Cassie is the one who gets the edited cut. Saying it out loud to Cassie would have made it a thing that's true in the dorm, not just in his bed. She's still keeping the rooms separate.

What bugged me: "It never answered anything, the swimming. That was the whole use of it. It took the words off things one at a time until what was left was the shape underneath." I'd already gotten that. I'd gotten it from the count eating the words piece by piece — *him*, and the ache, and the count, then the count — which was the best-built thing in the chapter. Then the book stood behind me and said it in a sentence, and I felt the hand on my shoulder again. It's done this before and I'd let it go, and I'll let it go again, but it's the thing I'd have cut.

So: a quiet one, honest, short, with one real sentence and one real thought in it. I'd text a friend "she swims??" and nothing else. If the next chapter is Vee alone with a sandwich and a stats reading, I'm skimming for a quotation mark. But I don't think it will be. The glaze on her lip is still sitting there unanswered, "Peter" has still never been said in front of Randi, and Cassie has now asked the question out loud — the book's got three fuses lit and it just spent a chapter letting the girl breathe before one of them goes. I'll give it that. One.
