REACTION

Okay, so I felt my shoulders go up the second the blonde came off the rail. I know this scene. I have read this scene four hundred times — the rival with too much makeup, the poisoned little "all us girls love him," the heroine storming off over a misunderstanding a single sentence could've fixed. This is exactly the manufactured-fight I've been dreading, and for about a page I was reading with my thumb ready. "The makeup was doing a lot of work" — that's the book leaning on the scale so I'll know who to hate, and it's the cheapest thing it's done in twenty-eight chapters. And then the wise bartender who racks glasses and dispenses the truth, "wasn't him they were jealous of, honey" — come on. I've had that bartender. She works at every bar in every book.

And yet. Here's what kept me: the venom isn't a misunderstanding. It's her mother. *A man like that has girls, and you'd be one of them* — it arrives "flat as ever" and then Vee looks at the dance floor and thinks *floozies* and I realized she's finally turned the word outward, on strangers, and it's no cleaner there than it was on herself. "Three dates, and she'd spread her legs like a slut." The book let her think that and didn't soften it and didn't explain it, and that's the difference between this and the KU version. She *knows* she's doing it — "she knew what she was doing, and she did it anyway" — and I've been that woman at a bar deciding to stay angry because angry was easier than the other thing. That's real. I hated her for about four paragraphs and I understood her the whole time.

And God, the irony is so good I want to shake her. She's jealous of the wrong women. She's standing there counting hugs and building a harem out of two-semester dance students, and the actual girl — the one who knows what his pillow smells like, who was riding him with her eyes shut Sunday night in his undershirt — is the one she calls her best friend. Every single thing she flung at him about "the girls" is *true*, just not about them. "He'd chosen her." Oh, honey. Two people chose you. The book didn't wink once and I was practically yelling.

The lesson itself is the thing I'd text about at 1 a.m. "Give me something to push back against. Being jelly doesn't work." "The space you're keeping — that's what gets you stepped on." That's the tape measure again, the pencil, the box — the one play in boots on a concrete pad — and I felt the exact moment she stopped guessing and let the lead arrive, because I've felt it on a dance floor and I've felt it in bed and they're the same muscle. The book got right up to the line of explaining that to me and stopped in the dance. Good. And then she lies there at 3 a.m. with the shame and *that* sensation — his hand at her shoulder blade — both at once, and doesn't pick one. That's the most honest thing in the chapter.

What I keep chewing on: he left her there. Mr. gets-every-girl-home-safe took her at her word — "I'll handle that myself" — set the beer down untouched, and drove off, and she paid for a cab that stank with money meant for other things. That's his play taken to the letter. She said no, he stopped. And it's right and it's also *a woman alone in a county bar lot at closing*, and I can't decide if I admire it or want to hit him. Both, probably. Which is where I've been with him since Randi drove up the gravel.

The shirt run through the machine, smelling of fabric softener and no one. Ouch. And Cassie asleep three feet away, again, not woken, again — and then the morning: go to Randi's. NO. Not Randi. Randi who said "it's selfish, Pace" over an untouched bowl and then got his own sentence handed back to him a week later. Vee's about to carry the fight to the one person with a stake in it, and she thinks she's going to a friend. The book cut this into slivers — the dashes, the fast-cut night — and it worked for a night that's all fragments, but I'm noticing the shortcut a little. Whatever. I'm already turning the page.
