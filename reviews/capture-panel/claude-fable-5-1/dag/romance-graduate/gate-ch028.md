Okay so the dance lesson. I sat there for the first two pages going, oh no, he's going to teach her the three-step and I'm going to have to read the count — "one-and-two, three-four" — and I nearly did the thing I do with a lesson chapter, which is skim for the hand. But the hand came. "Give me something to push back against. Being jelly doesn't work." "The space you're keeping — that's what gets you stepped on." I read that on the concrete pad under the field lights and I thought: this is the whole book, said out loud, in a knit shirt in October. And I braced for it to explain itself — my hair trigger, the book leaning over and going *you see what this means* — and it didn't. It just let her fight him for ten minutes and lose and then something gave and she went around the pad twice not thinking. "There it is," he said, quiet. "She said nothing. She was busy." That's the sentence. That's what it's been like on the box, in the truck, on the sheets, and she's never once had a word for it and now she does and it's *frame*. Fine. I'll take a metaphor if it's that clean.

And then the Rusty Nail, and the girl at the till going *hey, Pace*, and the hugs that went on a beat longer, and Vee starting a count she didn't mean to start — and her mother arriving "on the tail of it, flat as ever." I felt my stomach go. I *know* this scene. I've read the jealous-blonde-at-the-bar scene four hundred times, the "all us girls love him" plant with the lipstick doing too much work, and the heroine who storms off over a misunderstanding a single sentence would clear up. That's my exit door. That's the shape I quit series over. I almost put it down at "He's so good about getting a girl home safe at the end of the night."

I didn't, and here's why, and it's ugly: the book didn't let her have the cute version. "Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe from here, too?" — she hears the glass go past the point where she can catch it, and she throws it anyway, and then she stands at the wall and looks at bare midriffs and thinks *floozies*. Her mother's word. The one I've been reading as fuel for twenty chapters, the one I wanted retired. It's not retired. It just turned around. She's pointing it at other women now, and at herself — "three dates, and she'd spread her legs like a slut" — and the book let it be that bad without softening it and without excusing it. I hated her for about four paragraphs. I've never hated her before. I think that's the first time she's been a whole person instead of the girl I was falling for on Randi's schedule.

And him. "Yes. Sometimes. When it's required." No defense, no explanation, the same man who said "not tonight" over the scar and gave her the truth with nothing on it. And then he *left*. He set the beer down and walked out and the door let the cold in and he left a twenty-one-year-old forty minutes from campus with a stamp on her hand. And I sat there and went — Pace. *Pace.* The man who gets the drunk girls home. But she said "I'll handle that myself," and he does the one thing he does, every time: he takes the sentence as the true answer. He's four misreads deep on Randi because Randi never gives him a real sentence, and here Vee gave him a sentence with venom in it and he took it at face value and obeyed it. It's the same play. It's the first time the play has hurt her. I've been waiting for his flaw to cost somebody and it cost the wrong girl.

That's the true thing said plainly, Meg-style, and it didn't catch on anything, and Vee sat in the lights coming up and took it. And then the cab that smelled, and the fare that "had been meant for other things" — the credit card from the shoe store is still sitting there, I haven't forgotten, and neither has the book. And then the shirt. She washed it. "It came back smelling of fabric softener and no one. She took it down and put it on anyway." I'd text that one at 1 a.m. with nothing after it.

What I noticed and what I'm scared of: on the floor, one turn from the actual question — "how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd been building out of wanting him, wish laid over man so neatly she couldn't find the join" — and he turned her and the floor took it. That's the closest she's come to the real question in the whole book and it wasn't about girls at a bar, and the dance carried it off before she could hold it.
