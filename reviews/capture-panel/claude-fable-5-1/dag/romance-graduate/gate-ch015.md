REACTION

Okay, so I'm going to be honest about the first thirty seconds: when Meg said "you have to tell the shirt," my thumb twitched. I lived the truck in twelve, I heard it told to Cassie in thirteen, and now I'm at a third table hearing it a third time, and I was already composing the note to myself about a book that keeps replaying its best scene because it's afraid I'll forget it. And then I noticed what she left out.

She didn't tell them about the buttons. She told Cassie — Cassie got "I took my wet shirt off in front of him, on purpose, me." Kayla and Meg get the shirt and the gentleman in the rain and "I was about to kiss him but he was shivering," which is a *lovely* story, and it's the story with her taken out of it. The book didn't say a word about it. It just let the version get shinier ("she could hear the story getting better in the telling and did not care") and trusted me to hold the other one up next to it. That's the thing I keep coming back to this book for — it doesn't explain its own subtext, it just sets two versions on the table and lets me feel the gap. And Vee's the girl who *likes the gap* between what people say they're doing and what they're doing. She's the gap now.

And here's the part that made my stomach go cold, in a chapter with cheese fries in it: while she's editing herself down to "he didn't touch me, he's a gentleman" for these two, the actual thing — her belly under the flannel, his hand held against it — has already been spent. He gave it to Randi *inside her* last chapter. "Her stomach is soft." She's protecting a moment that's already been traded. She doesn't know it exists as currency. That's the whole jacket in one dining hall scene and I felt it in my teeth.

Meg. "Everybody's different at the start. I said it about Danny. Word for word." And Vee "let it go by, it didn't catch on anything." This book keeps putting a dog in the room to bark at the right thing — Cassie, now Meg — and Vee keeps walking past it, and every time it's gentler and truer and it lands on nothing. I want to shake her and I also know I'd have let it go by too, at twenty-one, twelve days in, counting the days without counting.

Small stuff I loved: Theo, poor Theo, nice enough to wave. "They're all nice until you don't want them and then they're wonderful" — Kayla's the kind of friend who's right by accident. Powerlifter, lifts first thing, writes six hours and stops, "my writing's no good after that" — Vee doing his flat voice with *pride* in it, and I got a little jolt because that's the body from the trail and the forearms from the coffee shop finally getting a name, and it's such an unglamorous name. Twelve days. She didn't have to count. And the shirt folded on her pillow "at this exact hour," which is the loneliest, horniest detail in the chapter and it's not even in a scene.

Then the text. "Would you come to my house Saturday. Two o'clock." Nothing else. And I was Kayla, both palms on the table. Because *I* know what's out past the gravel. I know about the table with the top on it and the bench and the chair by the door that had nothing on it. I know Randi's already asked "do you think she'll like the bench." Vee thinks it's probably dinner. Kayla says "there's no meal at two, bring a change of clothes" and it's the funniest line in the chapter and I did not laugh, because bring a change of clothes is *exactly right* and Kayla has no idea how.

Where's Cassie? They're eating in the athletic dorm and Cassie is the athlete and she's not at the table. Nobody says why. I noticed. I don't think I was supposed to not notice.

This was a bridge. A good one — real girls, real fries, a joke every third line, nobody sanded down. But it's the second retelling chapter in three, and the heat has been on the other side of the house for fourteen chapters now, and I've said I'd start skimming after one more walk home. This isn't a walk home. It's the door. Saturday, two o'clock. If the next chapter isn't her walking through it I will be genuinely angry.
