REACTION

Oh, there it is. There's the collision I've been waiting ten chapters for, and it didn't happen on the bench, it happened over tomato soup, and I'm a little wrecked by how it went.

Let me start with the thing I can't get out of my head: "You don't have to tell me." I've read probably four hundred seduction scenes and I have never once seen the door shut like that — not by the shy one, by the *seducer*, at the exact second the secret is half out of the girl's mouth. And I know what it is. I read it three ways at once and all three make me want to throw the book across the room and then pick it up again. It's mercy — Randi wants her, and for one second she couldn't stand to take it. It's the bench — Randi has been on her knees in that house and knows exactly what he saw, and hearing Vee say it out loud in Vee's dreamy voice was more than she could sit across from. And it's craft — a door closed softly is the one thing that guarantees Vee will come back and open it herself, because now the secret is "still half out of her with nowhere to go," and that line made my stomach drop the way Vee's did. Randi didn't need the confession. She needed Vee to *want* to confess and be denied. God. That's the sickest thing anyone's done in this book, and it's the tenderest, and I don't know which and that's why I'm still reading.

The interrogation before it — "which hip?" "the left one" "oh, all the best ones start at the left hip" — I actually laughed out loud, and then I felt something cold, because that's a hypnotist's patter, that's a run of yeses, and I've watched men do it to heroines for years and I've never seen a woman do it to a woman at lunch over a wine that cost more than the other girl's whole meal. "Nobody measures over a bra." She *normalized* it. She gave Vee the permission that made the confession safe, and then took the confession's landing away. I texted my friend at 1 a.m. — I really did — just: "she said the bra too, and then Randi said there it is."

"It was cold in the house." "Cold. Hmm." I put the book down for that. That's the best two lines Randi's had. She knows the house. She knows he turned it up. She's been half-bare in that heat herself. And Vee doesn't know she knows. The dramatic irony in this book is now so thick I could stand a spoon in it, and it's not making me impatient with Vee the way this usually would — it's making me protective of her, because the book keeps letting her be smart in every direction except the one that matters. Reading the right side of the menu first. Not letting Randi buy her seat. The scuff on the left pump she tried to rub out. I know that girl. I've been that girl at that table. The debit card going down "as she always did" is a better piece of class writing than half the "literary" things I've DNF'd.

And the tickling story. Randi's "catastrophe" last time was a lacrosse boy; this time it's a Hermès scarf and a headboard and "a little helpless can be a very good thing. Done right." She's telling Vee about the bench with the serial numbers filed off. She's telling Vee what she herself lost in his bathroom mirror — "that's the only time some of us ever put them down" — and packaging it as advice. Vee hears it and gets hot, and the book is so precise about *what* gets her hot: not the man, "the man was nobody," but Randi with her control cracked open. The jacket promised me a girl who never lets herself notice she wants the woman, and I've been skeptical it could be pulled off without the book nudging me in the ribs. It's pulling it off. Vee looked at the camisole, "registered that she'd looked," and turned it into a thought she had a use for. That's exactly how it happens. Nobody explained it to me.

The kiss. The kiss at the end, in the fire lane, the second press "that had something in it the first one hadn't." I've read so many first kisses and that one got me in the throat — because it's *nothing*, it's a cheek-kiss that missed, it's "a French thing," and Vee has it explained away before her mouth is hers again, and then the real reaction isn't desire, it's *had anyone seen*, counting the dark windows. That's her mother. That's the same girl who blacked out at the froyo cup. The shame moved before the wanting did, which is more honest than any book I've read this year. And I'm noticing, with a chill: Pace's fire lane got a cop and a siren. Randi's fire lane, nothing. Fire lanes are a thing that happen to other people. The book is rhyming and I don't think it's an accident.

What I'm suspicious of: Randi turning the water glass. Randi not drinking the water till after. Randi's attention "wound a notch tighter than the coffee places had ever drawn out of her" — this was scheduled. Saturday was the fitting; Sunday Randi was at the curb. She went to him Saturday night — didn't she? She's got the report from his side already and she wanted Vee's. The book didn't show me that and I'd have liked to see it; I suspect the next Pace chapter is going to open on a deadbolt and a "tell."

Where it lost a half-step for me: the green velvet and Sinatra and the smell of bread — I felt the two paragraphs of restaurant before I got to the booth, and I'd started reaching for "get to it." And I've now had the walk-home-and-report scene twice in a row (Cassie, then Randi) — this one was doing very different work, and it justified itself completely, but I felt the shape come back before I felt what it was for. One more debrief in a row and I'll notice it as a habit.

Heat: this is a fully clothed lunch in a booth and I was more turned on than I was by the whole Steely Dan chapter 14. The heat is entirely in what's not said — "cold in the house" and the "yeah… yeah… the left one" descent, and the second press. That's the kind of charge I stopped believing books could do.

How I feel about them now. Randi — I'm frightened of her and I'm heartbroken for her in the same breath, because I think "you don't have to tell me" cost her something and I don't think she knows what yet. Vee — she said "seventeen days" in her own head without counting, same as "twelve" at the dining hall, and that detail is so *hers* that I'd follow her anywhere, which is exactly what Randi's counting on. Pace — absent, and the absence has weight; he made her a dress and Randi's the one who got the story. Cassie — Vee kept the wet thing from Cassie and almost gave it to Randi. That's the whole book in one sentence and Cassie doesn't know it yet.
