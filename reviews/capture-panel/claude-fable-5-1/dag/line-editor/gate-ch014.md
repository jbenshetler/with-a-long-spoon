So the book agrees with me. I've been sitting on that bet since the bench — that he reads her wrong and holds her, happy, not knowing — and this chapter took me into his head and let me watch him do it again, live, with her clenched around him and her eyes shut and her belly gone hard as a board under his hand, and he "took it for the want." I said in my notes I'd be furious if the book sided with him. It didn't. It gave me "If it was something else he could not have said what," which is the half-inch again, the same half-inch as "he usually did," and I'm holding it like a rail.

The "tell me" is the hottest and worst thing in the book so far and I mean both. She takes the question back off his tongue — "How was it?" into his mouth, no room for the answer — and then, once she's on him, she asks the real one, and what she wants isn't the trail. She wants Vee's hair. And he *gives* it. Sweet, and chlorine under it. And she laughs thin, not her laugh — that's the laugh from the sorority table, the one at Kinsey's pitch, the one she makes when she hasn't had time to pick her own — and I felt the whole room go cold while the two of them were sweating. Then "her stomach is soft," and he keeps back how he knows. The truck. He kept it. He kept the one true thing Randi said after the bench, too, and now he's keeping Vee's truck from Randi, and I'm starting to understand that this is what he *does* with women: takes the thing they hand over and puts it somewhere she can't get at. Randi asks tell me and gets true sentences with the middle cut out. That's every conversation she's ever had with him.

And her stomach soft against Randi's belly tight as a board — the book set those two lines a page apart and didn't underline them and I nearly did it for it. Randi's body is doing the bottle-cap thing again, the thumbnail pressed where it hurts. She doesn't know. He doesn't know. I know, and I'm sick with it, in the good way.

"I want us." Torn out of her, at his ear. Which us. Him and her — or the three, the thing she's building. I honestly don't think she knows either, and the book was right not to tell me. That's the withholding-as-shape I keep asking for.

Then she doesn't stay. The chair by the door with nothing on it — no cashmere blanket — and he *saw it* and set it aside, which is the most Pace sentence in the chapter: he notices everything and files the wrong thing. "No thanks" to food, bright, from inside the camisole. Her mother lives on egg whites. He offers to feed her and she won't be fed, and Vee ate the bread hot and last and cried at the horseradish and didn't care that her eyes ran. That's the swim lanes. Two women, one man, and the food is how you tell them apart. And she turns the deadbolt *back* — she's been locking herself in every time and tonight she lets herself out. The book has been building that lock for ten chapters and it paid it off in four words and didn't look at me while it did it.

The last two lines undid me a little. He eats at the table. Still hungry, makes more. I asked whether feeding was a man or a technique and the answer is a man alone at his own table at night going back to the refrigerator, and it's the first time the narration hasn't been in love with him — it's just been in the room with him.

What I didn't get, again: Randi alone. I got her through him, which is nearer than I've been, but it's still the wrong lens. She drove down the gravel and the book went with the sound of her car and stayed with him. Fourteen chapters. I'm still asking.

Small things. Chlorine — when? She didn't swim that day. Either Vee's in a pool some morning I haven't been shown, or he's smelling her sixteen-year-old self, or it's a slip. I'm choosing to believe the book knows. "Breathing like they'd climbed something" is the writer enjoying the pun, and "Every garment was an interruption" told me what four sentences of hands had already shown me. But the chapter earned its small vanities. I'd text you at 1 a.m.: she asked him about the other girl's hair while she was on top of him and she came on it and then she wouldn't stay for dinner. And he thinks it's because it was too good.
