REACTION

Oh, there she is. Cassie. I've been waiting for the book to give her a whole room and here it is — no Pace, no Randi, no citrus, just two girls in a rec pool that smells like every rec pool I've ever stood in, and one of them fighting her way into a navy suit "like it had insulted her family." I laughed out loud at that. I read that line to the empty kitchen.

This is the chapter I'd have told you the book didn't know how to write, and it does. Look at what it's doing with bodies now that I've had a week to sit with Randi's. Randi has built her whole life around being looked at and eats apples in thin slices; Vee has a body that "gave her nowhere to hide" and walks the bleachers like an entrance because the only alternative is walking them like prey; and Cassie — Cassie doesn't go to a pool to be admired, full stop, and the book lets that be a kind of freedom instead of a consolation prize. Three girls, three ways of surviving having a shape. And Cassie's the only one who isn't performing for anybody, which is why I trust her and which is, I suspect, exactly why she's going to lose.

"Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman." She could have thrown that up into the bleachers. She had the lungs. She kept it for Vee. That's the whole friendship in three sentences and the book doesn't underline it — well, it does, a little, "She'd kept it for Vee," but I forgave it because I'd already gotten there and the confirmation felt like a hand on the back rather than a lecture. And then the splash from ten feet off, without moving a step closer. Cassie never closes distance. Cassie never asks. I know this girl. I have been this girl, in a different suit, watching my friend go under.

The underwater bit undid me a little, and I'm suspicious of how easily it did. "She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem." Synchronized swimming at sixteen, the scull her hands still remember. The one part of her that was never taken. And then "grieved, a little" — the book got out of the way there, one clause, and let me do the grieving. That's the thing I keep asking for. Compare it to chapter 1, where I was told what Randi's face was for in about eleven different sentences. Here it just says Vee stayed under a beat past her lungs, and I knew.

Where I did catch it: the body-as-problem phrase comes back three times in a short chapter — "nowhere to hide," "a body to be a problem," "the body that was such a problem" — and the last sentence, the towel on the deck waiting, is one beat past where I'd have ended it. "The splashing won. It always did, back before any of it." Stop there. I'd already seen the towel. And I notice the book is very sure I love Cassie. I do love Cassie. But it's the second chapter running that has built her out of nothing but plain speech and loyalty, and if she gets to be right about everything and never wrong about anything I'm going to start feeling steered.

What I'd text you at 1 a.m.: this is the "Goodbye, Vee" chapter. That was two chapters ago and it's still sitting in my chest like a stone, and now here's Cassie standing in the shallow end "like a person," saying I'll be right here, and Vee going off into the deep where nobody can see her and nobody wants anything. Cassie already knows. That's what "goodbye" was. And the book knows I know, and it gave me this pool anyway, a whole chapter of the thing that's about to be lost, before the froyo man and the queen take her the rest of the way in. That's withholding as a shape. That's the book knowing more than it's saying.

I'm also thinking — and this is the persona of my reading, not the chapter — about how Randi's warmth goes to everybody in the room, total, undimmed, and Cassie's goes to one person and stays there. Vee's going to choose the warmth that comes back to her from the queen over the warmth that's been at her side the whole time. Of course she is. I would have. That's why it hurts.
