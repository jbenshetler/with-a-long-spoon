REACTION

This is the chapter I bought the book for. Not the dress, not the fire lane — this. Two people under a quilt with cold tea they stopped drinking so they could keep talking, and the whole cold night held on the other side of the glass. Forty pages of kitchen, condensed. I'd have taken eighty.

The robe first. He handed her the robe off the back of the closet door and took the quilt down without being asked. Randi gets the cashmere blanket at the foot of the bed and the chill that takes her every time. Vee gets a robe that smells like him at the collar, tied badly, fallen open, and he doesn't ask before his palm settles over her breast — "without pressing, without asking" — and I sat up at that, because asking was the whole grammar of him and the book just let it lapse and called it warmth. Maybe that's the bond turning. Maybe that's the courtship done. The cold "stood on the other side of the glass and leaned in" and I thought, that's her, that's Randi, and I don't think I'm making that up anymore.

The saffron did it for me. "Old enough to vote." Vee is funny in a way that's hers and not for anyone, and he doesn't laugh with her at the casseroles, he says *affectionate*, he goes "first to the hand underneath the thing." Yes he does. He goes straight to the hand underneath — and there is a hand underneath her whole semester and it's his and Randi's and the book let that sentence sit there smiling at me. Same with "She had stumbled into her. Literally." That's a wink. I'd rather the book not wink; it knows I'm ahead, it doesn't need to elbow me. That and "the language the world is written in, most people walk around inside the poem" — that's the one place he sounded like a poster instead of a man. But then "it isn't arrogance, it's almost grief," and the stars shifting for her because *he* understood them and turned it toward her like light — that I bought. That's exactly how it goes, being twenty-one with someone who can read a thing you can't.

Peter. His real name is Peter and the chapter's called Rock and I'm a church-basement girl too, I know what Peter means. Petros. The one the whole thing gets built on. And Pace is a name he gave himself, and Pace is his *method* — "How long are you going to take, Pace?" — and the man renamed himself after tempo. She said it twice. Once to try it, once to keep it. "The way you say a word to keep it." I did that too, reading it.

Then the hall. Seventh grade, taught himself trig for a graphics program, asked a question the teacher couldn't answer, and they put his desk in the hall for a year doing long division. "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment." Vee said that and he looked at her like she'd put her hand on a place he hadn't offered and found the right one — and I thought, that's the house. The house heated too warm. The man who was left in the cold hall for a year keeps every room hot and builds a riser and hangs a mirror and turns the deadbolt and can't stop feeding people. "Less than staying." Home was worse than the hall and "my mother stays home, my father works in a chemical plant" is all we get, and he took his mother out of the answer and Vee let him. Whatever closed at "parents" two rooms away is still closed. Good. Not tonight.

And then Daphne, and I went cold under my own blanket. "When I got there, they assigned me someone." Assigned. Nineteen and fifteen, two years, and he set every reason in its slot — "nothing happened, nobody did anything wrong" — and the good order was the tell, the book said so, and it was. Vee held the word down under her tongue and I held it with her. But here's what I'd text at 1 a.m.: it wasn't only the ages. It's the *shape*. Somebody older was arranged for him, and it was tender, and it was the best two years he'd had, and he calls it luck. And what is he doing to Vee? Arranging. Somebody older and glamorous steered into her path, "lightly, laughing, as if by accident," and the last paragraph of this chapter is Vee lying on him thinking it all came to her "for no reason she had done anything to deserve. She had never been so fortunate." He named his arranged thing luck and she's naming hers luck and they're both wrong in the exact same way, and he's *doing the thing that was done to him* and thinks it's the kindest thing he knows how to do. That's not a villain. That's worse. That's a man building the only warm room he ever had and putting a girl in it.

He turned the cold mug a quarter-turn against his knee while he said Daphne's name. Vee turns her glass a half-turn. Randi turned hers a quarter-turn and back, the night her bowl didn't move. He's caught it off Randi. Or Randi caught it off him. Either way the same hand's at that table and it isn't Vee's.

The scar. Her fingers went along it and he put his hand over hers and pressed it *down* onto it and said "not tonight," and didn't move her hand, and she turned hers over so it was palm to palm over the thing. That's the best withholding this book has done — a door with a time on it. I don't need it opened. I need to know it's a door, and now I do. (My notes had "Daphne or older" for the scar; it's not Daphne. Daphne got told. The scar's older than seventeen or it's later, and either way it's what closed at "parents.")

The lift, though. "Nothing in my mind but that moment. No future. No past." And she says, that's how you are with me, it's the same face — and it's true, I've watched him be all the way here, in the truck and on his knees with his face turned up. But he's also the man with the plan. You can't have no future in your mind and a schedule Randi's asking about at the same time. Unless the lift is where he goes to get away from the schedule. Unless *she's* where he goes. That would be the tenderest reading and I'm not sure I'm allowed it.

Where's Randi? Not here, and I felt her not being here more than I've felt her in some booths. That's the chapter working. Vee thinks about her at the end, the stranger she stumbled into, "the one she could bring the worst of herself to," and it's the second sentence of her happiness, right after the man, and she still hasn't thought about a fire lane once.

Six weeks. It's been six weeks. I've been reading this for what feels like a year of nights and it's six weeks, and she's wrapped around him with her leg over his, soft, not tired, and the book has never been kinder to her than tonight and never more frightening about it.
