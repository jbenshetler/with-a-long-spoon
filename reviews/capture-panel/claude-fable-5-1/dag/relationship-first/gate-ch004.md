REACTION

Well. There she is. Three chapters of the man and the face, and now the book finally hands me the girl on the jacket, and it does the thing I most wanted it to do, which is let her be *hungry* on the page without anybody in the room explaining her hunger to me. Vee walks into that lecture hall in the third minute of not looking at the door and I was already hers. I know that girl. I've been that girl. I've been the one who ran *we should sit together* both ways enough times to wear a groove — that line hit me somewhere old.

And then the seam I keep pressing on, which is how much the book knows. Because I've sat in Randi's bathroom. I've watched her fix a piece of hair and decide the face is going back on and not coming off again. So when Randi comes sidestepping down the row with her "whole face lit" — I didn't feel warmth. I felt the face. *Yes, like the adjective, no, it's not a coincidence* — that's a bit, Vee even clocks that it's a bit, and she still walks straight into it because the bit is good and the girl doing it is better lit than the morning. The chapter is called "See You Later" and the whole thing is a joke Vee is being let in on that she is actually the butt of. The sign over the door, sounding it out, *chi, latte,* "funny only once you're allowed in on it" — Helen Rivers, I see you. Latent variables. Read the chapter, it's shorter than it looks. I laughed out loud and then I didn't like that I'd laughed.

The part that actually made me put the book face-down on my chest for a minute: she can't remember a single word Randi said. The transcript is blank. "The leaning-in was the whole content." That's the most honest thing anyone's written about being charmed, and it's also the scariest sentence in the book so far, because the reader knows what was in the room instead of the words. Randi was working. Randi was doing on a stool what she did across thirty feet of lawn, and Vee walked home the long way, warm, holding an empty cup.

Cassie. God, Cassie. Four words a day and she spent one of them right. "Cassie," flat, not taking the bit's open door — the first person in this book who has declined Randi, and she did it in one word without even standing up. And then *Goodbye, Vee*. Not see you later. The whole word. Vee smooths it because Vee smooths wrongness, and I sat there going *no, look at her, look at your friend* — she saw it. She saw it in two minutes from the next seat over. I want to know everything about Cassie and I suspect I'm going to be given very little, which — fine, that's the kind of withholding I'll stay for. That's a shape. The girl in the ball cap with the lab at noon is the one person in this novel who isn't performing, and she just said goodbye.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: the mothers. Randi's mother lives on egg whites and Vee's mother lives in Vee's mouth saying *floozies*. Two girls being fed a version of themselves by their mothers, one starving herself down to the sorority scale and the other buttoned to the collarbone over "the better shirt" — and the thing Randi wanted on sight, in chapter 3, was the girl laughing with her throat bare to the sky, who'd *forgotten she could be seen*. Now I'm inside that girl and she never forgets for a second. She's reading the room like a ledger. She knows exactly what she's got under the cardigan. So the book's already told me the laugh Randi fell for was the one unguarded minute Vee has in a day, and I'm supposed to watch two performers find each other and call it fate. That's mean and I love it.

What I noticed, because I always notice: the book has done this catalogue twice now. The sage quarter-zips, the big scrunchies, "girls who had gotten ready together and would leave together" — I got that tableau through Pace's eyes on the quad and now I'm getting it again through Vee's at the window, and the second pass has less in it. And "the right pen" gets used and then explained, and *the better thing* is the same phrase in the lecture hall and again on the sidewalk. Small. But I read slowly and those are the sentences where I feel the author's hand on the back of my neck steering my head. Same with "It was the best hour she'd had in months" — the paragraph above it already gave me that, I didn't need the receipt.

Also — and this matters to me — Pace isn't in the chapter. Not once, not a text, not a name. Two women at a table and one of them isn't in the room because a man put her at it. That's the most interesting thing the book's done with its women yet, and it did it by absence.

The heat here is a low, real simmer and it's all Vee's. "A flat helpless want" — knees nearly touching at the high table, the hand between her shoulder blades for one second. She doesn't have a word for it and the book is disciplined enough not to give her one. The jacket told me she'd never let herself notice. I'm watching her not notice. Good.
