**REACTION**

"Sheri." The name went through me too. Not because I believed in the blonde (I put her down back in chapter 44, where Sheri was stealing his fries and telling him to get over himself). It went through me because of where it lands. Last night, Thursday, Randi was in that bed licking the two of them off him and telling him "she's not the only one in love." Tonight it's Friday, and Vee is at his little table with the end of her wine telling him about Danny's egg whites. Nobody mentions Thursday at all. The book doesn't point at it, and I sat there doing the arithmetic anyway: the sheets, the blanket, whatever Randi left lying around. Vee is mining the party for him the same way Randi mined Vee for him twenty hours earlier.

Then the dressing scene. "She dressed for the woman her jealousy had built, and she knew it while she was doing it, and she did it anyway." I liked that a lot. She isn't a dumb jealous heroine. She's a smart one who knows her jealousy is fake and finds out that knowing it is "a coat of paint." Meg's keyhole shirt again, the one she wore to the Rusty Nail, and Randi's induction heels on a taproom floor in January. She's armed with every piece of her history against someone who doesn't exist.

What she gets instead is the best moment in the chapter, and it's a look. Sheri's eyes stop at the keyhole and go down the bare leg "with no math arriving anywhere," then get stuck on the shoes with a soundless *oh*, and Vee realizes the look is *enjoying* her. She laughs out loud at nothing and a month of tension leaves her body. That's hot in a way I didn't expect, and it isn't a sex scene. It's a woman being openly wanted by another woman and finding out she isn't threatened by it, she's relieved. With everything going on with Randi, with the reach she couldn't close Thursday night, I don't think that laugh is only about the blonde dying. Somebody looked at her the way Randi looks at her, without the cover story, and her body said *oh, good.*

Sheri is the best new character this book has given me. "Same thing, told different." "And you get to motorboat them things?" A bartender with an ivy sleeve gets watched back to the taps with "open appreciation." The coming-out story wrecked me: "Whole tablecloth come off the table and nobody caught a dish." And "I'm not crying about it now, so don't you start either." She's loud, and there isn't one careless thing about what she says, as Vee notices.

Now what cost it. Sheri spends a whole page explaining to Vee that Pace is a gentleman at the dance hall: hands stay where the dance puts them, one drink, walks her back, gets a water. I've read that speech before. The bartender gave it in chapter 23 and the book replayed it in 43. That makes three people at three bars vouching for his character. Then come the donuts, the lock, the faucet, the bike and the bacon cheeseburger. It's the merit-badge man, now vouched for by a third party. I believed Sheri meant every word, and I still rolled my eyes a little at Saint Pace of the Dripping Faucet.

And "she had never once seen him blush." Yes she has. He went red on his knees with the pins in chapter 14, and I wrote down that it was the first time he felt human. It's a small slip, but I felt it.

The real payload is in the folder Sheri shuts: "Went bad with his daddy. He hasn't been home since he was sixteen." That's the parents door, finally opened a crack, from someone other than him. He was sixteen, so a year into college, mid-Daphne. "I could tell he understood it, and I didn't ask how." The scar is sitting right next to that sentence and I can't stop looking at it.

What scares me: "I know who you are." Sheri asked "And the redhead?" on a bike in chapter 25. She knows about Randi. She knows the redhead was a *project*. She sits across from Vee saying "you've got him happy, that's the whole book," and she means it, and she's also holding the one thing that could blow it all up. She's the only person in Pace's life who's ever pushed back on him, and now she likes Vee. That's either the person who saves her or the person who finally tells her.

The ending is lovely and quiet: headlights held on a door until the porch light comes on, "bacon cheeseburgers" said to the window, his hands at ten and two "like something full he was carrying." I'm full too. But this is a quiet chapter after a run of heat, and the Randi undercurrent was nearly absent from it, so the pull I feel is warm, not urgent.
