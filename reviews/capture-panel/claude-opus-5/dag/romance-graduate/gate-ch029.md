Okay. The pie. I did not expect to be undone by a supermarket apple pie with a label she made in the stats lab in a serif font, trimmed with the good scissors, and I was. That's the whole girl in one object — the three a.m. conviction that a joke is a safe way to carry something, and then the driveway, the engine ticking, and the joke going dead in her hands before she's even rung the bell. I've read a hundred grovel scenes. None of them have ever had a *prop that stops working.*

And then she's taller than him. God. That's the best choreography in the book so far and it's one inch. She put the shoes on as armor and the armor reorganized the geometry she'd learned him in, and she's going down for the buckles with the pie sliding and the apology coming out in fragments — and he lifts the pie away like you'd take a glass off a drunk, stands her back up, and *fixes the scarf.* Two fingers, a half second longer than the errand needed. And the little vanity sticking its head up out of the wreckage: *he noticed the scarf, the scarf is working.* That's the most human line in the chapter. The "beauty like yours made for the gods and loaned out" is a shade more ornate than I usually let a man get away with, but he says it in the hello voice and she doesn't know where to put her face, and neither did I, so fine, he's earned the one flourish.

Here's the thing that actually scared me, though, and it's not in the text. She walked up that hill in Randi's shoes, with Randi's silk at her throat, running Randi's script — *don't go in sorry, go in sure, he won't text you, give it a day* — and he waited a day because Randi told him to give her a day. The two of them arranged the whole reconciliation over her head and then he stood in the doorway and adjusted the scarf another woman tied on her. He knows that scarf. He *knows whose foot was in those shoes.* The book doesn't say a word about it and I nearly put the book down and walked around the kitchen.

And then he won't let the apology close. That's the moment I actually sat up. She built the clean machine specifically so she'd never have to say the why out loud, she gets it out beautifully, she gets the relief of having gotten through it — and he says *but what happened?* in his plain wanting voice, and the whole structure just isn't there anymore. And it comes out of her in the wrong order with the blonde's lilt in her own mouth, which she hates, and *she handed me a lie and I picked it up because some part of me was just standing there waiting for one.* That's the fourth time someone's told the blonde story and it's the first time it's been told to the person it was done to, so I'll allow it; the edit is the plot, still true. *Thank you for telling me what she said.* Who answers like that. Nobody answers like that.

Then the chapter does the one thing I'd have fought it on: *the evening went on a long time after it, first lighter and then not light at all.* That's Saturday night gone. Lifted out. I felt the cheat and I was ready to be cross about it —

The soak argument is the funniest two paragraphs since *I was on a box*: her whole weight against his back, lobbying, *soaking is a recognized method, you're scrubbing things that want to be left alone.* Two minutes. Maddening, reasonable. And then she swats him — she starts it, she's the one who makes it a game — and twists the wrong way into his hand, and it lands once, warm and wet from the dishwater, and her body says three things she did not authorize in the flat bright light of the afternoon with the dishes still in the sink.

The rest of that scene is the best writing in the book and it's all about *concealment.* She kisses him to cover what he saw. She gets her face into his neck so there's nothing to look at. She keeps the room full of her voice because *a quiet room was a room with a question standing in it.* And then the part that made me actually make a noise: she keeps moving fast on purpose, hiding it under motion, and the motion is the thing lighting it — the sting answering once for every stroke — and she can't tell anymore which one she's doing. Then she files the first one away in the little ledger: *there. That's only what it's like with him.* I know that ledger. Every woman alive knows that ledger. And then his hand moves four inches onto the print of itself and bears her down and the whole accounting department burns to the ground. *Please.* Twice. Attached to nothing. *This?* — *Please,* out of the bottom of her voice. And both of them leave it there, unnamed, hers.

He said nothing. He has a bench behind a closed door and a woman who said *please* on it in a voice he's never heard again, and he just found the same word in another mouth in his kitchen, and he said *nothing.* That restraint is either the most ethical thing he's ever done or the patient hand on the back of her neck, and the book will not tell me which, and I'm not sure it's two different things.
