REACTION

Oh, that's a *drawer chapter*. Those are the ones that kill me.

And the book knew exactly what it was doing by putting it here, right after the pastry. I came into this chapter still tasting glaze on somebody else's mouth, braced for the other shoe, and instead I got a girl being swung off her feet in a doorway and fed salmon and allowed to be happy for three short sections. No Randi. No Pace POV. Nobody a step ahead of her but me, sitting there with my hands in my lap.

The drawer, though. He didn't say *it's fine, leave your stuff.* He didn't say anything — there was never a note — he took the blades and the comb and the ordered ranks of a careful man and *compressed his whole self into one drawer* so she could have the top one. The easy one. The one your hand goes to first. I actually put my phone down. That's the most married thing that's happened in this book and neither of them used a single word for it, and that is precisely, specifically the engine of the whole problem: he builds her furniture instead of sentences. Dress, lining, mirror with the bright screw heads, a *form* of scrunchies because a pack would have implied an end date, and now a drawer. He will give her an entire architecture and not one noun.

And here's the thing that made my stomach drop, and I don't think it's an accident, because nothing in this book is: Randi found his undershirt in the second drawer down *without looking*. Randi knows that house by touch. Randi does not have a drawer. Vee has the top one. Somebody is going to open that drawer sometime, and I would like to not be in the room.

The other thing I'm turning over is her decision. *She loved him, and he had never once said it,* and then she went and did the most dangerous act of generosity in the novel so far — she counted up the door and the spin and the slowness and the warm tomatoes and decided she could live inside the everything-he-does and not go hungry for the saying. She closed the account. She absolved him of the word. Which means when the word finally matters, she won't have the standing to ask for it, because she already told herself she didn't need it — to herself, in the dark, where it can't be taken back. And then *Peter*, into his sleeping ear, again. She's hoarding it. That little word is the only thing in that house she owns outright and she keeps spending it on a man who's asleep.

Two small sharp things. One: the three-second hitch at the mirror, his eyes going off her face down to the counter and *holding there*, and her holding still to see what he'd do — and him doing nothing and saying nothing, and her carrying "the smallest tail of it" out of the room. That's the book being honest about what his silence actually costs, even at its kindest. He is generous and he is *unreadable*, and she has started doing the work of guessing. She always has to guess. Two: the weight. She's eating like a field hand and her bras ride higher and everything else she owns was bought a size up because the racks never believed a build like hers — that detail is so exactly right it hurt a little, and I like that it's going *this* direction, that happiness is quietly rearranging her body and nobody's making a project of it.

I did notice the chapter steps a whole week sideways in section iii without much ceremony, and the opening paragraph summarizes the sex rather than plays it — *some nights his mouth, some his hands* — which is the closest this book has come to a montage, and montage is where I usually get off the ride. But it bought itself out, because the montage was there to say *this is her normal now*, and the normal is what's going to be taken away.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: he gave her the top drawer and squashed himself into the bottom one and she can't tell if he minded her hairbrush. These people are so close to being fine. It's unbearable.
