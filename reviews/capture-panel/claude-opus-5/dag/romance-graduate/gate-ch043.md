REACTION

She left the bag in the car.

That's where I went. Not the crying, not the folded arms — the bag. Three weeks ago she said *I'm going to start bringing a bag*, and then a drawer got made for her so she barely needed one, and tonight she drove out there with it on the seat and didn't carry it in, and then stood on the mat *level with the chair by the door where her bag went on the nights she stayed.* She knew before she knocked. Her body had already filed the paperwork. I felt that in my stomach.

And he'd built the whole evening again. Heat up hours early so she'd never once be cold under his roof, lamplight not overhead, two glasses already poured, chicken going low, piano moving like water. The man cannot stop making rooms. And the smell got past her guard "into the old wanting and made it worse" — that's the cruelty of this setup and the book finally said it out loud: every single thing he does is an argument, and she has been losing that argument to a warm house for two months. So she buttoned her coat to the throat and didn't turn her mouth up, and he *arrived at the fact of it* and didn't understand. A book of doors swinging open before she reached them, feet off the ground, and tonight she just stood there and let the kiss not happen. God.

Then: **"That's not what I asked."**

She doesn't know she's quoting him. She has no idea. That is *his* sentence — he said it twice to Randi on that couch, *that isn't the same answer*, *that isn't what I asked*, in the voice he takes down instead of up — and now it comes back across his own hallway out of the mouth of the girl he's been handing authorship to since the lawn, and it lands on the exact same soft spot. And what he'd tried on her was so *cheap* for him. *Do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have than this one?* That's a comparison shop. That's the only unclean thing I've ever heard him say to her, and she caught it — went down the line of them, found it true, and said *that's not the point,* and hated him a little for making the true thing pull against the point. Which is the whole mechanism of this man described in one clause.

And then **"Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me."** I put the book down for a second. That's the best line in the book and it's hers, not his, and it's *free* — it costs the author nothing, no scene, no construction, just a woman noticing that a principle and a convenience are wearing the same coat.

Here's what's got my hands shaking a bit: he didn't go cold out of indifference. *The still of a man holding a line at cost.* Arms folded, face shut, nothing coming, and it's *costing him.* And I know why and she doesn't, and this is the first time in two books that being a step ahead has actually hurt instead of just being delicious. He can't say *girlfriend.* The word is taken. It's in an apartment across town with a woman who can't eat. He's not withholding a sentiment — he's refusing to *lie,* which is the one thing he has never done to her, and his whole ethic has reached the point where the only honest thing left is silence. *Believe what I do* is TRUE. It is also the exact shape of a man who has arranged his morals so he never has to be the one who decided. Everything in that house is real and the frame around it is a fraud, and he's holding the frame with his arms folded while she cries in his doorway.

And she wouldn't take the hand. **Not the hand in place of the word.** She has absolved him with his hands at every single turn — the drawer, the juice, the scrunchies, the salmon, *he hadn't told her it didn't matter, he'd gone and made her a place instead* — she sat in the dark two weeks ago and closed that account herself and decided she could live inside the everything-he-does. And tonight she reopened it, in his hall, with her coat on, and when the hands came she took the only thing she had and walked out into the cold with it. That's the four strokes from the pool, on dry land, with air in her lungs. I asked for it and I got it and it's awful.

*"Vee," he said behind her, once. Just her name, and her name was not the word she'd come for.* Fine. Fine! I'm fine.

Now. It is Friday, December 11. **Tomorrow is Saturday.** She just walked out of that house with nothing in her hands and a thing in her chest she has exactly one person in the world she tells things like this to, and that person is his girlfriend, and that person will have heard about tonight from him before the eggs come. Or she won't — maybe he doesn't call, maybe he sits in that house with two glasses of wine and a chicken and does not call, and Randi walks into a booth tomorrow blind and gets handed *he won't tell me what I am* by the girl she wants, across a tablecloth, with the goodbye kiss at the end of it. Either version is a knife. And nobody has yet told me where Randi was on Thanksgiving.

The only thing I'd say sideways: 45, 46 and 47 all end on the same sentence in different clothes — *not the big one he wouldn't give her and not the small one she couldn't find* / *not the big one and not the small one* / *what am I to you out there.* Three in a row, and by the shirt chapter I could feel the book making sure I'd gotten it. I'd gotten it. But 47 earns the repetition retroactively, because this is the one where she stops narrating it and *does* something, and the escalation from a dead nacho to an empty text field to an uncrossed four feet of floor is actually a build. Forgiven. Fully forgiven. Keep going.
