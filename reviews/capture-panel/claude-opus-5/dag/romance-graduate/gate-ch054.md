REACTION

Oh, he folded her blanket.

That's the line I'm going to be carrying out of here, and it's four sentences long and the chapter doesn't blink at it. *Randi's cashmere blanket, the one for the chill that took her after.* Not "a blanket." Hers. For the chill that takes **her**. **After.** Present tense of the arrangement, habitual, a thing with a known physiology and a known place in the house. And he picks it up off the floor and folds it and puts it back *where it lives* before he's even had water, with his head splitting, on the morning after he drank himself sick over a different girl. That's the Randi drawer cracked two inches and I nearly went through the ceiling. He's grieving Vee in a bedroom that has Randi's chill-blanket in it. And Sheri knows — *she find out about the brunette, then?* — so the only person in the dark is the one it's about.

The rest of it got me in a lower, plainer way. The mop and bucket still standing in the hall with the foam crust above the waterline, and him stepping around them and shutting the door — he cleaned up his own sick at some hour he can't account for and then went back to bed, and the bleach smell climbed the house to find him. Then showering *again*. The "again" did more work than the shower did. And the freezer rows. The man who builds a fra diavolo while a woman sits in a falling sheet eats vacuum-sealed chicken and broccoli boiled in the bag out of one bowl, alone, and a woman once called it prison food and laughed and he laughed with her and then she went away and nothing in the world changed. That portrait — the cook who feeds himself like a prisoner when there's no one to feed — is worth six pages of backstory and it took him half a page.

The truck. God, the truck. His head coming up *before he knew he had moved*, the fork stopped, his whole body gathering toward a gravel cut in a hill, and the engine **not slowing** — and then one second where the house just hands him the whole thing, her car finding the drive, her face finding him. And then he bends over the table and sobs until he can't hear it anymore and *for a few breaths after*. I have been mad at this man for a hundred pages for folding his arms and I was not ready to be taken out at the knees by an engine noise on a highway.

And then: *He was not hungry. He ate anyway.* Which is Vee's whole book, run backward. She was starving and had no meal. He has nothing but meals and no hunger. These two are the same wound facing opposite directions and the book just put them side by side and said nothing about it, which is the version I want.

Now the part I'm less happy about, and it's the middle. The Daphne pages. I've been waiting fifty-three chapters for that door and when it opened the prose went flat and short and started handing me the thesis in sentences I could have written myself: *He had taught himself that the words were the difference.* *He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him.* I don't need that. I had that. I had it from the folded arms in his front room, and I had it better, because I had to go get it. This book has been so disciplined about letting me do the work — the kiss at every place his hands had been, the wine moved an inch — and here it stopped and underlined. The list, too: *his tutor, then his best friend, his first girlfriend, his first lover, his first love.* Pat. Rhythm doing the feeling's job.

But here's what the flatness bought, maybe, and it's why I'm not actually mad: **nobody in that section says how she went.** *Then she left.* *Her departure consumed his life.* *There had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone.* Gone. Departure. That is not how you write a girl who transferred schools. And last chapter he said to Sheri, about her dead people, *people who've never lost anything think grief is a choice* — and the jukebox played I'll Be Home for Christmas and she said *somebody always plays it* and neither of them touched it. And *I don't go back.* I think Daphne is dead and I think this chapter deliberately walked right up to it in words that would also be true if she'd just dumped him, and if that's the play then the flat sentences aren't laziness, they're a man narrating around a hole. I'll know in a chapter or two which it was. If it turns out she just moved to Delaware I'm going to be annoyed.

What I don't have an answer for, still, is why *knowing* it changes anything. He's figured out the mechanism. Great. Vee is in an Ohio twin bed under a shelf of little gold swimmers inventing a blonde to hand him. The thing he's learned so far is that whiskey leaves every memory intact. Fine. Get in the truck.

Which brings me to the only real complaint: this is three in a row with nobody in a room together. The cold bedroom, the diner, now the hungover kitchen. Three chapters of weather. Each one good — 52 especially, Sheri stealing the fries and saying *then not saying it matters more to you than having her* and holding his eyes till he looked down, that's the best scene of this stretch and it's two people eating cheeseburgers. But I can feel the book circling the drain of its own held breath. Put her in front of him. Or put Randi in front of *him* — I'd take that even harder right now.

Also I notice the long unusually proportioned table in his bedroom did not come up this morning, and I notice that I noticed.
