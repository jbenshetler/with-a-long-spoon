REACTION

Okay. I need to put the phone down and think about this because I think the book just handed me the key to the entire house in the flattest prose it's used yet, and I nearly skimmed past it.

He was fifteen.

That's not stated. What's stated is: "He had been young and new to the university. Daphne was older and knew how things worked. The school called her his tutor." Fifty-something chapters ago I wrote down *the nineteen-year-old at fifteen* in my own notes. He's twenty-two and a PhD candidate. He started at fifteen. The school *assigned* him a nineteen-year-old woman who then became, in the chapter's own list, "his tutor, then his best friend, his first girlfriend, his first lover, his first love." And the book sets it down like a line item on a form and moves on, and I sat here with my hand over my mouth.

Because now I know what *"it isn't you I'm checking on"* means. I've been carrying that sentence for fifteen chapters thinking it was a fancy way of saying he's careful. It isn't. He asks every single time, out loud, he never assumes a thing — Randi literally complained about it in Gstaad, *Pace made her say it, out loud, every time*, and found a man who *didn't* ask and liked it better — because when he was fifteen nobody asked him. The courtliness. The staging her limb by limb with a kiss at every place his hands had been. The water before the wine, her glass first. The door he opens and then stands on the far side of, waiting, never once pulling. It's not technique and it's not even kindness exactly. It's a man building, over and over, the thing he didn't get. Every courteous act in this book is a reenactment with the ending corrected. I have never had a romance novel do that to me where the reveal was in a *subordinate clause about a tutoring assignment*.

And "gone." The chapter says *left*, and *her departure*, and then it says **"There had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone."** You don't say *gone* about a breakup. You say gone about a death. And Sheri — Sheri who doesn't talk to her people, Sheri who got *people who've never lost anything think grief is a choice* from him last chapter, and he gave it to her like a man handing over something he owns. He has grief. He's not telling me what kind yet. I'm going to be furious or wrecked and I don't know which.

The small stuff was perfect and the small stuff is where this book lives. The bleach having *reached the bedroom*. The mop and bucket in the hall with the foam crust above the waterline — he was sick and then he got up and cleaned it himself at some hour, alone, because nobody is coming and nobody ever has. The vacuum-sealed chicken and the vacuum-sealed broccoli dropped in boiling water. THAT is the thing. The man who builds a fra diavolo as a love language, who makes a woman understand the philosophy under a plate of food, eats grey bagged protein standing in a cold kitchen when there's no one to feed. *The bowl held everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted.* The cooking was never for him. It was never even really food. And some girlfriend once laughed and called it prison food and he laughed too and then she left and *no part of the world altered by it* — which is him telling me, flatly, that that's what he chose. Survivable. Unaltered world.

But the detail that actually made me make a noise: **Randi's cashmere blanket.** Third paragraph. He wakes up destroyed over Vee and the first thing he does is fold the other woman's blanket and *put it back where it lives.* It has a place where it lives. In his house. And then he spends the whole rest of the chapter on Vee and Daphne and the word, and Randi does not cross his mind once as a reason he can't say it. I had a whole theory — that *girlfriend* would be a lie the size of the house because Randi's on that couch — and this chapter says no, sweetheart, it's not that noble, it's a superstition he invented at seventeen. Which is worse. He's not protecting anyone. He's knocking on wood.

The truck. God, the truck. The engine on the highway and his head coming up before he knew he'd moved and his body *gathering itself toward the narrow cut in the hill* — and it doesn't slow, and for one second the house gives her back to him, and he bends over the table and sobs until he can't hear it anymore and for a few breaths after. The man with the folded arms. The man who set four words down separate and shut his face. I wanted it to cost him and it cost him and I got no satisfaction out of it at all, I just felt sick for both of them, in two cold rooms, not texting.

What I'll push back on: **"He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him."** This book has spent fifty-three chapters trusting me and that sentence does not trust me. I had already built it out of the bowl and the blanket and the truck. I didn't need the card with the answer printed on it. I'll half-forgive it because it's a hungover man finally saying it to himself and that's different from an author saying it to me — but it's the only line in the chapter I'd cut. And the Daphne paragraphs read like a résumé. *Tutor, best friend, girlfriend, lover, love.* I waited the whole book for Daphne and I got a bulleted list. The flatness is doing hangover work, I see that, and for two pages it's right — but the single most important thing in his history deserved one concrete image and it got none. I don't know what she looked like. I don't know one thing she said. I'd have traded the thesis sentence for one sentence of her voice.

And: this is the fourth chapter in a row where nobody is in a room with Vee. Gstaad, her twin bed, the diner, this. The diner was two people talking and it was great, I don't resent that one for a second. But this one has no dialogue at all and I felt the stall in my thumb even while the content was the payload I've been begging for. One more interior chapter and I'm going to start skipping ahead to find a scene.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *he was FIFTEEN. the tutor was nineteen and the school assigned her. go reread "it isn't you I'm checking on" and then call me.*
