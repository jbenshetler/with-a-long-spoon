Okay. *Okay.* I need to put this down and walk around the room.

Daphne. She was one sentence four chapters ago and I wrote her name in my notes with a little flag on it and I was braced for the book to never come back, or to come back with something cheap — a cheating ex, a dead girl, something to explain the brooding. Instead: he was *fifteen*. She was nineteen. The university *assigned* her to him. Two years. And the way he hands it over — "Nothing happened. Nobody did anything wrong" — every reason filed in its slot, the full-glass voice, each word set down separately so the sentence doesn't fall on the floor. And then: *It was the best two years I'd had.*

He named it luck. He called it luck to the woman whose shame he's been undoing with his bare hands for six weeks, and he doesn't hear himself, and the book does not step in and tell me what I'm looking at. Vee gets the word in her mouth — the "clean ugly grown-up word" — and holds it under her tongue because *it was not hers to say.* That restraint nearly took the top of my head off. Any other book on my Kindle would have had her say it, or had a narrator helpfully gloss it, or had Pace have a Moment of Realization. This one let her be tender instead of correct, and in doing so made her complicit in the exact thing that's going to gut everybody: she protected him from the true word. She's got a whole shelf now. The wet she didn't tell Cassie. The word she didn't say to him. These people love each other by withholding and the book keeps doing it without once nudging me.

And here's the thing that actually made me cold: this is *the blueprint.* He was fifteen, alone, brilliant, nobody's, and somebody older and lovely and in charge arrived and was tender with him and made him feel chosen and he has filed it as the best thing that ever happened to him. And now there is a woman with a Mercedes and a band she never takes off who pointed at a twenty-one-year-old girl on a quad and said *her*, and he is being courtly and exact and asking before he takes, and he genuinely believes every step of it is hers. Of course he does. It was his. Nobody did anything wrong. *She chose it, Randi.* He is not a liar, he's a survivor running the only playbook he was ever handed, and nobody in this book — not him, not Vee, maybe not Randi — knows that's what's happening. I could not stop thinking about Randi while I read this. Does she know? Did she pick him *because* he's like this? Because a boy who was taken at fifteen and calls it luck is the one man on earth who'd agree to the game and mean nothing bad by it? The jacket says it was a game between two people. I have been assuming she aimed it at Vee. Tonight I'm wondering whether, long before that, somebody aimed something at *him*.

Seventh grade. They moved his desk into the hall and gave him long division. For the year. And Vee — Vee who gets called a floozie in her own mother's voice — got it in one: *they took the thing you loved and made it the punishment.* She said it and his face did the thing where she'd put a hand somewhere he hadn't offered and found exactly the right place. That's two people who were each taught that the best thing about them was the problem, sitting under one quilt with the cold on the other side of the glass. I was fine and then I wasn't.

The math didn't bore me, which surprises me. A cup of paint that fills the inside forever past the outside. Shorter ruler, longer coast. Newton's three sentences for the ball and the leaf and the stars, same thing, all one piece — and then Vee looks up and the stars stop being decoration because *he* understands them and turned the understanding toward her like light. That's the sexiest paragraph in the chapter and nobody took anything off. "Most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it" is the one spot where I felt the author's elbow, the one line reaching to be quotable — but he's twenty-two and lit up about his own field, so I'll let him have it.

*Twenty-two.* I had him pegged as thirty. I'd built a whole man out of the joinery and the patent and the two towels and the heat cranked three hours early, and he's a year older than she is. That recalibration went through everything behind me — the house, the self-containment, the way he's so *finished*. He's not a grown man who's figured out women. He's a kid who got taken out of childhood at fifteen and built himself a competent adult out of lumber and procedure, and the procedure *works*, and that's the horror and the whole appeal at once.
