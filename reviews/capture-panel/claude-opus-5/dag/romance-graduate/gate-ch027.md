REACTION

Oh, this is the one. This is the chapter I've been circling the book waiting for, and it didn't give me one flag — it gave me three at once and only kept the one I most expected it to keep.

*Peter.* He just — hands it over. She asks what his mother calls him and he says *my real name is Peter*, and the answer skips the mother entirely, and Vee **lets him**, and the book doesn't stop and tell me she let him, it says one sentence: *He had taken his mother out of the answer. Vee let him.* That's the whole trick of this book in two lines. She's learning to read him. She's getting good at it. And then she says the name once, out loud, "to feel how it sat in her own mouth," and at the end of the chapter she says it again, softly, into the dark, "the way you say a word to keep it." I had a whole physical reaction to that. She's not asking him for anything with it. She's just taking something for her own, quietly, the same way she kept the shirt.

And then Daphne. God. *Daphne.* I have been holding that name since chapter whatever like a coin in my pocket and the book paid it out in about nine sentences and I felt them all. Assigned. A *tutor*. Nineteen and fifteen, two years, and then she left because she turned twenty-one and the bars didn't let him in. And he lays it out in that terrible tidy order — nobody did anything wrong, it was only sensible, it would have been stranger if she'd stayed — and Vee clocks the tidiness as the tell, because she's heard him undersell the hallway and the fifteen already in the same conversation and she knows what his voice does when it's carrying a full glass. She does the arithmetic *without wanting to.* And the word comes up in her and she holds it under her tongue because it isn't hers to say.

I said the word. I said it at my kitchen table. And then the book did the thing that actually took the floor out: *he was defending it — setting himself between the memory and the word she hadn't said — and he did not hear himself doing it. He believed every part of what he'd told her.* **He handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck.**

That is the entire novel in one sentence and it's not sitting in the right chapter — it's sitting in HIS chapter, about HIS fifteen-year-old self. A person older than him, in a position of arrangement over him, chose him, and it was tender, and it was the best two years he'd had, and he will go to his grave saying nothing was taken from him that he didn't hand over. *Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over.* That's on the back cover. It's the same sentence. He's not running a game on Vee that he learned from a book; he's running the only shape of love he's ever been handed, and he's certain it's the good one, because his was the good one, because it has to have been.

I'm not going to get over that for a while.

The scar. *Not tonight* — and he doesn't move her hand, he presses it down over the seam and holds it there, and she turns her palm up under his so they're palm to palm over it. A door with a time on it instead of a wall. I've read four hundred books where the hero deflects and it's coy withholding for pacing, and this is the first one where the deferral was itself the intimacy. It's longer than she knew. *More of it than she'd known.* Fine. Keep it. I'll wait. But the book has now put a date on that check and I intend to cash it.

The desk in the hallway for a year. The year. Because he asked a question she couldn't answer. And Vee — Vee, econ, casseroles, Ohio — is the one who says *they took the thing you loved and made it the punishment*, and he looks at her like she put her hand on a place he hadn't offered and found the right one. She's smarter with him than she is with herself and that gap is where this book lives.

The math went on maybe forty seconds longer than I needed. The horn you can fill and never paint, the coastline getting longer the shorter your ruler — those I loved, those are *his*, those are a kid making graphics on a beige computer in seventh grade. But "most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it" is the one moment in the chapter where he's performing for someone, and it isn't Vee, it's me. That's the only place my eye slid. Small price.

And then the last page, which I want to be angry about and can't be, because it's perfect and it's cruel. She lies there soft-not-tired, doing the gratitude inventory: *six weeks ago I walked into a stranger outside the dining hall and stood there apologizing with my hand over my mouth.* **She had stumbled into her. Literally.** And then: it came to her all of a piece, the one and then the other, *for no reason she had done anything to deserve.* She's thanking her luck for the collision. She thinks the miracle is that she's fortunate. She said *may I choose* to Randi's face a month ago and she has no idea she's the only person in her own life who didn't get a vote in the seating chart. I put the book down for a second after that paragraph and just sat there with it.

Also noticing: the heat was almost nothing here — a hand flat over her breast under a quilt, not pressing, not asking, keeping warm off her like she's the fire — and it was somehow one of the most intimate physical beats in the whole book. A whole paragraph about a spice rack with unopened paper seals and I was rapt. That's the book earning the thing it's been spending.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *he was FIFTEEN and she was assigned to him and he calls it the best two years of his life and this man is currently building a girl a dress on a pedestal*
