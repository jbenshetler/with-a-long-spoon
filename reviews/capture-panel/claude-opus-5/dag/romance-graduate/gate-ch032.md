The air guitar. He's twenty-two and this is the first time the book has let me see it with my own eyes instead of inferring it from a résumé. A man alone in his kitchen with the bass coming through the siding, head going, four bars of the worst air guitar she'd ever seen, *no shape to it anywhere* — and her hand over her mouth on the stoop like a thief. I would have paid for that scene alone. And then the cost of it registering on him in the doorway, that it embarrassed him to be caught unassembled, because of course it did, because he is a person who has never once been allowed to be a kid in front of anyone. She came an hour early and got the real one.

And then the flinch. She hits his thigh the way you hit a sticking door and the muscle gives because he's sitting, and it *lands*, and his eyes tighten at the corner, and she clocks it: *she had just watched the wall flinch.* That's the whole chapter's engine in one sentence. Everything after it is her finding out what happens when you push a thing that can be pushed. She pokes him three times saying get up get up get up. She strips his shorts off and waves them over her head like a taken field. She finds out she's faster than he is — not stronger, *quicker*, cut and reverse while all that mass has to come around like a ship — and gets drunk on it. And then flashes him to make him come after her properly, and some astonished part of her stands off and watches her do it. That's the girl who kept her steps small three days ago at a podium. God, the slope of this.

Now. The bet. "Either you admit you lost, or we find out," and the chapter is named after the second half, and I went cold and hot at once because *he already knows the answer.* He has been told. Somebody sat on his chest and told him about a kitchen and a wet hand, or he worked it out himself off the kitchen alone — the "there it is" is the same two words Randi uses, the same two words the dance floor got — and either way he ran a bet he could not lose on a girl who thought she was gambling. That's the cruelty sitting inside the tenderness and the book did not explain it to me once, thank you.

But then — then — the hand on the inside of her knee, going up slow, and she says **"Don't,"** and it is *gone.* Not a pause. Not a hover. Palm turned up and away, empty hands. And the air goes out of her and what comes back in isn't relief. "Yes." / "Yes?" / **"We find out."** I actually sat up. I have read five hundred of these and I don't think I've ever seen a refusal taken that fast and that completely, and it made the yes a hundred times hotter than if he'd pushed through it. He doesn't negotiate. He doesn't tease her about it. He takes the no at face value and lets her feel the shape of the hole where it was. That's not a trope, that's a man who learned something expensive. (And it is *also* the most effective possible way to get a yes, which is the thing I can never stop seeing with him, and the book knows I can't.)

The finger. The whole length of it, gleaming, "more of her than she would ever have admitted she had in her" — and the shame doubling, the wet and then the *plenty* of the wet, which is such a specific and true humiliation and I have never seen it written down. And he looks at it the way he looked at her under the tree when she said she didn't kiss men she'd known a few days. *Like it was true. Like it was fine. Like it was hers, and good.* I made a noise.

And **"Slow." A beat. "For me."** — *he had asked.* It was hers now, give or keep. That's the line I'd text at 1 a.m. He could have taken her clothes off. He asked her to hand them over, and the asking made it hers, and she loved it, and that is the entire thesis of this man in four words.

Then the thing I'd been braced for since the practice room: Randi's story rising up in her like something she'd swallowed. *Would she have to count out loud?* She goes into it carrying another woman's operating manual, dreading theatre — and what she gets is quiet. No counting, no audience, a rough palm resting and *gentling* until the skin wakes up and wants the thing it's afraid of, a first tap that's barely anything, soothing after each one, and his mouth pressed once to the warm pink of her. The book set up the loud version in a soundproof closet two chapters ago specifically so it could refuse it here. That's not a man copying Randi's script. That's a man who is nothing like the slight soft-spoken one who apologized to the furniture, and Vee is the only person in the triangle who'll never know she was handed the wrong map on purpose. Also noting, loudly: he did *not* take her to the bench. He turned down a bed. The door stayed closed. He's still holding something back and he's still calling it kindness.

Her hips lifting into it while her mouth says *I don't* — "she could not have said which one of them she was lying to." Fine. Yes. And "shame wants a witness, and in the small lit country of his bed there was no such thing as a witness" — which is both gorgeous and the scariest sentence in the chapter, because there *is* a witness, she just files her report on Saturdays.

Then the juice. The *juice*. He hadn't planned it, she could tell, and that's what undid her — he gets up and comes back with something sweet and cold and faintly ridiculous and makes her drink it before anything else, then puts her head in his lap and says wonderful, wonderful, quieter each time, until the word and the hand in her hair are the same thing. And she falls asleep *treasured*, for the first time she can remember. I'm wrecked. I'm also keeping the receipt, because aftercare that good from a man running a stacked bet is exactly the thing that will make the eventual reveal unsurvivable.

The scrunchies. A whole *form* of them, deep green with white dots, the kind you only find online, no note, far more than a person could use — "the plenty of them said the thing no note would have, that he meant to be looking at her hair a long while yet." After chapter 26, after her crying over her hair and *you're always beautiful* — he went home and bought a year of them. He gives her everything but the word, says the jacket, and he keeps paying in objects, and they keep being *correct*, and she keeps reading them right. That's the cruelest trade in this book.

Small gripes, honestly small: "My Victorian LARPing" pulled me out for half a second — it's the one line all chapter that sounded like it came off a phone instead of out of her, and this book doesn't usually need the wink. And the curry paragraph went a touch grocery-list on me. But the mirror monologue around it is the best kind of quiet — *why do I keep pretending we're not going to sleep together* — and "I'm going to start bringing a bag," said to him and to herself and to a green scrunchie, and meaning it. She's moving in one object at a time and nobody has used a word yet.

It's Saturday, October 31. She has brunch. She is going to walk into that restaurant with his deodorant on her skin and a bare bottom still warm and the whole thing to tell, and the woman across the table planted the seed and has never once stopped thinking about her. Give it to me.
