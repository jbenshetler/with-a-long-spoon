REACTION

Okay so the title is the whole book and I didn't clock it until I was three-quarters through — *we take nothing and leave nothing.* He says it about a cherry pit. On a rock. With his thumb still warm from pressing foam onto her heel. And the jacket has been saying *nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over* since before I opened page one. I actually put the book down on my chest for a second when that connected.

But here's what got me, and I want to be precise because it's the thing I've been begging books for: the sex in this chapter — and it IS sex, she's naked to the waist in a cold truck with a man's eyes on her — runs on her mother's voice. Not despite it. *On* it. "The voice was the heat and the heat was the voice." She's hot in that cab *because* the word floozy is landing on her, she wants to be the exact thing the verdict says she is, and she wants him watching her be it. Every other book I've read does shame as the obstacle. Something to be argued down by chapter nineteen, usually by a man saying *you're not that, baby.* This one makes it the fuel line. And nobody says a single word about it. She doesn't explain it to herself. I've read hundreds of these and I have never had that done to me that cleanly.

And she ran it. She crooked a finger at him through wet glass. She tucked a curl back and *set the flannel down beside her, folded, ready* — she staged it, she knew she was going to need it after, that detail is doing so much — and then she kept her eyes on his for the first three buttons to tell him where to look, and then looked down on the fourth ON PURPOSE so his eyes would go with hers and she could see what he saw. That's not a girl being undressed. That's direction. And he didn't lean in. He didn't lift a hand. "The gladness in him was the whole of the answer, and it was right." I'm going to be annoyed about how well that worked on me for days.

Now. The part where I get suspicious. He read the sky and said *it's going to come down.* He had a folded clean dry flannel behind the seat. He gave her his only dry shirt AND the cab, and then went and stood out in the rain with his back turned so she could be wet and cold and alone with what she wanted for exactly as long as it took her to want it out loud. Is that the kindest thing a man has done in this entire novel? Yes. Is it also the single most devastating move available in that situation, and does he know it? Also yes. That's him. I've stopped trying to separate it. But the gym bag was on the shelf behind the seat and she *hadn't seen it on the way up*, and I noticed the book telling me she hadn't seen it, and I want to know whether Helen Rivers means *he packed it for the gym* or *he packed it.*

Then — and this is the move that actually earned the chapter for me — she reaches for his hand to bring it to her mouth, because her body's already going at the next thing, and his hand is shaking. And she stops. She puts his hand on her bare belly under the dry shirt and says *you're freezing.* SHE stopped it. Not him withholding again for her own good. Her, noticing a person. "He had been a long way from the question of whether he was cold." She out-tendered him for one second and he let her and left his hand there a beat longer than he had to, and I'd like a minute.

The boots were the other real thing. He missed. He didn't ask about her boots, she was walking on a raw heel and didn't say anything for forty minutes because of course she didn't, and when she finally did he said *I'm sorry, I didn't think to ask.* A genuine failure, admitted flat, no performance. That did more for him with me than the flannel. Though I clocked that he carries moleskin. Of course he carries moleskin.

Things I am writing on the wall for later: **the russet dress.** Silk, brick red, in a lobby magazine, and she set it down on the rock between them and said the black one in the back of the closet would be good enough, and he said *that sounds like a beautiful dress* and did not pick it up. He's buying that dress. That dress is arriving. And it's arriving to an induction dinner where her name gets read out at the front of a room and **her mother will be sitting in it.** All three of those things in one room. I am braced.

Also — Meg's boots. She borrowed boots from *Meg.* Cassie is a varsity athlete with a lab and a locker and feet, and Cassie didn't get asked. And chapter eleven had Cassie's look going past Vee to Randi and getting folded away, and Vee deciding out loud that the smoothie place "isn't really her place." That's how it happens. Not a fight. A borrowed pair of boots from somebody else.

And Randi wasn't in this chapter and was in it the whole way up the mountain — *ravish*, delivered at the white table, hung up like a picture, and Vee climbed a mountain underneath it. Randi doesn't even have to be present anymore. The install is done.

The summit: "the world swallowed his blue whole." The valley not caring she was standing on the edge of it, and the not-caring being the most beautiful part. He watched her face instead of the view and *let her catch him* and then gave her the rest of the looking alone. Fine. Fine! And then the horseradish — I have read a thousand food scenes in this genre and none of them made my own sinuses go. Hunger first, then the food. That's what the hike was for. This book is teaching a woman to have an appetite by feeding her in the right order and it hasn't gotten boring yet, which I did not expect at chapter twelve.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *she took her own shirt off in a truck in the rain and HE didn't touch her and SHE stopped it because his hand was cold and I have never once seen it go that direction, also a campus cop siren ate the one sentence she was finally going to say to him and I am going to be thinking about that missing sentence until Tuesday.*

One thing on the tally, not a complaint yet: that's three times now the book has brought her to the line and then not crossed it — the tree, the door, the cab. I don't mind because she's the one who moved each time and this time the pull-back was hers, but I can feel the shape of the pattern and the book is running out of room to keep doing it. Cross something.
