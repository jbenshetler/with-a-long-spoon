REACTION

Okay. Okay. I did not see the name coming and I should have, and the fact that I didn't is the whole trick. The entire chapter I'm reading this as *the* couple — first-night-of-the-book couple, here's our hero, here's our heroine, here's the bench — and I'm settling in, and then "There you are, Randi," and my stomach dropped out. I actually said something out loud. Because the blurb *told* me. Randi is the friend. Randi is the one who steers Vee into his path "lightly, laughing, as if by accident." So I've just spent forty minutes watching the two architects of the book's central cruelty, before there's a cruelty, and one of them got taken apart on a woodworking bench and decided in a locked bathroom to never let it happen again. The plan in the blurb isn't a plan yet. This is the *reason* for the plan. That's a hell of a way to open a series.

And I'd text a friend at 1 a.m. about the water bottle. He leaves it out in advance because she always drinks more than she expects, which is sweet, which is the kind of detail I've read in a thousand books — and then she takes the sharp inner ring of the cap and presses it under her thumbnail, slow and steady, into the nail bed, where it hurts, and *she doesn't know she's doing it*, and he takes the bottle away without comment because he doesn't know either. That's not a romance-novel detail. That's a detail out of a much meaner book. The whole chapter is doing that — handing you tenderness with something under the thumbnail.

The heat: yes. Genuinely yes. Not one coy word in it, and more importantly it's slow in a way that's actually about something. The passes up her sides that turn back before they arrive, and her drawing her shoulders back and asking with her body, and *the point did not survive the next pass* — I've read a hundred edging scenes and most of them are a rhythm machine, and this one is a character study. The apple game on his lap is the best foreplay I've read in a long time, because she's using it to not have to say what she wants, and he knows she's using it, and he lets her. And the two *pleases*. The one that's the waiter-voice please and the one that comes from below. That distinction is the actual erotic content of the chapter and it's psychological, not anatomical, and it still made me hot. That's the thing I've been starving for and hardly ever get.

Him, though. I'm circling him with narrowed eyes and I think the book wants me to. On the surface he's the fantasy — courtly, patient, checks her hands and shoulders, doesn't get off himself, "that's not what this night was for," respects the locked door because of some woman named Daphne he wronged back when he thought love was permission. A hundred pages of that guy would bore me stiff. But he is *wrong at the end of the chapter*, flatly, beautifully wrong — sitting there moved by the girl who finally let someone feed her, quietly happy, planning to answer her hunger as often as she brings it to him, while she has already gone behind the face and locked it from inside and decided *not ever*. He built her a machine to find her with and it worked and it cost him the only thing he actually wanted, and he has no idea. The man who sees everything, and the one thing in the room he cannot see is on his shoulder with her eyes closed. I'll follow that. That's not an alpha, that's a guy about to be punished by his own gift.

Where it nearly lost me: the long inventory stretch. "He brought his hands up the fronts of her thighs, and the pale skin went faintly gold where the lamp caught the rise of them" — there's a run of that, several paragraphs of him cataloguing her body by lamplight, and around the hipbones and the unpainted nails I felt the prose admiring itself a little, and I felt how *little* she was a person in those paragraphs. Which, fine, is arguably the point — she's a surface being read by a man who's about to find what's under it — but "arguably the point" is what I say when I'm bored and being generous. The anaphora gets heavy too; the face-was, the face-had, the eyes-had-been. Once it's devastating, five times it's a stylistic tic. Trim a third of the looking and this chapter is perfect.

But the mirror section bought all of it back and then some. *Nothing happened to her. That was what the face was for.* That's the cleanest statement of a certain kind of woman I've read in years, and it's not clever-for-clever's-sake, it's load-bearing — it explains everything the blurb promises she's going to do to Vee. She's going to hand a girl to this man because she can't be the girl. She's going to build the invitation out of the one door she slammed. God.

Give me the next one. I want to see her walk into daylight with the face on.
