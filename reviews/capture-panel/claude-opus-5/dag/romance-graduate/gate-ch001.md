REACTION

Okay. I was braced for the wrong book. I opened this expecting twenty-one-year-old Vee, the hungry girl from the blurb, and instead I got dropped into a scene already in progress between two people who don't perform for each other, and I spent the whole thing thinking *this must be Vee* — and then the last word of the bench section is **Randi**, and I actually said something out loud. Not because it's a gotcha. Because of what it does to the blurb. "It began as a game between the two people now closest to her." I thought that meant they were conspirators. It does. But they're also *this*. They're a couple, they have a word, she brings her own cashmere blanket because she gets cold, he knows she drinks more water than she expects. The woman who's going to steer that girl into his path is the woman who just cried on his bench and decided never to let him see her again. I am *extremely* here for that.

The thing that got me, actually got me, wasn't the spanking. It was the apple. Thin slices, cut side up, because she eats them faster that way. A man who has run that calculation on a woman's food before she walks in the door — that's the most erotic sentence in the chapter and nobody's naked in it. And then later, when he thinks about it fondly, about a girl "portioning herself" who finally let someone feed her — and he's *wrong*, he's so warmly, generously wrong, he thinks he fed her and she's in the bathroom bricking up the hole he opened. That gap is the whole book, I think. He's a man who prides himself on not interpreting things for her, and he has just completely misread her, tenderly.

The edging worked on me. I'll be honest, I've read nine hundred spankings and I can usually feel the author counting strikes, but the *"Hi."* — crouching down to her upside-down face in the middle of it, twice, the second time to take her shoes off, holding her bare foot — that broke something open in a way the hitting didn't. And the two *pleases*: the waiter *please* versus the one from below. That's a genuinely precise idea about what submission costs and it's rendered in the actual texture of a voice, not explained at me. Mostly not explained at me.

The water bottle cap under her thumbnail. He didn't see it. *I* saw it. God.

And the flinch at "you're beautiful." She can take being spanked raw and she cannot take a compliment, because the compliment is aimed at the one he found and not at the face. The mirror scene is the most psychologically brutal thing I've read in this genre in a long while — a woman looking at her best feature and realizing it's a door she's closing from the inside. "Not tonight — and this arrived without words, quiet as a deadbolt going over — not ever." I hated that for both of them. I want to watch it cost them everything.

Where I twitched: the Daphne paragraph. One page before I'd been told, beautifully, that he waits, that he doesn't knock — and then the book stopped and handed me a backstory receipt for why. I didn't need it. I'd have rather wondered. Same with a couple of lines in the mirror ("She had been perfect, and perfect was safe") where the prose names the thing it had already done perfectly well by showing me a woman arranging a smile on top of a face that had begged. It's a book that trusts itself about 90% of the time and then gets nervous. I'd like it to stop getting nervous.

Also: he's still hard, he sets it down, "that's not what this night was for." Fine, hot, I believe him. But I clocked that the book has spent a whole chapter in his point of view and he has given away almost nothing about himself except competence and patience. He built a bench. He has a Daphne. He noticed she never asked about the furniture he made with his own hands and he let it go. I don't trust a man who is that good at not needing anything. The jacket says Vee walks toward him because everything in her has been waiting to, and I'm now very curious whether this man is kind or whether kindness is just his best tool.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *the twist is the narrator's girlfriend is the villain and she only became the villain because he was too good to her.* Give me the next chapter.
