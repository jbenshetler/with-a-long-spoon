REACTION

Okay so I read the whole thing in one go with my knees up and I did not check my phone once, which lately is the only review I trust.

The thing I'd text at 1 a.m.: **it's not Vee.** I spent twenty pages assuming I was inside the blurb's girl — twenty-one, starving, being handled by a man who slices her apple thin — and I was building her a whole inner life, and then "There you are, Randi" and my stomach dropped out. That's the best use of a blurb I've hit in years. The jacket *told* me Randi was the glamorous friend with the plan, and the book still let me walk in and get undressed as the wrong woman. And the second it lands, every detail re-cuts: the overnight bag, the lie to Brooke, the money she has and he doesn't, the fact that this is already a *relationship* with an established toy box and a word and a cashmere blanket she brought over herself in week two. So the "game between the two people closest to her" isn't two cold operators. It's this. These two. Which is so much worse and so much more interesting.

The heat worked on me, and I'm picky now. What did it was the withholding being *specific* rather than decorative — the hands going up her sides and turning back before they arrive, three times, and her losing the bite of apple halfway. The apple is the whole book in a fruit bowl: he noticed she'll eat thin slices and not thick, he laid them cut-side-up because she eats faster that way, and he's *sorry* about the thing behind her face that polices it. That's not alpha-male mind-reading, that's attention, and attention is the only thing that's gotten me anywhere in a book in about a year. And then the bench — reading it as a list of absences, the handholds, the open middle where a surface should be, "her hands closed on nothing" — I got cold arms. Her figuring out what it was *for* before he said a word was hotter than anything that happened on it.

And then the actual negotiation of the *please*. The distinction between the please she'd use to ask a waiter for water and the one that comes from below — that's the real erotics here, and the book had the nerve to make the climax a *vocal register*. "No." "Not yet." I was undone. Also: he takes her hands out of his hair because he wants them empty, and she puts them behind her back *herself*, and it becomes an offering without her deciding it. That's the whole consent architecture the blurb promised, done in one gesture instead of a lecture.

But here's what I'm actually sitting with. She ran. He's over there glowing — "a remarkably good evening," "all I did was find the hunger and answer it" — and she's in the locked bathroom deciding, quiet as a deadbolt going over, that he can have the face and the one he found is never coming out again. He hears the lock turn and reads it as respect-her-door because of Daphne, and he's being *good*, and he's being completely wrong, and he doesn't know it. The flinch at "you're beautiful." The water bottle cap pressed under her thumbnail where it would hurt — he *watches her do it* and doesn't clock it and I nearly yelled. He's the most perceptive man I've read in ages and he's about to be the stupidest, and that gap is the engine. Also, if this is the woman who then goes and steers a twenty-one-year-old into his path — I know exactly what that is now. That's not a game. That's a woman handing over a decoy because she can't be the one on the bench again.

Small gripe, because I promised myself I'd be honest about this one: the mirror section goes a beat or two past where I'd found it myself. "Nothing happened to her. That was what the face was for" — I had that, I had it from the flinch and the thumbnail. When the book turned around and said it to me twice I felt slightly managed. It's the one place the prose stops feeding me and starts underlining. It survived because the *last* move — the smile settling, the face lying, "Hi," "Hi," the bookend — is pure show and it's devastating.

The last paragraph, the top going back on, her walking past it tomorrow and not looking at it. Oh I'm in trouble.
