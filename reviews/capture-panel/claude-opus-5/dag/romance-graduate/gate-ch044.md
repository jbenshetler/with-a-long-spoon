REACTION

Okay, the shirt. The shirt got me and I'm annoyed about how easily.

Because look at what she actually does there: she takes the one object he gave her carelessly — the flannel in the rain, no tag, never asked back — and she turns it into a *request*, which is the first thing in this entire book she has asked him for out loud and in words. Not a stride across a room. Not a costume. Not *I'm going to start bringing a bag*, which was a declaration she made at a mirror. An actual ask, with the humiliating reason attached: *it went stale, and then it went to nothing, and one day last week it didn't smell like anything at all, so I washed it.* She has been sleeping in a dying man every night she isn't out there and she told him so. And his face doing the puzzlement-then-hurt because he thought she was giving it back — that half second where he was already bracing to not ask — and then him putting it on *over the shirt he was already wearing*, standing in his own warm kitchen in two shirts because she told him to. That's the whole grammar of him again, the object instead of the sentence, except this time she wrote the sentence and he just said yes to it. *All weekend. Smelling like me.* Fine. I teared up a little at a laundry conversation. Forty-four chapters and this man still hasn't said the word and he has now agreed to be *worn*, and I don't know which of those facts is sadder.

The hall is the best sex in this chapter and most of it has clothes on. She tows him by two fistfuls of shirt. The jeans — cold from the car, tight, no brace — and her getting one-legged and hopping and kicking them at the office door with zero dignity and *she'd have burned the house down for him*, yes, exactly, thank you, that's what it feels like. *You're not helping. / I'm helping.* And the bra: he doesn't pull it off her, he just **holds it** and lets her keep walking, so she has to unwind out of it herself like something off a spool, and then she's standing there with her arms empty knowing she lost and putting her mouth back on him anyway. That's the thing this book does that nobody else on my Kindle does — he wins by *not moving*, and she knows she's being handled and goes in anyway because being handled is the point.

And her telling on herself. She called the five nights. She asked for them, she needed them, and some corner of her wanted him to *fight her for them* and he just said take what you need, I'll be here, and the grace of it cost her more than a fight would have. That's a grown woman's thought and I've read four hundred books where the hero would have sulked for a chapter to manufacture the conflict. Then the body files the receipt anyway — too loud, far too soon, *her body having just published the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private.* Her own throat ratting her out. Delicious.

Now. The pizza. Helen, my love, we have *done* this. We did the awakened-mouth aria two chapters ago at brunch — *like my mouth had been asleep since I was born, like I'd been eating with the sound off* — and it was beautiful there because Vee was reaching for it live, in front of someone, and couldn't find the words. Here I get the full procedure (bread machine, romas skinned, cornmeal on the stone, the snap of the wrist, water buffalo) and then the taste inventory *itemized for me* — the yeast, the acid, the late heat, the crunch, the mushroom holding the wine — and then the chapter stops to explain the point to me: cotton candy versus a bowl of cut strawberries. I got it the first time. I got it the second time. This is the only place this book ever treats me like I might miss something, and it's always over food. I skimmed. I actually skimmed, for the first time since the early twenties, and I noticed myself doing it and felt bad for both of us.

Also: *the long, unusually proportioned table that stood in the middle of his bedroom.* I'm sorry. **What table.** She throws a thong onto it and never looks and the narration goes out of its way to tell me its proportions are unusual, which is this book winking at me with its whole face. That's the raised door the jacket promised and it's been sitting in that room for forty chapters while I was looking at drawers. Text me when.

And the hole where Randi should be. We came out of the glass room on the fourth floor with MIRANDA sitting over the worst point in the county and *cheating bastards* said ragged to a screen, and this chapter doesn't touch it — not a text, not a thought in the car, nothing. Two for two on Randi never getting her own room.

I want that.
