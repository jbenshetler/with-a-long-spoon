REACTION

Oh, she kissed her. *She* kissed her. Four months of Randi leaning in across a tablecloth and doing the half-inch and getting a sweet girl who held still for it — and Vee gets her hand on the latch, and doesn't open it, and turns around and goes *back*, and takes her face in both hands under fifty years of sorority girls all looking slightly to the left. And then the thing I didn't know I needed: she *stayed, because she only knew she wasn't finished.* That's the exact mechanism of this whole book — Vee doesn't understand her own wanting, she just doesn't stop. She did the same thing with the second step, with the photograph, with the half-inch at the curb. "She only knew she wasn't finished" is going on a list.

And then the POV flip and I actually made a noise. The back of her hand to her own mouth, a thing she hasn't done since she was fifteen. The one short idiotic laugh in a hall with nobody in it. "Somewhere behind the noise a sentence was trying to assemble itself, something with *means* in it, and she left it where it was" — that is the single most Randi sentence in two books. She can run any scenario to the end and she will not run *that* one, so she goes to get matcha with her heart banging like she's come up three flights. I loved her for one entire minute. One.

Because: the advice. God, the advice. Everything Randi says to that girl on the bed is *correct*. She takes the three-a.m. blonde apart with her bare hands — "fear needs a face to point at, so you gave it one" — she reframes the folded arms as a boy holding himself still so it won't show how much Vee got him, she asks *how do you think that felt from where he was standing*, which is the first time in fifty-five chapters anybody has handed Vee the other side of the table. It's good counsel. It's *true*. And she can give it because she's been on that couch. She knows exactly what that man is, she knows he says *I am* about Vee, she has insider knowledge of the one thing Vee is dying for and she spends it as technique. Every word of comfort is also a thing she can't say the source of. And at the end of the good advice she sends Vee out the door bare under a coat, in *Randi's shoes*, pressed into her hands — wear these — and that's not styling, that's a hand on the body, that's Randi's shoes walking into that house on Vee's feet tonight. "Somebody has to get her used to my mouth." This is the groundwork and it is wearing the mask of the kindest hour of Vee's life.

And here's where I sat up and checked the book: Vee says the name. *Sheri.* Randi has *met* Sheri — hugged her at the dance hall before Thanksgiving. She could have ended Vee's whole invented blonde in four words: five foot nothing, permed, not it. Except — no. Sheri said it herself: *never did learn my name.* Randi walked past that woman and never got her name, so the name on the bed means nothing to her. The book closed its own hole before I got there and I'm so pleased I could spit. She dismantled the fear without the fact, because she *didn't have* the fact. Fine. Fine! But she still doesn't know she's one of the three people who could have ended that fear with one sentence, and that's the shape of this entire series.

The slut thing worked on me, which annoys me, because it's the oldest lever there is and the book knows it and plays it on the *inside*: her mother's voice worn smooth into her own, the burn going up her neck, and then — "the wanting coming up hand in hand with the mortification and sharper for it." That's the line. That's the whole engine of Vee in one clause, and it's why I'm still here and not reading someone's hockey captain. She's not being talked into something she doesn't want; she's being walked past the part of herself that would rather die, and she knew she'd do it "since the coat, maybe."

Then Brooke. Brooke, who has been two lines of warmth in the background for two books, and who turns out to have teeth and knows to the inch what every room in that house is worth. *You always say goodbye under the composites.* The absolute stillness of nothing moving in her face. And then the terms, laid out kind as anything: play, privately, or for the boys, and nobody says a word — *perform* it and the house will applaud, *mean* it and you're out. "I'm asking whether you want to keep your position." And Randi — who has never once had to be brave, who said so herself in that hotel room, who arranged her whole life so she'd never have to be — gets handed the bill for her best minute roughly ninety seconds after having it. The mug cold in her hand that she never put anything in. She puts it back in the cupboard. Empty. She doesn't even get the twenty warm minutes.

What I'm sick about: Vee is going to walk out to that house tonight, and we have just spent two entire chapters inside a man who sobbed over a truck that didn't turn in and ate cold chicken anyway because his body needed it. It's going to *work*. That door opens and that coat comes off and it will be the best night either of them has had. And it still won't get her the word, because the word isn't hers to get — it's Daphne's, and it's on the couch with Randi, and the coat is a peace sign that lets them both skip the conversation she walked out of his house to force. Randi literally said it: *you don't do it with words. Words are the whole war.* She has just taught Vee to stop asking, in the kindest possible voice, and Vee is grateful.

Also nobody — not Brooke, not Randi, not the raccoon joke — has said one word about the fact that this girl is disappearing. Fifty-five chapters. I'm keeping count.
