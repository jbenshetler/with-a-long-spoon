REACTION

She left the bag in the car.

That's the whole chapter for me and it happens in a subordinate clause. Four chapters of this woman building her *I'm going to start bringing a bag* into a life — a drawer, a shirt she hands him to refill, a smell she holds in the dark — and tonight she drives out there with nothing in her hands and stands on the mat and doesn't come into the room. She knew before she got on the highway. Her body made the decision and her mouth caught up at the door. I actually said *oh* out loud.

And God, the house doing its worst to her. He brought the heat up hours ago because she's never once been cold under his roof, and *it went through her now, and she hated that it did* — that's the line. The wine already poured. The piano already going, because she has never heard it start. Every single object he has ever used instead of a sentence is standing there in the room pulling on her while she tries to hold a jaw set. The book has spent two hundred pages teaching me to go soft at that inventory and then uses it as a weapon pointed at her. That's not a gentle thing to do to a reader and I loved it.

**"Words are cheap. Believe what I do."** / **"Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me."**

I have read that first line a hundred times in a hundred books where it was the romantic answer, the swoony one, the point. Here it's the dodge, and she turns it over in her hands in one breath and hands it back ruined. *You still won't spend them on me.* If it's cheap, then it costs you nothing, so what is it costing you. She's twenty-one and she got there on her feet in a doorway with her coat buttoned. The whole book she's been the one who keeps, who doesn't ask, who converted not-asking into fidelity and was proud of it — and she just dismantled a man's best defense with his own sentence.

And then his arms came up and folded and he *held*. "The still of a man holding a line at cost." That's new. Every other time she's pushed, he's answered — the don't obeyed in half a second, the *but what happened?*, the too-loud-rooms explanation that bought him the whole of chapter forty-seven. Tonight there's no object and no explanation, just a man with his arms crossed paying to say nothing. Which means it isn't shyness, isn't youth, isn't a man who doesn't have the word. He has it and he is *holding* it. **Not yet.** I wrote that down off a bicycle six chapters ago and it's standing in his front room now with its arms folded.

The other thing he did: *do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have than this one?* I watched that work on her and I watched her name it — he made the true thing pull against the point — and *that's not what I asked* is the best thing she's said in the book. He argues like a mathematician. He reframes the question into one he can win. Courtly, exact, and sideways, and she caught it mid-air.

Then: the hand lifting toward her. **Not the hand in place of the word.** That's the thesis of forty-nine chapters, set down in nine syllables, and it's the only time this author has let the subtext be said out loud and it earned it, because *she's* the one saying it now, inside her own head, moving away from the hand. She has been bought with objects all book — scrunchies, a drawer, a plate over a bowl, juice held to her mouth — and tonight she priced them and walked. In the cold. *And it was worse, and she did not go back in.* Same shape as the shirt in the dorm — took it off, the cold was worse, *stayed in it.* This woman has started choosing the worse thing on purpose because at least it's hers. That scares me more than anything Randi's done.

What I'm suspicious of: he's not withholding out of fear of commitment. He's withholding because of what's behind the locked drawers, and one of those drawers is a woman with a green Mercedes who *picked her* — and the jacket told me the three of them chose this table before Vee was anything to anybody. He can't say *I love you* to a girl he was handed. Or he can't say it since he was fifteen, since whatever *I don't go back* is. Either way the word isn't missing, it's mortgaged, and she just walked out over a debt she doesn't know exists.

Small thing that nags: the speech in the middle, the *you take me into your warm house, you cook for me, you touch me with such care* aria. I've read all of that. I was there. Having her recite the ledger to his face is the one place this book came close to explaining itself to me, and if the next chapter is Vee sitting in a car thinking it again I'll start skimming for dialogue.

But it won't be, will it. She's in a Corolla on a dark highway at ten at night with no bag and nowhere to be that isn't a dorm room with a cracked window and Cassie asleep under her. And there's one person she'd call. One person who's been waiting four chapters for exactly this phone to ring. Randi is going to get her tonight, raw, crying, with nothing between them, and the terrible thing is that Randi's comfort will be *real.* **That** is what I'd text at 1 a.m.: she's going to drive straight to the woman who built this, and the woman who built this is going to be the kindest anyone has ever been to her.
