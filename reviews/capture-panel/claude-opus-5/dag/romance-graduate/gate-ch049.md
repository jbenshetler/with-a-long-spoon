REACTION

Okay so I need to say the thing first, because I've been sitting here with my thumb off the screen for a full minute: **he always wipes.**

That's what that ending is. Hundreds of times — she *counted*, she started the count and gave it up, dozens, hundreds — he has put his mouth on her and then come up and kissed her, and it has *never once* tasted like that. And she hands herself the sweet little explanation, he forgot, this once, poor man, bad luck that the once made her sputter. And I'm lying in bed at 1 a.m. going: no, sweetheart. A man doesn't develop a hundred-times-perfect habit of wiping his mouth before he kisses you because he's fastidious. He develops it because the habit has to work on nights when it isn't you. *"How many times, and how carefully, and had there always been—"* and it slid away when she reached for it, and it **did not go all the way**, it settled under the affection, minor-key, waiting. That's the best last paragraph this book has given me and it's doing it with a flavor. Not a text message, not a lipstick on a collar. **Salt.** Her own tongue going out over her own lip afterward, looking for something, and her refusing to ask herself what it was looking for.

And then — *and then* — "Tuesday there's Randi, I'll tell her everything. Well. Nearly everything. The strange small thing, I'd have to find a way to say it that didn't sound like what it sounds like. Randi would know what to make of it." **Randi would know what to make of it.** She is going to carry the one loose thread in the whole weave across campus and hand it to the woman it leads back to. I actually said "oh my God" out loud.

Now back up, because the first two-thirds of this is the happiest I've been in this book and I want to be fair about it. She wakes up alone and for one lurch she's back in December and then his voice arrives from the night before — farmers market, sleep as long as you like — and the bread is already in the house, *timed so she'd wake inside the smell of it*. "Even his absence had been cooked in advance." That's the man in one line, the gorgeous part and the terrifying part at once: he arranges everything, including the hours he isn't there.

And then the sweats folded at the foot of the bed and she looks at them and **leaves them.** The whole bit where she reasons her way from *pleasure slave* to **pleasure princess** — counting it off on her fingers in the hallway, bare as the day she was born, sugaring with laughter — I loved it, and I was braced not to. A 21-year-old who spent since she was fourteen folding her body small, running the audit and arriving at *no, actually, I am the one being served here*, and shimmying at herself in a hallway about it. That's not a trope. I've never read that. The girl who said "it's such a slutty thing to do" twenty-four hours ago is spreading **the cashmere blanket** on the bed before he gets home — Randi's blanket, the one he folds and puts back where it lives, and Vee has no idea, and she chose it because it's the softest thing in the house, and she wrecks it. The book put that under her on purpose and said nothing. I'm unwell.

The breakfast. I said in my notes the food was starting to compress into one pan and I take it back for this one, because the blood orange is going to stay: top and bottom off, the knife riding the curve in long paring passes till nothing white is left, the segments freed whole off the membranes, the husk wrung out in his fist like a rag. *Nothing between anybody and the fruit.* And then he does the exact same thing to her an hour later and the book has the nerve to say so — "he was opening her as he had opened the orange, nothing hurried, nothing skipped, everything in the order the fruit demanded." I usually hate it when a book points at its own rhyme. This one I let have, because I'd already made it myself and it was fun to be met.

And the burnt toast. *Whoa—* and the rest of the sentence never arriving, and him reassembling his composure **like a man picking up dropped change**, and then "I got mushrooms." A man whose composure felt like a law of nature, and she's broken it twice in twelve hours, and she's keeping count. "I can put the sweats on, if you need to concentrate." / "No," before she's finished the sentence. That's the best comic beat in three books and it's *also* her learning she has power, which is the actual plot.

The sex: the pointing. Her lying on the cream field with her arms laid open like flowers and raising one finger at the inside of her own wrist, and the hurry **dying at her skin.** Yes. That's the whole thesis of him rendered as a game — he has infinite patience and she has just discovered she can aim it. *Bad slave.* The thoughts going to single words, "small lit windows going dark one street at a time." Her hand flying into his hair and then **making itself let go, finger by finger,** because pulling him up would end it and nothing could be allowed to end it — that's a kind of self-command I have genuinely never seen written in a sex scene. It was long. I did at one point notice I was at her feet and thought *we have the entire other leg to go.* But the toes got a trill out of her and I forgave it.

And the shame beat, the one this book keeps doing better than anyone: *there was so much of her, there always was, the give of her that came whether she permitted it or not — and he did not skip past it or pardon it or wait for it to thin. He was drinking it.* Hundreds of books and that is still the rarest thing on the shelf, a heroine's body being *too much* in her own head and a man treating the too-much as the point.

Then the flinch. She tastes herself and her tongue says yes **before her mind arrives** — "her mouth answering a question it had not been asked, curious, unforbidden, hers" — and then the installed voice comes up from very far down and shuts it over the tongue's head. *Ugh — gross.* The back of the hand across the mouth, the child's gesture. And her legs clamping before the no can be misfiled — "not that. It's fine. Don't stop. Don't you dare stop." And him reading *which no was which*, and one economical wipe, and **he did not push it and he did not sulk and he did not explain.** And then not going back to her mouth. Leaving her mouth for her, until she came and took it. That is the single most romantic thing he has done in fifty-three chapters and he did it by *omission*, which is of course the only way he can do anything.

But. Here's the splinter I'm going to be picking at all week. **She stopped asking.** She drove out there with Randi's strategy — *you don't do it with words, words are the whole war* — and it worked, spectacularly, and now she's lying under cashmere thinking "tonight there was no room in her for wanting anything he hadn't given." She went to that house in December and asked a man for a noun and he folded his arms. She came back naked and got fed and the question has been **taken off the table** — not answered, *retired*, exactly like the cracker-and-meal sentence went on the pile. The pile is getting tall and she hasn't looked at it once. And who talked her out of asking? The woman who cannot afford for Vee to ever get a word. Randi handed her a sword, sure. Randi also handed her a plan whose entire mechanism is *never ask him what you are again.* I don't think Randi consciously engineered that. I think she did it in about four seconds flat with her whole chest, the way you breathe.

What I'd text my friend at 1 a.m.: *HE WIPES. he wipes every time. she tasted salt and decided he forgot and she is going to go tell RANDI about it on tuesday.*
