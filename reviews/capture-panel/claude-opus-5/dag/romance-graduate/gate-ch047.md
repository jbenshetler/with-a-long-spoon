REACTION

Oh, *ouch*. Okay. This is the chapter I've been waiting for and it arrived sideways.

She built the sentence. She carried it assembled for weeks like a woman carrying a glass of water through a crowd. *I had been eating crackers my whole life, and you fed me a meal.* And it's a good sentence — it's the best thing she's ever said out loud in this book, it's *her*, it's the thing she told Randi in the booth about her mouth being asleep since birth, except this time she's handing it to the person it's about instead of to the woman who collects her. And she worked out the loophole so carefully: not the words, but the shape of the words, close enough to carry the freight, deniable enough that she doesn't break the rule she discovered by bumping into it from the inside. That line about not knowing the rule was a rule until she hit it from inside — that's the truest thing in the chapter.

And he says *I love how good we are together.*

I had to sit back. Because he *tried*. The two breaths — I noticed the two breaths, the hand that starts two responses and puts both down. That's the most visible he's ever been on the page from her side. Something landed on him. He felt the size of it. And what came out was the word *love* attached to a *how*, and then he made it about what her *body* does. *What you do — what your body does — I've never.* Honey. She handed you her mouth waking up and you gave her back her hips.

And here's where the book got me, and it's the cruelest, most honest thing it's done: the kiss on her hair was *longer than his usual kisses*. He held it. He put his cheek on her crown. He gave her the whole physical answer, correctly, generously, the way he gives everything — and so the thing underneath her breastbone, the small *wait*, went down into the body where small waits go, because the quilt was warm. The object instead of the sentence. Again. He has never once in forty-seven chapters answered a sentence with a sentence. He answers with a drawer, a plate, a scrunchie, a pie lifted out of her hands, a kiss held half a second long. And it has *worked* every single time, and it worked this time too, for about forty hours, until she was sitting on a folding table in a laundry room with the dryer going round.

The laundry room is the part I'd text somebody about at 1 a.m. Not the porch — the laundry room. *The two things had not been the same shape.* She gets all the way to the edge of it and then: *either end of it hurt.* Maybe he understood and chose to say it that way. Maybe it went past him entirely. And she can't test it, because testing it means saying the words, and the words go through him first. That's airtight. That's the whole architecture of her and I've been watching it get built since the drawer chapter and now she's standing inside it.

And then she *files it.* "The place was not empty." *There were other things in it already — small flickers, moments across the fall where what she knew about him had had to do small repair work to keep functioning.* THAT. That's the pile I've been keeping in my notes — the scar, Randi, the blonde at the bar, *not yet*, the photograph he never said a word about — and the book just told me she has the pile too, and she doesn't look at it. She has a locked drawer now the same as his, except his holds secrets and hers holds the evidence.

*The cracker-and-meal would not return to her speech.* That's a small sentence and it's a whole bereavement. She retired her best line. She killed her own best line for him. I'm actually angry about it in a way I haven't been angry at this book yet, and it's the good kind of angry — the kind where I'm angry at *her*, at the choice, not at the author for engineering it. Nobody manufactured this from an unsent text. This is two people who adore each other and one of them cannot speak except in objects and the other one has decided silence is a form of loyalty.

What it did to my suspicion: it sharpened. Because I know what he was doing with the word *love*. He's keeping it in the bottom drawer the way he keeps everything. He said *not yet* to Sheri on a bike. He's got a plan with a woman in a silk robe who said *God, I want her* into the back of his couch while he was still inside her, and he said *I am.* And whatever his reason is for not saying it — timing, honesty, the Daphne-shaped hole, the fact that the third game isn't lit yet — Vee is paying for it in a laundry room and he will never know she paid. That gap between what the reader knows and what she knows is the engine of this book now and chapter 43 loaded it and this chapter fired it.

Only gripe, and it's real: the prose got a little hand-on-my-shoulder in the middle. *She felt it as an adjustment somewhere behind her breastbone, a small wait, the kind her body produced before her mind had caught up* — I had it. Then we got *what?* and *what did he—* spelled out, and then the laundry room explaining the not-same-shape, and then a paragraph telling me she put it on a pile and wouldn't look at the pile. This book normally trusts me. The porch beat alone — the two breaths, the hand, the long kiss — would have done it, and I'd have carried the ache myself. It told me twice what I'd already felt once. That's the closest it's come all book to explaining its own subtext to me, and I'm docking it a point for that and not more, because the material underneath is so good.

No heat in it and I don't care. Zero sex and I'd call it one of the four or five best chapters in the book.
