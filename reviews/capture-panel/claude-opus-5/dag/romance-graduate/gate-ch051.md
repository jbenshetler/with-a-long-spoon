REACTION

Fifty chapters. *Fifty.* And she finally opens the door and lets me in and the room is freezing.

Okay. First: I need to say that the cut itself is savage and correct. I left Vee on a porch in the dark with her face wet and the door shut behind her, and instead of giving me the next hour of that, this book takes me to Switzerland. Any other novel and I'd be furious. Here I actually said *oh, finally* out loud, because I'd been carrying that debt so long it had interest on it, and because the two chapters turn out to be the same chapter.

Because look what she did. Chapter 48: Vee takes off the shirt and lies bare in a cold dorm room because she worked out that the warmth in it was her own heat given back and there was no one in there with her. Chapter 50: Randi lies against a beautiful sleeping stranger and is warm along one seam and cold everywhere else, *and the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers. She had brought it in with her and she would carry it back out.* Two women, two beds, two nights apart, both discovering the warm thing was theirs on loan. I sat back from the page. I have read three hundred books that would have had a character say that out loud to a friend over coffee so I didn't miss it, and this one just put them next to each other and trusted me. That's the whole reason I'm still here at fifty chapters.

Now. **The bracelet.** Plain white gold, locked on her wrist, she keeps it out of the window light while her mother's tennis bracelet plays in it, and it catches on the heel of her hand when John turns her out at the end of his arm, and "the white-gold bracelet that never came off" goes on with the diamonds like it's jewelry and it is *not jewelry.* It is locked. It does not come off. She dresses for a white-tie ball and the one piece she can't choose is already on her. Nobody named it, nobody explained it, and I have been begging this book to not explain itself and it went and did that. Who put it on her. How long has it been there. Does Vee's hand know that metal? Does Pace? Is *this* the third game — is she somebody's already, all this time, while she sets tables for other people? I have a cold spot in my stomach and I would like it to stay there.

**The breakfast.** "Returned the smile warmly and hugged him, eating her revulsion." And then she makes up boys for her father — "the invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table." Oh. *Oh.* So the fake bad dates aren't instruments she invented for Vee. They're her first language. She learned to fabricate a romantic life to hand across a table before she could drive, and now she does it to the one person she'd never lie to about anything except the only thing. And I'd just come off chapter 43 — I know what's in MIRA, I watched her read it, I heard *cheating bastards* come out ragged and aimed too close — and here she is eating eggs across from the man and talking about his business and hugging him. The revulsion has a date on it now. She knew before the library. She has known.

**John.** I was braced for a hot interlude and instead got the most elegantly cruel piece of structure in the book. He's not a worse man. That's the trick. He's *better* at every measurable thing — better skier, better dancer, cotillion-trained, flies in a chef, conjures a sleigh out of a side street. And the comparison she runs on the dance floor is the one that gutted me: Pace's lead is the lightest pressure, a thing she had to be listening for and good enough to catch, and it left the rest of the way to her. John's hand tells her body where to go and there's never a half-beat where she has to guess. *"She thought she was done with the work. Easier was better."* And then easier turns out to be a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon. In a book where food is the entire moral system — where Vee learned to taste and said *I had been eating crackers my whole life* — Randi gets smoke in a bubble and foams she can't place and keeps exactly one plate. Everyone in this novel is starving, and she's the one who's eaten the best meal in it and come away hungry.

And God, the lift queue. Four minutes with a stranger and she's got a husband, three children and their names and the ski camp they're at. That's not charm. That's the reflex. She builds plausible lives on demand, instantly, out of nothing, for an audience — and then she stands in a hotel room and can't run *one* scenario to the end, and it's Vee's face.

That part is the best writing about wanting someone I've read in a long time. She can model everything: Vee wouldn't cut her loose, Vee's too kind, Vee would be gentle, would try, would find something soft to say — *and they would not be this again, and the talk that ran hot and easy would learn to watch itself.* The arithmetic is airtight and it's also entirely cowardice, and she *names* it: "She was not brave enough to lose her. She had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be." And then the decision, which is the thing I'm going to be sick about for a week: *don't tell her in a way that makes her decide what to do with it. Let her arrive at it on her own. Keep the brunches exactly as they are. Vee hears it one way; Randi means it two.* That's it. That's the machine, confessed from the inside, and it isn't a scheme — it's a woman too scared to be refused, which is so much worse, because I can't hate it. I've done smaller versions of it. Everybody has.

*"Vee was her gorgeous."* Four words and I put the book down.

And then the texts. Vee from Ohio in a kitchen full of parents who love her out loud: *i'm here if you want to talk. or not.* The *or not.* Vee leaving the door open with no weight on it, two days after getting into her car crying because a man wouldn't say a word. And Randi sends back the fairytale, and the line "Every word of it true. None of it the thing," and then the one line she means, and turns the phone facedown. Both of these women spent this chapter lying in the dark next to the wrong amount of somebody, and each of them thinks the other one has it good.

Things I'll admit: the opening breakfast paragraph is written in a flat, compressed, almost-synopsis register that doesn't match this book — "Finishing, Randi stood, exchanging affections with her parents before excusing herself to go skiing" is the author's notes, not prose, and for about thirty seconds I thought I'd been handed a bad chapter. I've half-decided it's on purpose, that the table gets no interiority because she gives it none, that the whole scene is being performed and so it reads like a performance. Half-decided. And the ball is a touch travel-magazine — star chef, truffle, silver from the ceiling, horses — but she earns it by making the glitter the point and then letting it be worth nothing.

The sex is behind a door and I'm not even mad, which almost never happens with me. "He drew her in, and she went." Normally that's me gone. Here the blank *is* the content — the act doesn't get rendered because it didn't register, and what does get rendered is the aftermath: the print of her mouth on his chest and the ring of lipstick lower down, and *she had felt her own mouth do it from a long way off.* That's the most explicit thing in the chapter and it's a woman watching herself from the ceiling. "Nothing had happened to her eyes" — come on. That's a knife.

What I want now, badly: Vee is still in that car. Nobody has said the word. Randi is wearing a lock. And the one person in this book who keeps saying the true thing straight out is Cassie, who is going to walk into all of this blind, holding a laptop with her laid-off father's county on it.

Do not make me wait another ten chapters for the next one of these.
