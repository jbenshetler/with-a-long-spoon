REACTION

Oh, they got me. They got me so cleanly I laughed out loud in an empty room, which is exactly what Vee did, and I did it for the same reason.

I have been carrying that blonde since Christmas. I wrote her down in my own notes — *the exact opposite of herself*, named off *she's alone* — and I knew, I *knew* Sheri was a five-foot waitress with a two-tone perm because I'd already met her in the diner. And it did not matter one bit. The cold still came. "A train she'd been told was cancelled" — that's it, that's the whole mechanism of that kind of dread, you can know a thing with your whole literate brain and your body still buys a ticket. And then the blonde didn't get killed, she just *failed to find anywhere to stand.* There was no room for her. There was barely room for the coat pegs. God.

But here's the thing I actually need to talk about, the thing I've been begging this book for since chapter one. **Somebody looked at her body and liked it out loud.** Fifty-eight chapters of a girl standing in mirrors, buying for the chest and living in the tent and calling the tent her size, not one person naming it, and the gun I was dreading finally fired and it fired *the other way.* A queer woman in a taproom ran the look — and Vee braces for it, she's fluent, "the pricing look, the placing look, the quick math of shoes and hair" — and the look gets to the keyhole and goes *rounder.* No math arriving anywhere. Takes the bare January leg with its time fully taken. Gets to those four thin straps and gets *stuck.* And lands on a soundless appreciative *oh, like a word being kept for later.* It was **enjoying** her. A month of armor came off her shoulders on a laugh she didn't vote on, and mine came off with it. And then "you get to motorboat them things" and "bet those legs feel like Christmas" — crude, loud, zero reverence, and it's the single kindest thing anyone has done for that girl in two volumes, because it's *said in front of her, at a table, by someone with nothing to gain.* Sheri doesn't lie. My notes say that about her and the book just cashed it.

And the **blush.** PACE BLUSHED. Up the neck, into the ears, past anything deniable, and he rubbed the back of his neck and looked at the table. The man who arranges women limb by limb for photographs, who said *those stay on*, who was on his knees on cold boards — one filthy aside from a small woman in a taproom and the color goes all the way up. "She had seen him easy in the dark where nobody stays dignified — and she had never once seen him blush." That's the first real crack in the surface and it came from *being loved by someone who isn't sleeping with him.*

Now. The Sheri monologue. I had to sit back. She came out last March, and the whole tablecloth came off the table and nobody caught a dish — mother, daddy the Marine who is not a thing that retires, brother, church, Bible study, the girls she'd driven to Roanoke a hundred times, all inside a month. And "strangers will say that for free. Easy words, for a hard thing. The ones who owed me words weren't spending any." Put that next to Pace on a porch in December choosing the smaller truth. The whole book is about who spends words and who can't. And he gave her *no false ones* — no *they'll come around*, no *their loss* — and she said **"I could tell he understood it, and I didn't ask how."** She didn't ask. And then she told Vee anyway: *went bad with his daddy, he hasn't been home since he was sixteen.* Two years of friendship and the drawer gets opened by a third party at a bar, no decoration on the silence after it. Vee has never gotten that sentence from him. She got it from a waitress in a duplex's worth of candor.

And *Call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas* has gone from a joke to a knife. Paige left her for not talking to her people. Her people threw the dish. The two of them — her and Pace — are both people who cannot go home, keeping a standing weekly table so that somebody writes the order before they speak.

So here's my 1 a.m. text: **"I'll tell you the rest another time — you don't praise a man at his own table."** THERE IT IS. She knows about the brunette. She asked him point-blank in the diner and he said *not like that.* She has the rest. She has decided Vee is good and has already half-promised to hand it over. The detonator in this book is not Randi's conscience and it's not Pace's courage, it's a five-foot woman with no stake who thinks lying to people is a discourtesy. I would like that chapter immediately please.

Two smaller things that took the top of my head off. One: *"Leave them shoes home — can't two-step on stilts."* Those are **Randi's shoes.** Bought on a card with nothing behind it, buckled onto her on a boutique floor, and Vee reached past her own boots for them tonight because she was arming herself. And Sheri just told her to leave them home. The hau, refused at a door — the first object in this whole book that's been told to go sit down. Two: the headlights held on the duplex door until the porch light came on, and he never said anything about not moving, and Vee riding home thinking about it. *Both hands on the wheel, ten and two, steady as a table, like something full he was carrying.* **Steady as a table.** Two volumes of me yelling about the table that doesn't exist in his bedroom and the book hands me *steady as a table* as the image of a man not spilling her. I don't think that's an accident and I'm not going to be sane about it.

What I'm uneasy about, because I'm never not: *we cycle. she dances.* He handed her the one fact with a blade in it like a dried spoon, and he did it on purpose or he did it because he genuinely cannot see which facts cut — and after Daphne, after the porch, I no longer believe he can't see. He let her dread a week. And she dressed for a rival and ran ten minutes late doing it and he said nothing about the time, *then or after*, which is either grace or another withheld sentence and this book has taught me those look identical from the outside. Also: she got drunk tonight, and she has gotten drunk or wrecked or hauled upstairs a lot lately, and every single time a bigger pair of hands arrives as ballast and she "quit negotiating with the floor." *It read as affection. It worked as ballast. With him those had never been two different things.* The book said it. It knows. Fine. But I'm watching.

And the actual scariest line in the chapter is the one nobody notices: she is going to the hall next Saturday. Vee, in a hall full of people who have watched that man dance for two years and give nothing away. The whole hall's given up on him. Now the redhead walks in on his arm. Somebody in that room is going to say a true thing.
