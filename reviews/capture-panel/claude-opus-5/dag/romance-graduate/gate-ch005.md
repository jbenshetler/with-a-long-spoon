REACTION

Okay so the thing that got me is that I knew. I've known since the blurb and especially since the pointing game, and this whole chapter I'm sitting in the coffee shop with them watching a girl have the best twenty minutes of her semester and it is *so* good and it is *entirely* a setup, and the cruelty of it is that it's also genuinely the best twenty minutes of her semester. He actually helps her. That's what wrecked me. He could have done the slick version — swoop in, two lines, look how smart, let me buy you a coffee — and instead he asks what her professor said, writes the ugly formula in the margin, tells her *don't*, puts the pencil back in her hand at the exact line where she can finish it herself. That's not a pickup. That's a man who understands that the thing she'll remember is the part she did. And she *will* use that for the rest of her life, which is the worst and best part — it's a real gift, handed over inside a trap, and the gift doesn't get less real for it.

The "may I call you" landed on me the way it landed on her. I've read nine hundred men say "give me your number" like it's a decree and I'm so tired of it, and this guy says *may I* and my whole chest went stupid. Genre, you've been doing it wrong.

But the thing I actually want to text somebody at 1 a.m. about is Randi's hands. Her hand going to the cup when he crosses the room. The cup coming to her mouth twice with no sip in it. Her giving him *one word* — "Randi" — no adjective bit, no parents-had-a-sense-of-humor, the whole performance stripped off because she can't run it right now. She built this. She picked the girl, she bragged about the bench on the walk home, she's the one who said *do you think she'll like the bench* — and twenty minutes in she's white-knuckling a latte while her boyfriend forgets there's anyone else in the room. And then she's the one who turns around and sells it to Vee afterward: *he gave up his place in line, he walked out without his coffee, a man doesn't forget his coffee.* She closes the deal on her own ache. That's a woman I want to watch for four hundred more pages.

And Cassie. Cassie is the only person in this book with 20/20 vision and the book keeps letting her say one flat true thing and then walk out of the room. "He looked at her the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt" — to Randi's face, at the table, out loud. And *Goodbye, Vee*, last chapter, the whole word. Cassie knows. Cassie is going to get sidelined and I am already pre-grieving.

Vee I'd follow anywhere at this point. She tried on Randi's little name-bit and it *worked*, and then she immediately grabbed her own name back — "Vee, people call me Vee" — and that tiny flinch away from the borrowed poise told me more about her than fifty pages of inner monologue would. She reads a room top to bottom in four seconds and files herself as "the most of what the room is selling and the one with the most fabric on" and doesn't look at either fact straight. That's an actual interior life. That's the thing I've been starving for.

Two complaints, and one's mild. The paragraph where she itemizes everything he did right — the tutor's path versus the mathematician's path, *not showing off, not hiding it either* — the book already showed me all of that in the scene, beautifully, and then it turned around and annotated itself. I don't need the footnote. I had it. And "the thought did not finish" three times in one chapter went from a nice effect to a tic I started waiting for. Also: Euler's at right angles, two things that won't give alone turning into one plain thing together, in a chapter called "Substitution," after a lecture that ended on *latent variables*. I'm not mad. It's a wink, and the wink is earned by the fact that the math is actually correct and actually taught. But if the next chapter puts a thematic label on the whiteboard too I'm going to start rolling my eyes.

Heat: basically none, and I didn't mind. Her eye catching on his forearms a half-second past where she'd have chosen, the pencil going from his hand to hers — that's it, and it was enough to keep the room warm. I know what this book does when it decides to; it earned a dry chapter.

What I'm suspicious of: how real this is on his side. He's been "warm, unguarded" with Randi while running a game on her, and now he's being genuinely, specifically good to Vee while running a game on her too, and the blurb promised me it gets real for everybody. Also — Daphne. One sentence, chapter one, a door he didn't respect, cost him more than he knew anything could. I want that back.
