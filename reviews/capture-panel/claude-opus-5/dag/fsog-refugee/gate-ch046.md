She cropped the leg out.

That's what I'm going to be sitting with. The long second step, the slit lying shut and then opening, the murmur going through a dull room because she decided it would — the one moment of her life she won standing up — and she trimmed it off at the waist before she mailed it to Ohio. And her mother printed the half that was left and carried it in her purse and showed it to people who'd already seen it, and *still* touched her own collarbone while she looked. There's no version of that picture small enough. Her mother would find something to put a hand on her own throat about if Vee sent a passport photo.

And then the dad paragraphs, which I did not expect to be the most frightening thing in this chapter. *Too good is its own kind of suspicious.* A man in a kitchen with burned rolls, who knows nothing, who has never met anybody in this book, is the only person in forty-six chapters to say the true sentence out loud. He's pricing a dress and thinking *what's the catch,* and he's wrong about why and right that there is one, and I had to put the book down on my knee for a second. He thinks the catch is a man too old or too rich or too smooth. The catch is that two people picked her off a lawn in September and one of them is at brunch every Saturday. Dad, your instinct is perfect and your aim is forty-five degrees off.

But the thing that actually got me is *I'd tell him the sex before I'd tell him the sewing.* That's such a specific, true, slightly funny piece of self-knowledge — weird is worse than wrong, because wrong he can reject and weird has nowhere to go — and then she lands it with *I'm not ashamed of it. The opposite, if anything,* and I believed her completely. She's proud of the man on the sun porch with the machine. She's proud of being someone a man built a dress for. She just has no container to hand anyone that the pride fits inside, so she hands out the hiking and the boom box and the doctorate, the parts that fit, and keeps the rest.

And then Cassie. *So what is he. Not to your dad. To you.* Careful, exact, not prying — and Vee has *nothing.* Not the word, not the story, not a version of herself that stands up cleanly in the telling. That sentence is the whole book. And then Cassie does the thing nobody with an agenda does, which is ask the second question instead of filling the silence: **is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to?** And Vee says *both.* Out loud. To another person.

I've been waiting forty chapters for somebody to ask Vee the big one and I kept picturing it as the arranging, the floor, *did you know they chose you.* It isn't going to come that way. It came as *can't or won't,* and she answered honestly for the first time, and the honest answer was *I am choosing this.* That's the editing turning visible to her, even if only for one second in a dining hall over steel on hard plastic.

And then: *Cassie nodded and went back to her plate. She didn't push at it. Vee was grateful. She was also, a little, not.*

That's the crack. That's the first time Vee has wanted to be made to say it. She's been building a second life out of things she decided not to say and calling it sovereignty, and just now, for one beat, some part of her stood at the door and hoped Cassie would kick it. And Cassie — who is the only decent person in this entire novel and who has figured out more from a tomato burn than Vee has volunteered in a term — respected her friend's privacy, which is the single kindest thing she could do and the exact wrong thing, and she'll never know. Protect her. Also I want to shake her. *Push, Cassie. She's begging you with the back of her neck.*

Coming off the couch chapter, this one is almost unbearable to read sideways. I know what Randi does with her Saturdays now. I know what Randi does with the goodbye kiss afterward, braced on the top rail of a couch with her face in a mirror she forgot about. So watching Vee sort the world into who gets how much of her — parents get the waist up, Randi got every inch of it and the toes, Pace got the photo without knowing what else was in the frame, Cassie gets *the parts that fit* — and seeing that she's given the most to the one person who's farming her and the least to the one who'd catch her, that's a horror arranged like a dinner. The jacket said *seated at the table since the first course.* Yeah. I'm starting to resent my seat a little, and that's a compliment.

Nothing in it for the body and I didn't want anything. Two sentences of Cassie's own life at the top — the baby, the dork husband, *they don't quite see me as an adult yet* — doing double duty, because that's exactly what Vee's parents did with the printout and exactly what Randi does to Vee with a brighter smile.
