She didn't carry the bag in. She didn't carry the bag in and he probably never even clocked it, and I clocked it on the first line, because I have been watching that bag since chapter whatever — the girl who could pack a dress for him but not a toothbrush, who finally got a top drawer and a cardboard form of green scrunchies in it and called it *her space* and smiled about it crossing the dark grass. And tonight it stays in the Corolla. She stops on the mat. She is level with the chair where the bag goes on the nights she stays and she stands there and does not advance one inch into the warm he put up for her hours ago. That's the whole chapter before anybody speaks. God, that's good.

And the heat and the wine already poured and the piano going and the chicken low on the stove — the entire grammar he's been writing in for fifty chapters, every wordless I-love-you, deployed on schedule — and this time it lands on her like a bribe. *It all went straight past her guard into the old wanting and made it worse.* That's the first time this book has let his accumulating pile read as what it also is: an argument that never has to be made out loud. He has built a house that says it so he never has to. And she's finally standing in it with her coat buttoned to the throat refusing to be answered by furniture.

Then he does the thing I will not forgive him for tonight, and it's not the silence. It's *"Do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have than this one?"* He's a mathematician and he reached for a comparison class. He changed the question. He took *who am I to you* and turned it into *isn't this better than the alternatives*, and the cruelest part is it's **true** — she goes down the line of them and there isn't one she'd trade into — and she has to stand there holding a true thing that is not the thing. *She hated that he'd made the true thing pull against the point.* I said out loud, in my kitchen, at eleven at night: that's a dodge, Peter. That is a *dodge*, and you're too precise a man not to know it.

And then *"Words are cheap. Believe what I do."* — and she hands it back: **"Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me."** That is the best line she has ever had and she found it under fire, crying, in a doorway, and it's unanswerable, and so he doesn't answer it. *His arms came up and folded, he held there, the still of a man holding a line at cost.* At cost. The book told me it cost him. That's the tell and I'm so glad it's there, because that is not a man who doesn't love her and it's not a man who's shy — that's a man who knows what's behind the one sentence she's asking for, who said *not yet* out loud on a cold road to a cyclist before he'd said one word of it to her, and who has just discovered that *not yet* has a clock on it and the clock is her standing on his mat not taking her coat off. He can't say *girlfriend* because the floor was poured without her. He can't say *I love you* because it would drag the rest up behind it. Every ounce of the care I've spent forty-nine chapters loving him for is now also the reason he cannot speak, and Helen Rivers knew that from chapter one, and I'm sitting here genuinely slightly sick about it.

And *Not the hand in place of the word.* Six words. That's the whole series turned over and shown from the other side. His hands have been the entire language — the drawer, the lamp click, the scarf laid back with two fingers, the juice held to her mouth before anything else, the flannel pulled on over the shirt he already had on — and tonight she looks at the hand coming toward her, the hand that settles everything, and knows it's being used as a substitute, and *goes out into the cold.* He said her name once. Once. *And her name was not the word she'd come for.*

What's killing me: he asked *"Do you want to keep going?"* about a pair of underwear. He said *"Slow. For me."* He said *"This?"* and then gave her nothing she hadn't already asked for. That man is the best consent I've read in this genre, and tonight the one question she asks him he will not answer, and the asymmetry is finally sitting in the middle of the floor with the lamps on. He gets to ask. She asks once and gets a rhetorical question and folded arms.

Also — she walked out. *She* did. First volume ended with the door closing on him, and I bought the next one before I slept, and I'd buy this one too. And I notice she's walking out into exactly the place where there's a woman in a dark green booth who says *you poor thing* like a bath and asks for nothing and will absolutely be available on Saturday with her hand out. The debt's about to come due as comfort. I can see it coming down the road like headlights.
