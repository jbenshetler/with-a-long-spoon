REACTION

I left Vee crying in a driveway with the door open behind her and the heat of that house pouring out into the cold, and the book turned the page and said *Stone columns and a rough ceiling beam framed a wall of beveled glass.* I almost put it down right there out of sheer spite. I don't want Switzerland. I want his face. I want to know if he came out onto the porch.

And then about four pages in I forgot I was angry, because this is the chapter I've been asking for since the practice room and I didn't know it would come like this.

She was eating her revulsion over steak and eggs. That's the first line of hers I've ever had from the inside and it's that. And then she *made up boys for him* — "the invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table" — and I actually put my hand over my mouth, because I have been sitting here for ten chapters calling Randi's three invented men a *lever.* Switzerland. The mouse. The Redford boy. I wrote in my own notes that each one showed up exactly when she needed something put in Vee's head. And it turns out the invented man is not a tool she picked up for Vee. It's the thing she built when she was a girl at her mother's table to keep anybody from finding the real one. She's been handing Vee her own armor and calling it gossip. That reorganized her for me the way the swimming reorganized Vee.

The bracelet. Plain white gold, **locked on her wrist**, and she kept it out of the light next to her mother's tennis bracelet, and it caught on the heel of her hand when John turned her out to the end of his arm, and *the white-gold bracelet that never came off.* She told me three times. I'm going to be useless about this all week. That is a collar, and somebody has the key, and it is not that beautiful dead lawyer asleep in the faultless room. The jacket said one more door raised and not yet opened and I've been reading that as Vee's third door — it's hers. The third game isn't a game she's running. It's one she's *in.*

And then — Pace. *Pace made her say it, out loud, every time.* Every time. Plural. Recurring. So that's confirmed and it's doing two completely opposite things to me at once. One: his thing is not a performance he built for Vee. The *"Don't," she said. The hand left her* man is just who he is, all the way down, with everybody, and that makes me love him more, not less. Two: Vee doesn't know she's standing in a practiced grace. And this is where the chapter got genuinely cruel to me — Randi, glowing, gliding on a marble floor, deciding *easier was better*, deciding she was done with the work. Pace's lead was the lightest pressure, a thing she had to be listening for and good enough to catch, and it left the rest of the way to her. That is the entire thesis of this series described by a woman choosing, that night, to be relieved of it. And she gets exactly what she asked for, and it's a sandwich.

The cold. She's warm only along the seam where she touches him and she says it out loud: the cold didn't belong to the room, she brought it in and she'd carry it back out. Two chapters ago Vee was lying in a cold dorm bed holding a shirt and realizing the warmth in it was her own, given back, that he wasn't in it. **Same image.** Rivers put the same shape in two women on either side of the same man and I didn't see the rhyme coming and when it landed I made a noise. And the lipstick — the color of her mouth gone from her own face and left in a ring on him. The eyeliner held. *Nothing had happened to her eyes.* Devastating. She spent her body on him and the only part of her that was ever actually hers stayed exactly where she'd painted it.

But the thing I'll be chewing on at one in the morning is the decision. She can run any scenario to the end and she cannot run this one, because she can't picture Vee's face while she says it. And so: *So she would not tell her, not in a way that would make Vee decide what to do with it. Let Vee arrive at it on her own.* There it is in her own voice. Not a plot. Cowardice, named as cowardice, by a woman who admits she arranged her entire life so she'd never have to be brave. And I have to be fair to this book: it isn't winking. It doesn't ask me to find that charming. It shows me the loving is real — *she had never once before said it to anyone without meaning it as currency, and with Vee there was neither* — and then shows me the loving is precisely what's funding the dishonesty, and lets me sit in both. That's the hardest trick in the genre and she just did it in a hotel room with a sleeping man in it.

And then she lies back down in the warmth she can borrow and cannot make, and sends *you ANGEL, it's honestly a fairytale out here,* every word of it true and none of it the thing. She's doing Pace's move. Keep the enormous thing in the dark and don't sign it. These two chose each other out of a crowd for a reason and it's not taste.

What's not great: the first paragraph reads like someone's outline got in — *exchanging affections with her parents before excusing herself to go skiing* — flat where everything else in this book is felt, and for a page I thought I was being handed a different, worse author. And the middle is a checklist: the meet-cute, the hütte, the champagne, the white tie, the star chef, the foam, the horse sleigh. I was skimming the tinsel. Then the last third turned around and told me the glossiness was the whole point — *this was the best the acceptable world could do for her* — and I forgave the montage, mostly, because it had to be that generic to be that empty.

What I actually need now is Vee. It's been two chapters since those two were alone in a room and the last thing in that room was his hand coming up toward her and her getting the door open, and I have been carrying that the whole time I was up on this mountain. *Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me* is still sitting in my chest. Give her back to me.
