REACTION

I opened this and the first thing I had to do was swallow my own irritation, because I left a girl crying on a doormat with her coat buttoned to the throat and her bag in the car, and chapter 51 hands me *stone columns* and *beveled glass* and a man named John on a ski lift. Around the time she was standing in front of the mirror in the pale blue dress narrating *the year I met your father, the ball, the snow* I genuinely thought: Helen, what are you doing, have you lost it, is this a bonus chapter, is this a Hallmark movie. And then the whole thing turned over in my hands and I understood that the fairytale was the experiment and Randi was the control group and the book had been building the apparatus since the first paragraph.

Because what this chapter does — coldly, patiently, over twelve hours of champagne and white truffle and a sleigh with bells on it — is prove the argument I have been having with this entire genre for about six years. John is *flawless*. He's tall, he's funny, he skis better than she does and spends none of it proving it, he conjures tables and sleighs, he puts both hands over his heart at the bottom of a staircase, he quotes Byron and can't finish it. He leads a waltz so well her body doesn't have to guess. And the book lets her prefer that — lets her think *easier was better*, lets her think *I'm done with the work* — and then lets her say the thing out loud at the door: **"Pace had never assumed anything in his life. Pace made her say it, out loud, every time. She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked."** My stomach dropped. And then the book spends the back half of the chapter making her pay for it in the only currency that matters: *it had meant no more than a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon.* **"She had put nothing on the table and had won exactly that."** That is the cleanest statement of why the asking isn't an obstacle to the heat — the asking *is* the heat — that I have ever read in a book like this, and it got made by a woman lying awake next to a man who did everything right.

**"Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best. She wished it wasn't true."** I put the book down on my chest for a second. Fifty chapters of me trying to decide whether that man's attention is a gift or a mechanism, and here's the verdict delivered from the one perspective that has nothing to gain by flattering him.

And the *bracelet.* Plain white gold, **locked** on her wrist, kept out of the light at her mother's table, catching on the heel of her hand when John turns her out to the end of his arm, *the white-gold bracelet that never comes off.* She wore it through the whole fairytale. She wore it to bed with another man. That's a collar, and nobody in the chapter says the word, and I've apparently had it in front of me without knowing what it was. That single detail rearranged everything about the couch and *say something nice to me.*

The parents. *Eating her revulsion* while a hearty man hugs his adored daughter — that word, revulsion, over steak and eggs, with MIRANDA sitting invisible under the chalet and the tennis bracelet playing with the window light. And her mother *all edges and points against her.* And then the fiction: **"She made them up for him, the boys who chased her and the one or two she'd let catch her a little. The invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table."** That's the Redford boy. That's Switzerland, that's the mouse, that's every man Randi has ever produced over eggs Benedict exactly one week before she needed him. She doesn't invent men to manipulate Vee — I mean, she does, it works, it's a lever — but she learned to do it at nine years old to survive her father's hand on her shoulder, and she brought the same trick to the only table she loves. I didn't expect to be handed the origin of the lever and I don't know what to do with how sad it made me.

And the cold. *"She was warm only where she touched him... the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers. She had brought it in with her and she would carry it back out."* That's Vee in the top bunk. **It was warm because she was warm.** Same discovery, same night-cold, two women three weeks apart, and look at the difference: Vee took the shirt off and lay there bare in the real cold and *did not put it back on*, left it in a heap where a dropped thing lands. Randi fixed her hair, checked that the smile still worked, **and got back into the borrowed warmth and stayed.** The twenty-one-year-old is the brave one. The book knows it and says so — *she had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be* — and I wasn't ready for Randi to be the smaller of the two.

The mirror got me too. Mouth bare because she left the color of it in a ring on him, the rest of the face thinned and smeared, *the eyeliner had held, of course it had, nothing had happened to her eyes* — and then she puts the face back on, alone, in the dark, for nobody. Vee stood at a mirror and built a version of herself to be kept and a man said **no, not like that, you'll have to wait,** and kissed her at every place he set her. Nobody has ever said *not like that* to Randi. She just reassembles and the color is always good.

But the part I will be carrying around all week is this, and it's the whole book: **"So she would not tell her, not in a way that would make Vee decide what to do with it."** There it is. In her own voice, in a hotel room, out of *love* and cowardice and nothing else. The reason Vee doesn't get the truth is not strategy, it's that Randi cannot run the scenario of Vee's face to the end and she is not brave enough to lose her. *Vee heard it one way; Randi meant it two ways. Let the thing keep the shape it was already taking. Let Vee arrive at it on her own.* That is a woman choosing, deliberately, to keep another woman from being able to consent to the thing she's already inside — and the book doesn't wink once. It lays it down as the saddest thing in the chapter, which it is, and it's also *exactly* the move I left this whole shelf over, just performed with a tenderness that makes it harder to be angry and worse to look at. **Vee was her gorgeous.** God.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *she wore his locked bracelet to bed with another man and then decided not to tell the girl she's in love with because she wouldn't survive her face. also the perfect man was the point. also VEE TEXTED HER FROM HER PARENTS' KITCHEN "i'm here if you want to talk. or not." AND GOT A FAIRYTALE BACK. "every word of it true. none of it the thing."* That text — the gentlest, most open-handed nothing-asked-for message in the book, from a girl who had just been refused a single word in a doorway and still had warmth to spare for somebody else's night — arriving on that nightstand, in that room. I made a sound.

And now the terrible arithmetic: the girl is in Ohio having been turned away with nothing, and the woman who will be across a brunch table from her in a week has just decided, in writing, to let her arrive at it on her own. The bill is coming and it's coming as kindness. I'm so scared of January.
