REACTION

Oh, the blanket.

Four lines into the chapter, before I even understood he was hungover, there it is: *Randi's cashmere blanket, the one for the chill that took her after,* slid off the foot of his bed in the night — and he folds it and puts it back **where it lives.** Where it *lives*. Not where he left it, not in a drawer. That blanket has an address in his bedroom. And *the chill that took her after* — after. The man has a standing accommodation for the way another woman's body cools when she's done. I have known since the mountain that these two chose Vee together; I did not have it as a thing with a texture and a place on the bed until this morning, said in half a sentence by a man too wrecked to notice he was telling me, and then he goes and makes it tidy. Last chapter Sheri said *she find out about the brunette, then?* and he said *no, not like that,* and I let it ride as the arrangement, past tense, the old deal. It is not past tense. He told Sheri *every weekend, I'm there, when I've got her there's nothing else in the room,* and the weeknights were Vee's own request, and I am now doing arithmetic on those weeknights that I do not enjoy doing.

And then — God help me — the rest of the chapter is the single thing I have wanted for two volumes and I got it and it hurt.

He drank. *That* man. Measured-coffee, vacuum-sealed-portions, fork-squared-to-the-table-edge Pace drank until he was sick and then mopped it himself with bleach at some hour, and left the bucket in the hall with the foam dried above the waterline, and the one thing in the house that didn't get put right is the bucket, because the blanket got put right first. *Whiskey River was a liar.* It took his balance, his dinner, hours he couldn't use, *and it left every memory of Vee intact.* That's the whole chapter in a line and he's the one who says it.

The truck. I actually made a sound. His head coming up before he knew he'd moved, the fork stopping, *his body gathering itself toward the narrow cut in the hill* — his body, not his mind, his body has the shape of her arrival in it — and for one second the house gives her back to him, the car finding the drive and *Vee finding him on the covered stoop.* He misses her *arriving.* He misses the half-second when she looks up from the turn and locates him. That's the most specifically in-love thing anybody has said in this book and he says it to nobody, in a cold kitchen, over prison food. And then the engine doesn't slow, and he bends over the table and sobs, and sobs *for a few breaths after he can't hear it anymore.* He has been still for fifty-two chapters. Stillness has been his entire grammar. This is the first time his body has broken ranks and nobody's there to see it, which is the only condition under which it would.

*He was not hungry. He ate anyway.* The man whose love is food, eating because a body requires fuel. Gutting. And earlier: the woman years ago who looked into that bowl and called it prison food, laughing, and he laughed with her, and *they stopped seeing each other with no cruelty on either side and no part of the world altered by it* — that's him describing his own method as a crime scene without knowing it.

Daphne. So the floor behind the sentence is a girl who taught him where to stand. Older, knew how things worked, showed him which dining hall made the plan stretch and where to put himself when everyone else was born knowing — which tells me a great deal about what he came from, and still nothing about the scar or *I don't go back,* so there's more floor down there. He said it to her, once, meant it, and she left, and *there had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone, because Daphne was the person he would have told.* That's the loneliest sentence in the volume. And then the superstition: *he had taught himself that the words were the difference.* Say it, and losing her takes your life; don't say it, and loss stays survivable. Which is magical thinking from a man who does mathematics for a living, and he almost knows it — *he had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him* — and I notice he knows it and still didn't reach for the phone. The chapter ends with him eating, not dialing. Honest. Maddening. Correct.

And *he had chosen the smaller truth.* He watched *I love how good we are together* reach her and **fail**, and he saw it fail, in the moment, and let it stand *because it was safe, and because he was afraid, and because at the moment she asked him to be brave he had been weak.* He names it weakness in his own head. That buys him an enormous amount from me — and it does not buy him anything from her, because she's in Ohio under a shelf of little gold swimmers inventing a blonde at his stove, and she has already retired the cracker sentence, and he has no idea he's on a clock.

The one place I slackened: the Daphne stretch reads like a file being opened rather than a memory being had. Short flat declaratives, *she had been his tutor, then his best friend, his first girlfriend, his first lover, his first love* — a résumé of a wound. This book normally hands me a pan's talk changing or a kiss at the knee; here it hands me a summary, and I felt the seam. But it bought the truck with it, so.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: HE FOLDED HER BLANKET AND PUT IT BACK WHERE IT LIVES. Also he cried at a truck that didn't turn in. Also I'm furious that both of those are true about the same man in the same ninety seconds.
