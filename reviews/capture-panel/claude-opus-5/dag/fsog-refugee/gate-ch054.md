REACTION

Oh. *Oh.*

He mopped up his own vomit with bleach before the sun came up, and the first thing he does when he wakes is fold Randi's blanket and put it back where it lives. That's the opening beat and I had to read it twice. *Where it lives.* The girl is gone, hasn't had a word out of him in two weeks, and the other woman's cashmere has a permanent address in that bedroom. The book didn't say one syllable about it. It just set it on the floor, had him fold it, and moved on, and I sat there with my thumb on the page going — you know what you did, Helen Rivers.

And then the chapter went and broke me anyway.

Because here's what I've been waiting fifty-four chapters for and didn't recognize when it arrived: he eats crackers. The man who built his whole self around setting a plate down at the exact moment the hunger peaks — he takes two vacuum-sealed bags out of the freezer, boils them, cuts them open over a bowl, and eats food that "held everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted," at the little table, alone, black coffee, hands shaking. *Prison food.* A woman years ago laughed at it and he laughed with her and the world was not altered by it. That's his whole life when there's nobody in the room to feed. He fed her a meal and he has been eating crackers the entire time. I don't think she'll ever know that and I'm not sure I forgive the book for showing me and not her.

The truck. God, the truck. His head coming up before he knew he'd moved, the fork stopping, his *body gathering itself toward the cut in the hill* — and for one second the house gives her back to him, the car finding the narrow drive and her looking up from the turn and finding him on the stoop with the smile she gives only him. And then the engine doesn't slow. And he sets the fork down *before he drops it*, because even wrecked he's precise, and then he bends over the table and sobs until he can't hear the truck anymore and for a few breaths after. That's the man who has never once let a cost show. Sheri said it last chapter — *everyone wants something off him and he pays it out without letting the cost show* — and this is the cost, in an empty house, with nobody to perform it for. The sobbing is the realest thing he's ever done and it's the only thing he's ever done that she wasn't allowed to see.

And *I missed watching her arrive.* Not the sex. Not her body — though it's in there, and it's the gentlest version of it he's ever had, her walking barefoot to the bathroom with her backside easy and unguarded, *no performance in it*, which is a man loving a woman for being unobserved in his house. Her face emptied of every question in the middle of his bed. The first bite always telling him. He misses *the moment she looked up from the turn.* I'm done. That's it. I'm his again, which is infuriating, because I spent my whole last stretch of notes writing down the ways he's rigged the floor she's standing on.

Now. Daphne.

Here's where I'm complicated. He gave me a reason, finally, and it's a real one and it's *clean*: he said it once, to the one person who taught him where to stand in the world, and she left, and losing her ate his life; after her he kept the words and the losses stayed survivable; so he taught himself **the words were the difference.** It's superstition. It's a man who does arithmetic for a living running a correlation on a sample size of one and building a religion on it. *He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him.* That's the plainest sentence this book has ever given me from inside his head and it's devastating and it's also — not the whole bill, and I know it isn't, and the chapter knows I know, because the one thing that never enters his head during all this honest self-accounting is **Randi.** Not as a reason. Not as a complication. Not at all. She's a blanket he folds. Fifty-four chapters and I've got the man's confession and the floor of it is still poured on a lawn in September with Vee's name not on it.

So: *words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me* now has an answer, and the answer is "because the last time I spent them, the person I spent them on disappeared and there was nobody left to tell that she was gone." Which is unanswerable in a completely different direction than her line was. And I notice what the Daphne section didn't say: *why* she left. Nothing. Not one detail. Older, knew how things worked, called his tutor, and then — "Then she left." The book has *I don't go back* and *my mother stays home* and the scar and now a woman who vanished out of a sentence with no door in it, and I don't think those are four things. I think they're one thing with four edges showing.

The only place I got restless was the Daphne paragraphs themselves — she arrives as a flat little biography, tutor-then-friend-then-girlfriend-then-lover-then-love, laid out like a CV, after fifty chapters of this book never once telling me anything it could make me feel instead. I understand why; he thinks in order. It still read like a file being opened rather than a wound being touched, and for half a page I was reading *about* him for the first time instead of being in there with him.

But then: *Whiskey River was a liar.* The music is always already going in that house and last night it was Willie and he went to it the way she goes to the black line in the pool, and it did the same thing hers does — took the hours, took the dinner, took the balance, and **left every memory of Vee intact.** He has her four machines and he found out they don't work either. He and that girl are the same animal and neither of them has any idea.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *he threw up, he mopped it himself with bleach, he folded the other woman's blanket, he cried at a truck that didn't turn in, and then he ate the chicken anyway. HE ATE IT ANYWAY. I'm not okay. Also he has loved exactly one woman before and she walked out of a sentence without a reason and I need to know where that woman is.*

And underneath all of it, the thing that actually scares me: he now knows exactly what it would cost to say it and exactly what it costs not to, and Sheri put it to him flat — *then not saying it matters more to you than having her, and from this seat that feels pretty shitty* — and he looked down and didn't argue. So he's got the arithmetic. He's got the grief. He's got nothing to lose that he hasn't already lost. Term starts, and Vee has one thing circled on the calendar and it is **Randi**, a class twice a week at seven with no Cassie in it, and this man is going to spend another week deciding things can keep. *He had long practice at deciding a thing could keep.* That's the sentence that's going to cost him everything and he put it in the first paragraph like it was nothing.

Get in the truck, Pace. Go look at her before she's ready to be looked at. Just this once.
