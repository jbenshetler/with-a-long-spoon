REACTION

Oh. Oh, they gave me the fireside chapter and then put a knife in it.

First: I got what I asked for. Two of them in a room, a quilt, cold tea, nobody debriefing it afterward to a third party over a mimosa. Six chapters of everything Vee lives getting laundered through somebody else's lunch table and then finally just — them, talking, his hand on her breast under the quilt the whole time not as a move but the way you'd rest your hand on a dog's back. The robe untied and him looking and his face going soft and neither of them doing anything about it. That's the register I came to this book for and it's been a while.

But Daphne. I have been circling that name in my notes since whenever he first didn't say it, and I was braced for a dead girl or a brutal ex and instead I got: he was fifteen, she was nineteen, the university *assigned* her to him, it lasted two years, and he has filed it under the happiest thing that ever happened to him. "It was the best two years I'd had." And Vee does the arithmetic she doesn't want to do and the clean ugly grown-up word starts to form and she *holds it down under her tongue because it isn't hers to say.* I put the book down at that line. I've read a hundred books that would have played that as tragic backstory flavoring, or worse, as the thing that made him so good in bed, wink. This one lets him defend it in that too-careful voice with every reason slotted neatly into place — "which was only sensible," "it would have been stranger if she'd stayed" — and lets Vee hear the carefulness *as* the tell, and lets him not hear it at all. The book knows. He doesn't. And it doesn't make her fix him.

And then the thing that came up my spine cold: that's Randi. An older woman who decided about a younger person, whose tenderness was completely real, who was the whole soft middle of someone's world, and the one inside it experienced it as the best luck of his life. Pace learned his entire religion of asking from a woman who never had to ask him for anything. He is currently doing to Vee — with Randi, as a game that got real — the structural thing that was done to him, and he'd pass a polygraph swearing nothing was taken. *Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over.* That's the jacket, and I just watched it get said twice in one chapter, once by him about himself.

The hallway. Seventh grade, taught himself trig because he wanted to make pictures, asked questions his teacher couldn't answer, and she put his desk in the hall for a year and gave him long division. Vee says *they took the thing you loved and made it the punishment* and he says "that is approximately what happened," which is the most Pace sentence in the book, and then — this is the part that got me — he doesn't stay in it. He comes back up out of the hall and what he actually wants to tell her is the *good* part, the rooms full of people who thought it was beautiful. He's not collecting sympathy. He's trying to hand her something.

And the paint. A shape you can fill with a cup and never paint the outside of. The coastline that gets longer the more carefully you measure it. I'll be honest, the math monologue ran a half-step long for me — I felt the author's hand — but then "most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it, and I got to read some," and I forgave the whole thing, and so did she, and the book straight-up says she stopped listening and started watching, which is exactly what I was doing. Also the coastline bit is the book describing itself: shorter ruler, longer coast. Look closer at this and it goes on forever.

*Peter.* She asked what his mother calls him and he answered a completely different question and neither of them named the swerve. His mother stays home, his father works in a chemical plant, full stop, and that's all of it, next to her casseroles and saffron and jet engines. Something is behind that door and it is not nothing. And she says the name once, softly, "the way you say a word to keep it," and doesn't ask him to be Peter for her. God.

The scar. Her hand found it before she decided to, and he didn't move it — he put his hand *on top of hers and pressed it down* and said "not tonight," and she turned her palm up under his over the old seam. A door with a time on it. That's a man declining to tell you something without making you feel like you shouldn't have asked, and I want to put it in a frame.

The last three paragraphs hurt in the good-awful way. She's lying on him going through her luck: she *stumbled into* Randi outside the dining hall, literally, six weeks ago, and ten days later complained about an integral out loud and a man crossed a room for it, and it all came to her of a piece for no reason she did anything to deserve. One piece, he said. It is one piece! That's the problem! She's marveling at a coincidence that was a floor plan, and I'm the one holding the blueprint, and I have never so badly wanted to reach into a book.

Two things I'm sitting with. One: he's still planning to tell her. "He wanted to tell Vee. He could not, not yet." Every chapter that clock runs is a chapter the debt gets bigger. Two: nobody in this chapter mentioned the dress, the induction, or Randi's shoes, and I was grateful, and I'm now slightly afraid of how good the fall is going to be.
