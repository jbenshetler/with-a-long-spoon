Oh, finally. Fifty-two chapters and she lets me in behind his eyes, and the first thing in there is a man deciding not to ruin somebody's week.

That's the detail I'll keep: whatever he brought in from the truck, that he decided could keep. "He had long practice at deciding a thing could keep." She put the whole man in one clause before he's even sat down. That's the mechanism. That's the trapdoor, in miniature, with a different woman's name on it — he's holding something back from Sheri too, *for her sake*, and he has no idea that this is the thing he does, or he knows exactly and calls it kindness. I don't know which and I want to know so badly I could chew the page.

And then the two things I've been begging for, handed over inside four lines.

One: **"I told her she was the most important thing in my life. I said it, out loud, in words. And she looked at me like I'd handed her half of something."** He *saw* it. The laundry-room question — *maybe he understood and chose to say it like that, maybe it had gone past him entirely* — is answered, and it's the worse answer. It did not go past him. He watched her take delivery of half a thing and he has carried that look around for a month and he still won't spend the word. She retired her own best sentence in a laundry room thinking it might have missed him. It did not miss him. God.

Two: *"He could hear himself laying it up brick on brick and couldn't stop, because every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something."* That is the most generous sentence anyone has written about that man and also the indictment. The heat brought up hours early, the weeknights handed over without a fight, nothing else in the room when he's got her — all of it true, all of it masonry, and he built her a house with no door on it. *She didn't ask you for reasons.*

And then Sheri. Sheri just became my favorite person in this book and I'm including Cassie, which I did not see coming. **"Then not saying it matters more to you than having her."** And she held his eyes until *he* looked down — and then, because she's the only one who's ever been ahead of him about himself, she didn't let him off with a silence: **"And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty."** *From this seat.* Because she's sitting in the chair of a person whose people won't say the thing either, or can't, or are dead — and the book is too good to spell out which. *Not talking to my people. Call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas.* The sing-song and then killing it flat. *Ironic. Isn't it.* And Elvis coming on about being home for Christmas and nobody saying a word over it, and her not looking up from her plate: **"Somebody always plays it."** I went quiet in my kitchen. These two found each other two years ago over the same shape of hole and the whole friendship is built on not pressing it, and when he does reach for her it's with the one line he had: *people who've never lost anything think grief is a choice* — and she won't take it. *Don't waste the good stuff on me. Save it for your redhead.* She won't be comforted and she'll comfort him. She steals his fries. She orders him to the Brooks and Dunn cover band and she says *I don't care what the redhead's got to say about it* — which is the first time in two volumes anybody has treated Vee as a standing fact in his life, out loud, in a public room, and it wasn't him who did it.

Also Paige was a girl. *There was somebody. Paige.* Birds, a laugh too big, a house with seven at the table and everybody hollering and Sheri liked it there — of course she did, of course that's the thing she'd love and couldn't keep. Everybody in this book is starving at a table.

But. **"She find out about the brunette, then?"**

I had to read that twice and then I put the book down on my knee. *Not like that.* NOT LIKE THAT. There it is, said flat across a laid-down-amber tabletop by the one person who doesn't manage him, and he didn't flinch and didn't correct the premise. There is a thing about Randi for Vee to find out. He knows there's a thing. He's braced for the day. And then — **"That brunette you brought dancing before Thanksgiving — the sorority one."** He took *Randi* dancing. To the Rusty Nail. Before Thanksgiving. And Randi came in and *hugged him hello* in front of Sheri and then looked at Sheri like she was a harem applicant and **never did learn my name. But I got to see her.**

She went to *look at her.* Randi got herself taken to that hall to take inventory of the one woman in his life that she couldn't account for, and she didn't bother with the name because the name wasn't the point, the *sight* was. That's the woman who thumbed back through a camera roll clocking dates. And it means that when Vee tore that night to pieces over Sheri — the night she later lied about, *you went cold on me after the Rusty Nail*, with some back room of her knowing who'd carried a pie up whose steps — Randi had already *been* there, already measured the threat, already found it was nothing. Vee was running an audit Randi had closed weeks earlier.

And it lands on the chapter right before this one like a boot. Vee lay in a twin bed under her little gold swimmers at 11:52 and *built a blonde out of spite* to put in her own kitchen, in her own sheet, in her own best night — and the real answer is that he's in a double-wide on a county road going red in the face, saying *the air's different when she's in the room, it crackles,* and correcting a woman who called her "the redhead": **"Her name is Vee."** He corrected her name. And then said *I want Vee. I miss her.* Plain. No hedge, no arrangement, no *how good we are together.* He can say *I miss her* to Sheri. He can say *most important person in my life* to Vee. He cannot say the four words to the one who needs them, and now I've seen both rooms and I know the shortfall is not a shortage of feeling. It's whatever is standing behind the word in line, and Sheri is sitting right next to it, and the book will not tell me yet.

*She sees things nobody else does. She gets what it's for. The whole idea under it.* / **"The philosophy of your food. Lord almighty. Get over yourself."** And he laughed, the first full one of the night. I laughed too. I needed that as much as he did.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: HE TOOK RANDI DANCING AND SHERI SAW HER AND RANDI DIDN'T ASK HER NAME. Also: the sweetest man in the book is a coward and his friend just told him so to his face and he looked at the table. Also: Sheri's dead or estranged family, somebody put a coin in the jukebox, and he is sitting on news he's decided can keep. I don't trust that truck.

Two small flinches. *"Yeah. The one with the big tits"* and the Christmas-legs line — I get what they're for, it's how these two talk, it's the one register where he's allowed to be twenty-two and crude and it's honest — but it's the second time this volume I've heard the author elbowing me rather than the people talking. And it's now three chapters since those two were in a room together, and I feel it in my teeth: Switzerland, Ohio, a diner. Three empty chairs in a row. That's the only thing fraying me, and this chapter was good enough that I'd have forgiven a fourth. But not a fifth.
