REACTION

Okay. So that's what the book is. I knew from the jacket there was a plan and that I'd be ahead of Vee the whole way, but knowing it and watching it get *made* on a Tuesday afternoon under a burning maple tree are two different things, and I am sitting here with my hand over my mouth a little.

Let me take the parts separately because they did completely different things to me.

The bed. God. He asks her a question — one question, into her hair, while she's still coming down — and it detonates her. "The question reached her body before it reached her." And then the line that actually made me put the book down for a second: *He knew her body's answer. He did not take it for her answer.* That is it. That is the exact thing I have been looking for in this genre for years and almost never get. Every other book I've read would have had him say *your body already told me* and treated that as checkmate, and I'd have thrown it. He waits for the heat to leave the room. He waits for her to be gathered back into herself. He hopes out loud — to himself — that she can find a way to say something true without having to sit up and look at it, and then he lets her hand him the costume ("only in a threesome, with you") and he takes the costume and doesn't tear it off her. He *sees* that the wanting would be unsurvivable to her as hers, so he lets her hold it as a gift to him instead. That's not just consent-literate, that's kind. The man has the exact skill I want in a man, rendered better than I've ever seen it rendered.

And then he takes that skill out onto the quad and uses it on a girl who was never asked anything.

That's the trick of this chapter and I'm furious about how well it works. The pointing game is *fun*. I was having fun. I was shopping with her — too expected, those two go everywhere together, you'd get both or neither, god no. The sage one and the lavender one and the girl on the library steps with the beanie and one earbud. He kisses her in the middle of the walk with her own taste still on his mouth so she gets hit with it in public, and I felt that. "I point. You pick" — and watching her realize the choosing is hers, watching her go from good-sport to *scanning ahead of him*, the dancer's coil, the follow leaning into a turn that hasn't been called — that is as hot as anything in the bench chapter and nobody took a stitch off.

And the whole time the thing I'm being handed is: this is predation and it is delightful, and here is exactly how delightful, so you can't pretend later you didn't enjoy it. "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." Fine. I see you, Helen Rivers. I'm not being winked at — that's the thing that keeps me in the chair. A winking book would have made Vee less than she is. Instead Vee walks out of the dining hall and she is the only person on that whole campus who's *alive* — head thrown all the way back, laughing with her throat bare to the sky, laughing "like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen." That's a love letter. And the very first thing that happens to her is a shoulder-check engineered across thirty feet by a trained dancer so clean that *Vee* apologizes. Vee's hand flies to her own mouth. Vee says sorry. I felt sick and I thought it was gorgeous and those were the same feeling.

And Randi. Last chapter she lay on a made bed in last night's clothes with her shoes on and decided, quiet as a deadbolt, that she was never coming out again. This chapter is the first time we see her unmanaged in daylight — the dropped stride, the bare *yes* with nothing around it, the half-second of happy footwork on the brick she didn't decide to do, the *yes* that squeaked out of her. She is radiant. She is the most herself she's been since she cried on that bench. And it's the hunt that did it. The only way she can be real is if the realness is pointed at someone else. That's the saddest thing in this book so far and I don't think Pace has clocked it at all — he's over there glad she's excited, thinking it's the best idea he's had in a month. He read her perfectly in bed and he is reading her completely wrong on that lawn, because what he thinks is appetite is partly a woman running from a locked bathroom door.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *"she asked if the girl would like the bench. she asked that before she knew the girl's middle name. I am so unwell."* Because that's the line, isn't it. Vee has existed to these two for four minutes and she already has a furniture assignment.

What I'm watching for: whether Vee gets to be a person with her own inside, or whether she's the object this whole lovely machine gets pointed at. The jacket says we get her desire rendered from inside and that every step is hers, and I'm choosing to believe that — but it's on credit now. And whether Pace, who is so scrupulous about Randi's yes that he'll wait an hour for the right one, ever turns that scruple on the fact that the woman he just pointed at wasn't in the conversation. If the book thinks that asymmetry is invisible, I'm gone. If the book put it there on purpose — and the "I'm not the one being asked" / "I point, you pick" business suggests it did, he's already building a structure where nobody has to hold the whole decision — then this is the smartest thing I've read in this genre in years.

Give me Vee. Give me her side of that collision. I want to know what she thought when a beautiful stranger's hand stayed on her arm half a beat too long.
