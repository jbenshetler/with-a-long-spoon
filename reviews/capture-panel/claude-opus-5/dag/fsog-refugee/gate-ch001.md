REACTION

Oh, that's *clever*. I bought this book about Vee and the blurb told me there's a plan and two people who made it, and then chapter one hands me the other woman, naked and facedown and crying on a bench she didn't know was a bench. I did not expect to meet Randi first. I definitely did not expect to be gutted by her.

And the man — okay. Yes. This is the thing I've been looking for and mostly not finding. He asks. "Tell me your word." "Tell me again if you forget. I won't be upset. Tell me anytime." Not once as a formality, and then he keeps *coming back* — how's your back, hands and shoulders, you with me — and she gets exasperated at him for checking, which is exactly how a real person would react, and he checks anyway. The whole shape of it is him making room. He backs off from the kiss at the door so she can decide whether to take more. He lifts her hands out of his hair with two fingers under each wrist, *no grip in it*, and she puts them behind her back herself, and the book says it became an offering "without her deciding it." That's the line where I stopped being an observer. That's what I read for. Her yes is hers and it's doing things she hasn't authorized yet.

The apple. God, the apple. Sliced thin because she'll eat thin slices, cut sides up because she eats faster when the cut sides show. That's a man paying attention at a level nobody in my last six books managed, and it costs him nothing, and he's *sorry* about the calculation running behind her face. And she doesn't thank him for it and he doesn't need her to. Bite, kiss, bite, while she grinds on him and refuses to acknowledge it and he lets her refuse. Those hands going up her sides and turning back before they arrive — three passes, "You're doing that on purpose," "Doing what," and doing it again — I was so far inside that I forgot to be suspicious of anything.

Then the bench. That whole passage of her reading it, finding the face cradle, the handholds, the open middle where a surface should be, *the nothing under her* — I read it twice. "That's not a table." "Not only a table." "You said it was a table." "No, you said it was a table. I didn't correct you." And crucially she says *show me*. She walks in. Nothing is done to her that she doesn't step toward.

And still — *still* — the thing I can't shake is that he got exactly what he built it for and it broke something. The edging is the hottest sustained stretch of writing I've read in a while, the wet sound, his own heat returned to her in the wrong place on her spine, the three pleases that weren't the right pleases because they were said in the voice you use to ask a waiter for water. That's a devastating detail. And then the real one arrives from below and he says *good girl* and I felt it and then two pages later I'm in the bathroom with her looking at the face that has been lying for years, deciding — *quiet as a deadbolt going over* — that she is never coming out again. He can have the face.

That's the thing that scares me and it's not the spanking. It's that he called her beautiful and she *flinched*. It's that she pressed the bottle cap into her nail bed where it would hurt and didn't know she was doing it. It's that he's sitting there thinking he's had a remarkably good evening, moved by the girl who finally let someone feed her, quietly proud of taking her further than he expected — and he is *wrong*, and he's wrong in the only place his enormous attentiveness doesn't reach, which is the place she's defending. She locked the bathroom door for the first time ever and he respected it, and respecting it is the right thing and it is also exactly how he loses her. Daphne is sitting there in one paragraph like a loaded gun: he learned not to go through a closed door, and now the lesson is going to cost him. I don't know yet if that's tragic or if it's the mechanism by which everything in the blurb happens, and I'm dying to know which.

Which is where the plan comes in and where my hair stands up. Because the jacket says two people planned Vee's year. And this woman — the one with the monogrammed overnight bag and the sorority sisters who don't know his last name and the mother who lives on egg whites, the one who just decided her interior is permanently closed for business — *she's* the one who's going to steer a starving twenty-one-year-old into his path. Is Vee the thing she offers him instead of herself? A gift she can give without ever being seen again? Because that reads to me like the most elegant and most horrifying motive I could have been handed in chapter one, and the book didn't say a word of it out loud. It let me do it. And the blurb promised me it gets as true for the two who started it as for the girl who never knew — so somebody's deadbolt is coming off.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *the man is perfect, the safeword check-ins made me emotional, she cried and then fixed her hair and lied with her face and I think she's about to hand another girl to him and I have to keep reading right now.*

The only thing holding a sliver of me back: I want to be sure this book actually knows what it's showing me about him — that his pleasure in "having taken her further than he had thought she would go" is a real edge and not the narration patting him on the head. Right now I think it knows. That last image of the bench waiting under its tabletop tells me it knows.
