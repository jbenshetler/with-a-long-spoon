REACTION

Okay so I read the blurb, I bought this for Vee, I open Chapter One expecting a twenty-one-year-old walking into a room — and instead I'm in a warm house with Steely Dan on and a woman locking the deadbolt behind herself before she even says hi, and I'm three pages in going *who is this*, and then at the end he says *There you are, Randi* and I actually sat up. That's a hell of a move. It means the whole chapter reframes on the last word of the scene: I've just watched, in full, intimate, no-cuts detail, one half of the conspiracy get taken apart on a bench by the other half. The people who are going to do this to Vee. And the thing is — I wanted it. I was *in* it. He undressed her on one knee and I was right there with her, hands flat on her thighs, nowhere to put them that wasn't him.

The man. God. This is the thing I've been looking for and mostly not finding. He asks. He asks *more than once* — tell me your word, tell me again if you forget, I won't be upset, how's your back, hands and shoulders, *tell me* when she says fine — and none of it breaks the heat, it *is* the heat. The whole slow-hands sequence up her sides, arriving nowhere, turning back before he takes what she's arching into — that's dominance that makes room for her instead of erasing her, that's a man whose control is built entirely out of attention. And he never moves fast. The bit where he takes her hands out of his hair with two fingers under each wrist, no grip in it, and sets them back at her sides, and she puts them behind her back *herself* — that undid me a little. He didn't make it an offering. She did, and he let her find it. Also: he's still hard from the kitchen and he just... sets it down. *That's not what this night was for.* A man who doesn't need his turn in order for the night to have counted. I would read four hundred pages of that.

And then the last fifteen percent walks in and puts a knife under all of it, and I can't stop thinking about it. She locks the bathroom door — first time ever in his house — and he *respects it*, which is a gorgeous piece of character (Daphne, whoever she is, cost him that lesson and I want the rest of that story), and meanwhile she's on the other side of the door deciding, quiet as a deadbolt going over, *not ever*. He gets to keep the face. He thinks he's had a beautiful night. He thinks he *fed* her. He's sitting there quietly pleased with himself about how far he took her while she's pressing the sharp edge of a water bottle cap into her own nail bed and he clocks it and takes the bottle away and doesn't say anything. I noticed that. I noticed he noticed. That's the scariest thing in the chapter and it's not the spanking.

So here's my 1 a.m. text: *this man is the most careful lover I have read in years and he still doesn't know a single thing that's actually happening.* The whole book's engine just showed itself — he is genuinely, meticulously, consent-obsessively good, and it is not enough, because you can ask every question on the list and still be given a performance. And she walked out of that bathroom and curled into him and said *hi* and I felt sick in a way I'm going to keep reading toward.

What I'm suspicious of: how much of this is his gaze owning her. The narration inventories her — the sorority money, the mother's egg whites, the thing behind her face that polices food, the script for the *wanted* position — and it's all acute, but it's *his* acuity, and there's a smugness in how neatly he's got her filed. Right up until the end proves he had her filed wrong. I think the book knows that. I think that's the point. But if the next chapters ask me to find his connoisseurship of her damage romantic without ever letting her narrate back, I'll get restless. The two pages inside her head at the mirror were the best-written thing here and I want more of that, not less.

And now I know what the jacket meant by *as true for the two who started it*. If this is the woman who's going to go to lunch and open Vee up like a flower — a woman who just decided she will never be seen again by the one person who saw her — then this book is going to be much crueler and much sadder than I bought it for. Fine. I'm in.
