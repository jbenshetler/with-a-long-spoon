REACTION

Okay so I spent this entire chapter thinking I was reading Vee. Twenty-one, sorority, expensive monogrammed overnight bag, being undressed with that terrible patience — I had her slotted in as the starving girl from the blurb, and I was already bracing for the part where a book asks me to find it romantic that a man has memorized how thin to cut a woman's apple slices. And then: *"There you are, Randi."* I actually sat back. That's the best structural cheat I've been dealt in a while, because it doesn't just surprise me, it *retroactively re-genres* everything I just read. I wasn't watching the seduction. I was watching the *demonstration*. This is the method. This is what the two of them are going to do to a girl who doesn't know there's a plan, and the book just made me watch a full runthrough while I was enjoying it.

And I *was* enjoying it, which is the part that has me sitting here at one in the morning. The bench sequence is superb. Not because of the hardware — though "No, you said it was a table. I didn't correct you" is going to be the thesis sentence of this entire series and I'd bet money on it, the withheld correction, the consent that's technically clean because the information was unevenly distributed — but because of the *pacing* of denial. The edging is written from inside his attention rather than inside his appetite, which is a different and much colder thing. He's not greedy. He's *curatorial*. "Greed now would spend the night early, and there was better ahead of her, and he meant her to have it." He's still hard at the end and sets it down like a fact because *that's not what this night was for*. A man who doesn't need to come is so much more frightening than a man who does, and the book knows that, and doesn't wink about it.

Here's where it won me, though, and it's not the heat: the framing gap. Pace's last page and Randi's last page are describing two different evenings. He's got the warm glow of a man who fed a girl who'd been portioning herself — genuinely moved, genuinely tender, *genuinely wrong* — and she's in the bathroom deciding, "quiet as a deadbolt going over," that she is never coming out from behind the face again. He gets the beautiful night. She gets a permanent retreat. And the details the book plants in *his* POV that he doesn't read: the flinch at "you're beautiful." The lock turning over, which she has never done before. And oh, the water bottle cap — the sharp inner edge pressed slow and steady under the thumbnail, where it would hurt, and she doesn't know she's doing it, and he takes the bottle away without a word about it. That's the author telling me she sees exactly what she's built. A man this observant, observing that, and not clocking it. I don't have to argue with this book about whether its hero is alarming. It already knows.

The Daphne door is doing double work and I see it. He respects the closed door *because he learned*, which is real and earned and also — the reason he doesn't go in is the reason he doesn't find out he's just lost her. His ethics and his blindness are the same organ. That's genuinely good construction, not naive tenderness.

What I'm carrying forward, warily: the eating stuff. The mother on egg whites, the sorority "built to the one scale," and Pace lovingly optimizing *within* the disorder's permitted lines rather than ever naming it — cut thin because she won't eat thick, cut-side up because she eats faster. He calls it hunger and feels moved. I think the book knows that's management dressed as care. I *think* so. If chapter two lets him keep being moved without the narration flicking an eyebrow, I'll start to worry it's admiring him. And the "Good girl" landing right on the broken *please* — I clocked it, I don't hate it, it's in-genre, but it's the hinge where his pleasure and her dismantling become the same event, and I want the book to keep looking at that rather than settling into it.

The thing I'd text a friend: *this is a book where the woman being expertly and consensually taken apart in chapter one is one of the two predators.* She's not the victim, she's the co-author, and she just got done to her exactly what she's about to help do to somebody else, and she's going to carry that hum in her ribs into it. If Vee's chapters have this much interiority I'm in for the whole volume.
