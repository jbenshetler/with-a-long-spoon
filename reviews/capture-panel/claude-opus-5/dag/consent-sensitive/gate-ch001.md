REACTION

Okay so I have to text somebody about the name. I spent this entire chapter reading it as Vee. The jacket handed me a twenty-one-year-old college junior who's quietly starving, and then the chapter opens with a sorority girl and a bowl of apple slices cut thin because she won't eat thick ones, and I thought: there she is, that's my girl, that's the "quietly starving" from the blurb, gorgeous, literalized. And then — *"There you are, Randi."* I actually sat back. Because it means the woman who is going to hand Vee to this man as a game is the woman we just watched get taken apart on a bench and decide, in a locked bathroom, never to be findable again. The book didn't just withhold a name, it loaded the entire premise while I was distracted by a spanking. I'm annoyed at how well that worked.

And here's the thing I care about most: the scene itself is *technically* immaculate. Safeword asked for, asked for twice, "tell me anytime, I won't be upset," check-ins on her back and hands and shoulders, water left out in advance, the cashmere blanket she brought herself, twenty minutes of just being the thing she sits against. If you showed me only the mechanics I'd say: fine, this man does it right. But the book is doing something much colder and smarter than that, and it knows it. What he took from her tonight was not something a safeword covers. You can red out of pain. You cannot red out of being *seen*, because you don't find out it's happening until it already has. She consented to the bench. Nobody asked her about the rest of it. The book puts those two facts side by side without underlining either, and that's the exact thing I read for — the dark content it *knows* about.

The instrument I checked hardest: his last interiority. "A girl who had been portioning herself... who tonight had finally let someone feed her. The hunger had been in her all along. All he had done was find it, and answer it." That is a man writing himself the warmest possible review, and the author lets him have it *immediately after* we've stood in the bathroom with her while she decides he is never getting near her again. He thinks he opened a door. She's already gone over the deadbolt — and the "quiet as a deadbolt going over" callback to her locking his front door the second she walks in, first thing, before anything else? Come on. That's a writer who plans. He's holding a woman who isn't there and feeling "the small warm pleasure of having taken her further than he had thought she would go," and the chapter ends on the bench sitting damp in two places, *waiting*. Not romantic. Ominous. Good.

Details that got under my skin: the apple sliced cut-side-up because she eats faster that way — noticing-as-tenderness and noticing-as-engineering are the same gesture and the book refuses to separate them. "No, you said it was a table. I didn't correct you," which is a man perfectly at ease with a technically-true nondisclosure, and the prose flags it rather than smoothing it. And the water bottle cap: she sets the sharp edge under her thumbnail and presses down into the nail bed where it will hurt and *does not seem to know she's doing it.* That's dropped with zero commentary and it's the most frightening sentence in the chapter. He takes the bottle away. He doesn't say anything. He's not naive — he's just decided not to interpret her to herself, which is either restraint or the most efficient way to keep a person.

The Daphne thing is a real hook. He learned door-respecting the hard way, and now the respecting is precisely how he loses this one — she's behind the door reassembling the face and he's on the bed being honorable about it. Beautiful trap.

What I'm watching: the prose gets appraise-y. The catalogue of her — "years of cheer written into the muscle," the dancer's legs, the buffed-to-a-high-gloss inventory — has a whiff of connoisseur-examining-the-thoroughbred, and if the whole book lives in his admiring head I'll tire. It didn't, tonight; she got the last interior word and it was the truest thing on the page. But that's the fault line.

Also, for the record: "There you are" and then "Good girl" — yes. Fine. I'm a person.
