REACTION

Okay so I went in braced. The blurb had already told me Randi and Pace are the two who make a *game* out of a twenty-one-year-old girl, and I fully expected chapter one to open on Vee getting steered. Instead the book hands me an hour and a half in a warm house with a woman I assumed was the predator, and by the end of it she's locked herself in a bathroom because she got found. "There you are, Randi." I actually said something out loud. That's a hell of a way to spend your opening move — spending the reveal of who's who to instead reveal *what happened to the architect first*.

And it reframes everything on the jacket. If Randi was the one on that bench, begging in a voice she hadn't used since she was a child, deciding in a locked bathroom that she is never coming out from behind the face again — then steering Vee toward Pace isn't a game, it's a *substitution*. Here, look at her instead. Find her. Stop finding me. I would text a friend at 1 a.m.: THE PLAN IS A DECOY. She's not sharing him, she's hiding behind another girl's body. That's the theory and I'm now reading the whole book to see if it's right.

The consent machinery is genuinely good and I relaxed a little: the safeword, "tell me again if you forget, I won't be upset," the mid-scene checks on her back and hands and shoulders, the refusal to open a locked door because of Daphne. This is a man with an ethics he learned the expensive way. Which is exactly why the thing underneath alarms me so precisely. He cut the apple thin because she won't eat thick slices. Cut sides up because she eats faster that way. He built a piece of furniture, let her call it a table, watched her lay her underwear on it for weeks, and when she catches it his answer is "*you* said it was a table. I didn't correct you." That's not a consent violation. It's something adjacent and much harder to name — a man who runs a private model of a woman more accurate than her own, and manages her with it, and never lets her see the model.

The book knows. That's the thing that kept me. The water bottle. She sets the sharp edge of the cap under her thumbnail and presses down into the nail bed where it hurts, and she doesn't know she's doing it, and *he takes the bottle away and says nothing and pulls her closer and feels warmly pleased with himself.* He sees it. He does not read it. Then he sits there thinking about the apple slices and how *moving* it is that a girl who portions herself finally let someone feed her — that line made my jaw tighten, a man finding an eating disorder poignant — and twenty feet away she's in the mirror doing the math on how to make sure this never happens again. The two interiorities are laid right next to each other and one of them is flatly wrong. Nothing in the prose nudged me to notice. It just let him be wrong, at length, tenderly, while she went dark. That's the book having awareness rather than announcing it.

It's also — I want to be honest — extremely hot. The apple game, bite, kiss, bite. The hands going up her sides and turning back before they arrive, three times, until she gives up and puts them where she wants them. Him on one knee undressing her before a single button of his own came undone. The bit where he lifts her hands out of his hair with two fingers under each wrist, no grip, and she puts them behind her back herself and it becomes an offering *without her deciding it* — that's the whole thesis of this book in one gesture and it also got me. And he doesn't come. "That's not what this night was for." Which is generous and is also a man keeping a reserve, and I don't think the book thinks those are different things.

What I'm watching for: the prose likes him. A lot. There's a trophy note — that small warm pleasure of having taken her further than he thought she'd go — and the closing image of the bench sitting there "waiting for the next time" is a little pleased with itself. If Vol. One spends chapters admiring Pace's technique without ever again cutting to what it's actually doing to somebody, I'm gone. But this chapter earned a lot of rope: it gave the woman the last word, and her last word was a deadbolt.

Also: "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." I thought that was blurb swagger. It isn't. She said yes to all of it and the yes is precisely what cost her.
