REACTION

Okay so first: I got played, and beautifully. I came in from that jacket braced for Vee, primed to watch a twenty-one-year-old get walked into something, and instead chapter one hands me the *other* woman — face down on a bench, coming apart — and doesn't tell me her name until she's already crying. "There you are, Randi." I actually made a noise. Because now I know what the technique looks like from inside, and I know it before I've even met the girl it's going to be pointed at. That's a genuinely expensive structural choice and the book spent it on page one. Good.

The thing I'd text at 1 a.m.: **the water bottle cap.** She drinks, she keeps it, she turns the cap in her fingers and presses the sharp inner edge under her thumbnail into the nail bed *where it would hurt*, and "she did not seem to know she was doing it," and he takes it away and pulls her closer and it is never mentioned again. That one sentence is the whole book announcing what kind of book it is. Nobody writes that by accident, and nobody writes that while thinking this is a story about a nice man giving a girl a lovely evening.

Which is the test I was running the whole chapter, and the chapter passed it in a way I didn't expect. Because on the surface Pace is doing everything right — the safeword, "tell me again if you forget, I won't be upset," checking her back and her hands and her shoulders, the blanket she brought, the water he set out in advance, the twenty minutes of just being the thing she sits against. He respects the locked door. He has a whole learned history about *why* he respects the locked door — Daphne, and a door he went through once. Technically this is model behavior. And I still had my hackles half up the entire time, because look at where his attention actually lives: he sliced the apple thin *because she won't eat thick slices*, cut-side up *because she eats faster that way*. He clocked the thing that polices her eating, he named her mother's egg whites and the knife in her voice — and then he used it as an input. Not a concern. A lever. And the "you said it was a table, I didn't correct you" — small, charming, and exactly the shape of the thing: he holds information she doesn't and lets her walk into the room unbriefed. He built a piece of furniture for a purpose, let her put her underwear on it for weeks, and unveiled it.

And here's why I'm still here instead of gone: the book puts his self-congratulation and her mirror scene *back to back*, and lets me see the gap. He's on the bed feeling "the small warm pleasure of having taken her further than he had thought she would go." He's moved by the apple. He thinks she "finally let someone feed her." He's deciding between dinner and asking her to stay. He is completely, warmly, unmistakably **wrong**, and the narration is not on his side about it — it just declines to say so out loud. Meanwhile she's at the sink deciding *not ever*, quiet as a deadbolt going over, putting the face back on and sealing it. The flinch at "you're beautiful" — involuntary, landing somewhere she wasn't braced — is the tell he doesn't catch and I did. That's a book that knows. That's a book that thinks its charming man's greatest skill is also the thing he can't see past.

Also, unexpectedly, I feel for her. I did not expect to arrive at the end of chapter one with the designated schemer as the most wounded person in the room. "Nothing happened to her. That was what the face was for." Christ. That's not villain interiority, that's a whole person with an armor problem, and the book gave it to her before it asked me to judge her for anything.

What turned me on: honestly, the long slow undressing on one knee, and the withholding-hands game at the kitchen table — his palms going up her sides and turning back a little higher each time until the apple's forgotten in her hand. That's craft at the level of *attention*, and attention is the kink here, clearly, for everybody. The edging sequence worked on me. The transition where he crouches upside-down at her face and says "hi" worked on me harder than the strikes did.

What I'm suspicious of: I want to know whether the book intends "he is exquisite at reading bodies and catastrophic at reading refusal" as its thesis or as its romance. One chapter in, the thumbnail and the flinch say thesis. I'll be checking again the second nothing's happening.
