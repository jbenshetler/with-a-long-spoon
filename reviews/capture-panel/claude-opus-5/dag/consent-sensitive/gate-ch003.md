Oh, they *picked* her. They stood on a lawn in the good autumn light and picked her, and the book let me watch it happen from inside the head of the man doing the pointing, and I am sitting here with my hand over my mouth.

Here's what's got me. I came into this chapter expecting the bench woman to be Vee — the jacket says twenty-one, says the men have been fine — and Chapter 1 pulled the name at the last possible second and Chapter 2 kept me down in the cold corners of that sorority house with her, and *now* I understand what the first two chapters were for. They were the origin story of the trap, told entirely from inside the trap-builders, and then the trap walks out of a dining hall with her head thrown back laughing, "laughing like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen." That line hurt me. That is the *only* thing about Vee we know so far and it is exactly the thing they are going to take.

And the shoulder-check. God. Randi engineers a collision so precise that *Vee apologizes to her* — the victim's hand flies to her own mouth — and Pace stands at his light pole reading it like a connoisseur, a decade of dance folded into three seconds of execution so clean it looked like nothing. That's the "lightly, laughing, as if by accident" from the back cover and I've now seen the mechanics of it and I will never be able to read Vee's version of that meeting as a meet-cute. That's the book delivering on its promise to put me ahead of her. It's horrible and it's delicious and it's *earned*.

Now the part I was watching hardest, because this is where a book gives itself away: Pace asks the question while she's mid-come, still open, still pulsing, "before she was done" — and the text says flat out he had decided this beforehand. He timed the question to arrive at her maximum undefendedness. And then: *"He took the body's answer for the true one. He usually did."*

That sentence is the whole book for me right now. Because that is the epistemology of every man who has ever overridden a woman's mouth with her own hips, and the author put it there in a short declarative with a little chill on the end — *he usually did* — and did not decorate it. If that line hadn't been there I'd have closed the book. It was there. So I'm staying, warily, with my hand on the door.

Same with the reframe. Randi can't own wanting a woman — "unsurvivable" — so she converts it into a gift for her boyfriend, a threesome, the same appetite with a different owner, and Pace sees the entire maneuver and lets it stand because it's "the wrapper the wanting needed to come out in." He is choosing, consciously, not to correct a self-protective fiction because the fiction gets him what he wants. He tells himself it's tenderness. It's *also* tenderness. That's the thing I can't get over — the book won't let me pick. He really does want her to have joy. He also runs her like an instrument he built, and the book keeps showing me the lathe.

And the thing he doesn't know! He's lying there thinking the bench was a door opening. She decided in that bathroom mirror that it was a door closing — *he could have the face, and she wasn't coming out again* — and this whole chapter she's handing him the bright daylight version of herself and he's reading the seams half-right and the center completely wrong. All that surveillance, all that attention, and he has no idea the woman he "found" already went back inside and bolted it. He's not omniscient. He's just very good, and being very good is exactly what's blinding him. That's the best structural thing in the chapter and it's the reason I trust the author more than I trust Pace.

Things I'm carrying: the cheeseburger. "I can't eat that," instant, not even a glance. And he closes the chapter with "Let's go feed you." He has cast himself as the man who feeds the starving woman — the apple slices, cut sides up — and he is *so* pleased with that role, and nobody in this book has yet asked whether a man who portions a woman's appetite for her is the cure for a woman whose mother portioned her appetite for her. I want the book to notice. I think it might. Chapter 2 makes me think it might.

The thing that could still lose me: this is now three chapters of a POV that admires itself and is largely validated by events. Ch 2 was the counterweight — quiet, cold, no heat to hide in, and it let Randi be a person with a cost — so I know the author can do it. But if Volume One is a long parade of Pace being right about everyone while the women's interiority shows up only as ninety seconds of aftermath per chapter, the warmth will go smug and I'll leave. Give me Vee's head. Give me Randi's head somewhere other than a locked bathroom.
