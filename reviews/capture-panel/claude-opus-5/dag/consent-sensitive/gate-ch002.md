REACTION

Okay so the first thing I have to say is that the jacket lied to me in the good way, and chapter one already did it, and chapter two is the book putting its hand on my shoulder and confirming it knew exactly what it did. I went into that bench scene assuming I was watching Vee. I was sitting there doing my usual math — twenty-one, glamorous older friend steering her, man who builds furniture to specification — and the whole time I was reading a woman named Randi, who per the blurb is the *other* half of the plan. The co-author. And she's the one who got taken apart.

So now my reading of the entire premise has rotated ninety degrees and I love it. Because here's what chapter two tells me: Randi didn't wander into this. She sat on that bench, met something in herself, went into a bathroom, locked the door — *locked it,* first time ever, and he clocked it and honored it, which was the single most reassuring beat in the chapter for me — and made a decision, quietly, in the shape of a deadbolt: *he can have the face, I'm not coming out again.* And then chapter two is her carrying that decision home through a room full of women who read faces professionally, and lying down in last night's clothes with her shoes on under a duvet that is explicitly not the cashmere she keeps at his house.

Here's my 1 a.m. text: **she's going to hand him Vee.** Not out of malice. As an escape. The plan the blurb promises isn't a hunt, it's a *substitution*. Randi found out what it costs to be the one he actually looks at, decided she cannot survive being seen, and needs somewhere else for that gaze to land. Steer a hungry twenty-one-year-old into his path, stay in the room, get to watch it happen to someone else, get to have the intimacy secondhand over lunches "too intimate to name." That's not a game between two clever people. That's a woman trying to stay behind glass, and the blurb's line — "as true for the two who started it as for the girl who never knew" — suddenly reads like the trap closing on *her*. If that's the book, I'm in for all of it.

What buys my trust, concretely: the placement. The author wrote the hottest, most technically loving scene they could — the apple slices cut thin and cut-side-up, the hands that keep turning back before they arrive, the whole architecture of that bench — and then made the *very next chapter* the invoice. No heat to hide inside. Just a woman who can't sit down on her own bed. And the invoice doesn't say "she was violated"; it says something much more interesting and much harder, which is that she got something enormous and true and it cost her the only structure she has. That's a ledger being kept honestly. Nobody in chapter one did anything wrong and it still wrecked her, and the book knows both halves.

And the not-crying paragraph. "It felt like there being no door." "The breath was held." That's dissociation written from the inside with no diagnostic voice hovering and no eroticizing of it either, and set next to the water-bottle cap pressed into her nail bed last night — which she "did not seem to know she was doing" — I now believe this author knows exactly what condition this woman is in. Which matters enormously, because Pace *doesn't*. That's the thing I'm chewing on. His closing beats in chapter one are him being *moved* — a girl portioning herself, and all he did was find the hunger and answer it. He's building a story about himself as the one who feeds her, and he's wrong, and the book let us see he's wrong by giving us her bathroom mirror. He is careful, he checks in, he takes her word and offers it back to her twice, he leaves the water out in advance, he doesn't open the locked door because of some old wound named Daphne — and he is also reading her completely incorrectly and cannot feel it. Competent and blind. That's a genuinely scary combination and much more interesting than a predator.

The sorority scene is where I got my best instrument reading, and it's excellent. Four minutes, mostly smiling, and it's a full interrogation. Brooke not standing up. Brooke asking about the paper she already knows is a lie. Brooke filing the name — "*Pace,*" like she was filing it, which she was. The engineering guess that lands him a notch down without seeming to reach for it. And then Brooke does the thing that made me sit up: *he doesn't teach, does he — you're not in one of his sections.* The book brought up the institutional power question itself, unprompted, and had a woman in authority flag it as the thing that would be scandalous. Then it released it in two seconds with a laugh.

I don't believe the release. "The fraction-of-a-second was wider here." That is the widest pause in the scene, wider than the lie about the coffee shop on Wilson. Either she's lying about the section, or — and this is what I actually think — that question is a loaded gun the book is laying down for Vee, who is a junior with a math requirement. And Brooke, who runs this house and files names, is going to be the one holding it. Randi sits on *Standards*. The girl who adjudicates conduct violations is sneaking in at ten a.m. and lying about a coffee shop. The title of the chapter is doing work.

Two small things off. The Sloane/Kinsey chorus dips toward caricature — the Raptor bit, "short-cute" — and it's fine as camouflage, they're the noise Randi has to smile through, but they're thinner than everyone else on the page. And there's one line where the narration puts its thumb on the scale: *a woman who would one day be some CEO's third wife.* That's contempt, and it's aimed at a woman, and it comes from a voice that has been scrupulously non-judgmental about Randi's eating and Randi's lying. I noticed it. It's the one place the book felt like it had a favorite and was willing to be cheap about someone else to protect her. I'll let it go once.

Still no Vee, two chapters in. I'm not worried yet — the structure of showing me the machinery before the mark is deliberate and it's working, I'm being made complicit exactly as advertised. But I want her soon, and I want her to have as much inside her as Randi has, because if Vee turns out to be a device rather than a person this whole thing collapses.
