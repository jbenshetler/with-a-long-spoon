Oh, this is the chapter I've been waiting for and I didn't know it. Eight chapters in and this is the first time Vee has been *alone* — not alone in a room, alone in the sense of nobody narrating her. No Pace reading her like a page he already owns, no Randi sitting on a man's lap describing her as a cup built by a girl nobody ever told to stop. Just Vee, a bad old swimsuit, and her own body as a logistics problem.

And the book gave her the underwater. That's the thing that got me. "She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem." I put the book down on my chest for a second. Because that's the whole architecture of her, isn't it — the cardigan over the better shirt, the mother's voice arriving whole before the thought finishes, *floozies*, the froyo cup that got away from her because for once nobody said *that's plenty*. And under all of it there's a girl who was weightless at sixteen and got interrupted. She goes down there and *grieves, a little.* Not about a man. Not about being chosen. About the part of herself that got taken before anyone in this novel showed up. That's interiority the book didn't have to give her, and giving it here — in a chapter with no heat in it, where there's no payoff for the author except honesty — is the single most reassuring thing that's happened so far. This book knows what it's doing to her. It knows what she's made of, and it knows it's a bruise, not a kink.

The walk past the bleachers is handled beautifully and it made me furious in the correct direction. The pre-loaded face, the rolled towel carried *down at her side, not up against her*, the long blink, the eyeroll aimed at the ceiling — a whole vocabulary of practiced non-reaction, a girl who has been doing this since she was what, thirteen? And then Cassie's *tossers*, low, kept for Vee, when she has the lungs to pitch it into the bleachers and doesn't — and Vee's laugh going *past* what the joke earned, fifty yards of held breath letting go. God. That's the first time anyone in this book has given Vee something without taking a reading off her afterward.

Which is why I am now genuinely scared about Cassie. Cassie doesn't ask. Cassie splashes her from ten feet away instead of walking over and making it a scene. Cassie clocked the look Pace gave Randi — "the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt" — and said it out loud at the table, to Randi's face, without a flicker. Cassie declined the yellow coffee shop on Draper flat, no sulk, no edge, and said *Goodbye, Vee,* the whole word, the word for a longer parting than a Wednesday. Cassie is the tripwire, and she's also the only love in this book that isn't engineered, and I know exactly what a book like this does with a tripwire. If Cassie gets managed out — gently, by a woman with a beautiful smile who *includes* her every single time so it's never Randi's fault — I will be sick about it, and I think that's the plan. What I need is for the book to know Cassie is the cost. Right now it looks like it does: she's in shorts and yesterday's shirt and a navy suit that does her no favors and she is written with more dignity than anyone else in the novel.

One flicker of worry: "There was more of her now. That was the trouble with Vee's suit: it fit." I braced, for half a line, for the book to start enjoying her discomfort the way it enjoys describing her at the doors of the dining hall. It didn't. It stayed with her side of it. But that narrator does love looking at her, and I'm keeping a hand on that.
