The credit card. That's the chapter. This book has counted her money since page one — the right side of the menu first, the Thursday arithmetic, the mimosa she priced and then the second one she didn't — and I have been reading all of that as texture, and it turns out it was a ledger being kept so that this one gesture would have a cost I could feel in my own stomach. She stood there a half second with the debit between her fingers. The card that only spends what's already behind it. And she put it back. *There was nothing under it. She knew that even while she did it.* I actually put my hand over my mouth. That's the first thing anyone has taken from her — not the shirt, not the green satin, which she gave on purpose and gladly and which cost her nothing but shame she was done with. This cost her something that will still be there next month, and the jacket told me at the register in the bookstore that nothing would be taken that she didn't hand over, and here she is handing it over smiling, *because the one thing she would not do in this room was flinch.* Who taught her that. Who taught her that a woman doesn't flinch about money in a room like that.

And then Randi buys loafers. Several times the number. Without looking down. Telling a story about a woman who ruined a coat, hands going, the whole time — which is the most expert piece of cruelty in this book so far and I don't think Randi knows she's doing it. That's the thing. She wasn't performing wealth at Vee; she was just being herself in a shop, and being herself in a shop is a thing that happens *to* Vee. Then she walks out in them, trades shoes mid-afternoon like nothing, and Vee carries her box against her chest with both arms like it might get up and leave her, and *you can put it in the trunk, it's not going to spoil* — which is affectionate and funny and lands right under the rib, exactly as the book says it does, and is also the single most class-legible sentence anyone has said out loud in twenty-six chapters.

But the knee. Oh, the knee. Randi puts up a hand at the saleswoman — *I've got her* — and goes down onto one knee on a pale floor in her own beautiful clothes, and takes Vee's foot in both hands, and doesn't reach for the box. She turns it to the light and looks at the toes. At the frosted plum. Which she asked about at brunch and didn't get an answer to, because the answer came up in Vee's face instead, and Randi read that face and let it go by. So she is kneeling on the floor of an expensive shop holding a foot painted to match the color of a dress her lover has been making, all week, at hours with no business being awake in them, for the girl whose foot is in her hands. *"Mm."* That's all. One thumb across the nail of the big toe, light, and the floor of Vee tips and her hand shoots out to a shoulder to stop the room swinging, and Randi looks up and *smiles and says nothing at all* and bends back to the box. She had the door right there and she didn't open it and she didn't slam it either. That's twice now she's gotten to the edge and declined. At the booth it was mercy. This time I'm not sure it was mercy so much as she couldn't afford to hear herself say it.

*He didn't make this for you. He made it to you.* Yes. God. That's the best line anyone has been given and it's given to Randi, which is the book's whole cruelty — she has the vocabulary for what he did and Vee had only tears. And she hands it over and then watches Vee's throat go. What is it like to name that out loud, and then to reach up and lift a strand of the girl's hair off her face. She touched the silk at the hip first, reading the cut with her fingers the way you read something you're going to have to account for. *Charmeuse*, not to Vee, to the dress, or to herself. She was standing in a cinderblock dorm room appraising the evidence.

And the comedy actually worked on me, which I wasn't expecting from this book — the canoe with the snow-tire sole, *do yetis shop here*, the rhinestone platforms and *do these go with burgundy* delivered grave and strained like a woman at serious work, and then the flat that's one brisk walk from becoming a sandal, which is the one that gets her all the way, folded over the bench, *the real laugh, the one that costs her something to give.* Vee's actual power in this book is that she's funny. She stumped Pace about the bust measurement and made him laugh a real laugh; she breaks Randi with a broken clearance shoe. Both of them get taken out by her jokes and neither of them planned for that. The book knows it and hasn't said it, and that's the withholding I trust.

Cassie's bed, made tight and square, nobody home, and Randi's eyes going over it. She's not even a person in this chapter, she's bedding. Vee didn't shut her out this time — the book did, by scheduling. I'm watching that. The eyebrow that went up and came back down about the shoes last chapter was the last thing she's had.

*Same size, same everything. We can share. God help us both, it's practically a marriage.* That's a hook going in and it's dressed as a coincidence, and I want to know how much of the boutique was improvised. *I know just the place.* The dress hung on a brass hook at the end of the fitting bench, *there for exactly this*. And she said *your lover* and watched Pace rearrange in the girl's chest into something adult and lit. She named him. She gave Vee the word for him. She is building this with both hands and I still can't tell how much of it she's doing on purpose anymore, which is precisely the problem she's in.

The only thing that scratched: *to you* got said three times and the third one was a sentence explaining the feeling the previous two already gave me. Though I'll grant the man's whole method is say-it-more-than-once-so-the-noise-can't-kill-it, and Randi's using his technique on his girl, so maybe I'm being handed that on purpose.
