REACTION

This is the chapter I've been waiting twenty-seven chapters for and I didn't know it was this one. Two people under a quilt in front of black glass with cold tea, talking, and I didn't move for the whole thing.

First: he finally stopped being buffed and started being *explained*, and the explanation is so much worse than any flaw the book could have given him. The desk in the hall. A whole year of long division in a corridor because he asked a question his teacher couldn't answer, and Vee — Vee, who I have been watching learn to say things — says *they took the thing you loved and made it the punishment*, and he says *that is approximately what happened*, and I put my own tea down. And then he comes up out of it, because the hall isn't where the story lives for him, it's just the door, and he gives her the paint shape and the coastline and then the three sentences that do the ball and the leaf and the stars, *it's all one piece*, and I am sorry but I was gone. That's the *say it more than once so the noise can't kill it* man doing the thing he actually does, which is hand you the world with a light turned on it. I would have let him talk for another forty pages.

And then Daphne. I've been carrying that name since chapter whatever, and here it is, and it's not a heartbreak, it's a crime with the paperwork filed. *They assigned me someone.* An arrangement. An older student to help the child find his feet. She was nineteen. He was fifteen. Two years. And he lays it out in *good order* — nothing happened, nobody did anything wrong, she went on, which was only sensible — and the book is smart enough to know the good order is the tell, and Vee is now smart enough to hear it, and she gets the clean ugly grown-up word all the way to her tongue and holds it there because *it was not hers to say.* That restraint nearly took my head off. And then: *he handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck.* That is the sentence of the volume. That is the whole book saying its own name out loud.

Because — okay, this is the thing I'd text at 1 a.m., in caps — **it's the same shape.** Somebody handed a fifteen-year-old to a nineteen-year-old as a kindness, as an arrangement, as a thing decided about him that he never knew was decided, and every step of it he gave gladly and calls it the best two years he'd had. And he and Randi *made a choice about Vee in a room she wasn't in.* He is running Daphne. He doesn't know he's running Daphne. *Nothing happened. Nobody did anything wrong.* Which is the jacket's sentence with the serial numbers filed off. When Randi said *it's selfish, Pace* in that kitchen over the bowl she wouldn't eat, she was more right than she could possibly have known, and she doesn't know either — or does she? She built a file on him. Did she know about Daphne when she picked him? If she knew, that's the ugliest thing in this book so far, and I want it.

The other detail I can't put down: *I was fifteen in a dormitory full of men.* / *I never had to find out.* So the powerlifting has a predator-shaped origin — he built that chest so nobody could take him — and the one person who *did* take him didn't need to be stronger than him, she just had to be the only tender thing in the building. He armored against the wrong door. He still thinks the tender one wasn't a door.

Smaller things that got me. *My real name is Peter* — offered as the answer to *what does your mother call you*, which is not an answer, and the book says so, flatly: *he had taken his mother out of the answer. Vee let him.* Twenty-seven chapters and the door at *parents* is still shut and the book has never once pretended it isn't a door. I trust this book completely on that. And then *Peter*, said twice — once to feel how it sat in her mouth, once at the end, softly, *the way you say a word to keep it.* Not to use it, to keep it. She has a drawer now too. She has a name in it.

The scar. Her hand went along it before she thought about whether to, and *more of it than she'd known*, and he put his hand over hers and pressed it *down* and held it there and said *not tonight* — he didn't move her hand off it, he pinned it to the thing. A door with a time on it. She turned her palm over under his and that was the whole asking and the whole answer. That's better than being told. That's the withholding being a shape.

And the sex talk without sex: her hand on his thigh, his palm on her breast holding the weight of it without pressing, *as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm*, the robe tied badly and her not minding — and then she assembles the squat bar and him in bed into one thought, *it's the lift, it's the same face*, and it comes together in her body before it comes together as a thought. She figured out what he does to her by listening to him talk about steel bending like dry spaghetti. That's hotter than most of the explicit chapters and nobody took anything off.

What I'm uneasy about: the last three paragraphs. *She had stumbled into her. Literally.* A man crossed a coffee shop for it. Six weeks. *She had never been so fortunate, or so happy.* I know what the book's doing — it's the drum, it's the reader-ahead-of-her thing the jacket promised, and I signed up for it. But this is the third or fourth time we've been walked through *lucky* and I'm starting to feel the elbow. Randi already told her *you're not caught, sweetheart, you're lucky* at brunch and that landed because Randi said it. Having Vee narrate her own gratitude in summary at the end of the best conversation in the book is the one place tonight the author stood between me and them. I don't need the ledger read back to me. I was there.

Still: he named it luck. She named it fortune. Same word, two mouths, one of them was groomed at fifteen and one of them is being chosen right now by two people in a kitchen. The name that means love in one mouth means trouble in the other. He said that himself, about *Vivienne*, forty minutes earlier, and didn't hear it.

I want Randi's face when she hears the word Daphne. I want to know if it's already in a folder in that green car.
