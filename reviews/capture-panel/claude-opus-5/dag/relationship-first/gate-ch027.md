REACTION

Oh, this is the chapter. This is the one I'd have stayed for six more nights to get, and it arrived without a single event in it — two people under a quilt with cold tea, and I did not look up once.

Let me get to the thing first because I have been walking around with it since the sentence landed. *She was nineteen and I was seventeen* — no. Back up. *She was nineteen* when they assigned her to him, and he was **fifteen**, and it went on two years, and she was his *tutor*, an arrangement made by adults who were worried the opportunity would be wasted. And Vee does the arithmetic and the word starts to come up in her and she holds it down under her tongue because *it was not hers to say*. I want to put my hand flat on that paragraph. And then he defends it. Not defensively — he lays out every reason in good order, each one in its slot, and the good order is the tell, and the book *says* the good order is the tell, and then: *he had handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck.* That's the best sentence in twenty-seven chapters and I'm not sure it's close.

Because here's what it does to everything else. He was fifteen and someone older chose him and arranged it and was tender with him and it was the best two years of his life, and he came out the other side certain nothing wrong had happened. And now he is twenty-two and there is a woman in her twenties who selected a twenty-one-year-old for him as a game, and he is running the most exquisite patient courtship in the world on her, *every step of it hers*, and he believes that with his whole chest. I have been reading this book for three weeks wondering what the engine under Pace's carefulness was and it turns out the carefulness *is* the wound, reproducing. He's not replicating what Daphne did to him. He's replicating what he decided it *was*. Ask, leave the room to choose, never take an inch — that's a man building the thing he was given and can't call by its name. Christ. And I love that the book let Vee nearly get there and then had her lay her palm on his chest instead of saying it. She's going to say it eventually. I want to be in the room.

Then the last page and I actually made a noise. Vee lying there warm and soft doing her gratitude inventory: she stumbled into a stranger outside the dining hall, *literally* stumbled, and ten days later said out loud to her own table that she couldn't do an integral and a man crossed a coffee shop for it. Six weeks. *For no reason she had done anything to deserve.* And I'm sitting there holding the whole apparatus — the pointing walk, the plan, the collision — while she counts her luck. This is the thing the jacket promised and the first time it's cost me something real. It's not suspense. It's grief in advance. She's never been so fortunate or so happy and she's right, and that's what's awful.

Smaller things I want to keep. **Peter.** She asks what his *mother* calls him and he answers with a name and not with her — he took his mother out of the answer and *Vee let him*, and the book said so plainly. The door at *parents* is still shut and now it's shut with a name sitting on the doorstep. And the way she says it twice, once to feel how it sits in her mouth and once at the end, *the way you say a word to keep it*. That's the chapter's romance for me, more than the palm on her breast.

The scar: **not tonight**, and his hand coming down over hers and pressing it into the seam instead of moving it, and her turning her palm up under his so they were both on it. A door with a time on it. Twenty-seven chapters of that scar and he gave me a promise instead of an answer and I trust him more for it. The book knows what it's withholding, which is the only thing I actually need from a book.

The desk in the hall for a year. Long-division worksheets. *They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment*, and him looking down at her like she'd put her hand somewhere he hadn't offered and found the right spot — that's the same gesture as the scar, done in reverse, twice in one chapter, and the repetition is deliberate and earns itself.

My one grumble: the math aria ran a little long and Gabriel's horn is a party trick, the kind of thing an undergrad does at a party, and the coastline is its little brother. Newton's three sentences and *most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it* is gorgeous and also is the book buffing him again, and I felt the hand on my shoulder the way I felt it with the MRI patent. The hallway did more with less. Also Vee gets a shade too writerly in the Ohio stretch — "Ohio has its mysteries," the saffron old enough to vote — funny, but that's the author's wit on loan. Whereas *casseroles deep enough to forgive almost anything* and *it never tried to surprise anybody* are hers and are perfect.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *the tutor was NINETEEN and he was FIFTEEN and he calls it the best two years of his life and now he's doing the polite version of it to a girl someone picked out for him.* And then: *does Randi know about Daphne.* Because if she does, if she knows and built this anyway, then I've been wrong about which of them is the injured party all along. And if she doesn't — she's about to find out she's the sequel.
