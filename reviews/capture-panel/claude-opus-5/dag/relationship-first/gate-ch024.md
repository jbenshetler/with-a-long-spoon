REACTION

Okay so the thing I did not expect from this book was *comedy*, and it's the best thing in the chapter by a distance. "There's a lot of you covered in a lot of me" and then him twitching in her hands and her saying it again, slower, on purpose, to make it happen again — that is two people who have just discovered they're funny together, which is a completely different discovery from the ones they've been making. And then the whole relay of it: nowhere safe to put him, elbows fending off the silk, her leg sliding off his hip, and *neither of them able to let go of one single thing they were holding.* I laughed out loud at midnight. And it didn't cool the chapter down one degree — it ran hotter after, because now there's a room they can both be stupid in. The two stripes she wipes down his flanks while his arms are up and he can't do anything about it, and then "are they clean enough?" and "let me see" and him taking his time about it like a hem. That's the hottest thing in the chapter and it's two fingers. I'd text a friend just that: *he inspected her hands like tailoring and then put them in his mouth.*

The dress. He hid the work. That's the sentence that got me the way it got her — took the trouble, then took the further trouble to hide the trouble, so all that's left to see is the part that looks like nothing. And the lining: oyster-pale, doubled, only through the bodice and over the hips, *the only parts of her the dress had decided not to give away.* Nobody says a word about what that's for. A man measured her nipples through satin a week and a half ago and then quietly built a wall exactly there and she'll walk into a room full of people not knowing why she feels safe in it. That's the book withholding correctly, which it does about a third of the time, and when it does I'd follow it anywhere.

And then she takes the underwear off herself. That's the move. Not him, not a request — she sees the pale edge cutting the line and her second thought comes up hot behind the first and doesn't care, and she *left it on the chair with everything else she'd walked in wearing.* The girl who folded her own evidence in thirds and handed it over now strips herself for a mirror with no one in the room. And then strides — strides — down the hall to show him. She's spending the power again. She's getting good at it.

What I'm sitting with, though, and the reason I'm not just warm: this comes two days after Randi put her hands flat on the table and said *how long are you going to take* and *it's selfish, Pace* and ate the salad and not the stew. And then he was up all night finishing a dress. Vee lies there at the end knowing, without being told, that he stood at that table at some hour with no business being awake in it while she slept across town — and she's right, and she has no idea what put him there. She thinks the dress is about her. It is about her. It's also a man answering an accusation he couldn't argue with. Nobody in the chapter says this. The book doesn't nudge me once. I'm just holding it, ahead of her, which is what the jacket told me I'd be doing and I keep being surprised it still works.

Also, shoes. The hem pools; the dress wants a height she doesn't own and the sensible pumps "would have read like an apology." That's a hole shaped exactly like a gift, and I do not think Pace is the one who fills it. Randi buys those shoes. And the induction daydream — coming in late on *purpose*, standing in the middle of the light, letting the room turn — that's thrilling and it made my stomach drop, because her mother is coming to that induction. Her mother who had a word for frosted plum in October. A bias-cut wine silk with a slit to the hip and a bare shoulder and no bra, in a high room with the other girls in department-store navy, and her mother in a chair. That's not a triumph being set up. That's a detonation.

One gripe, the same one: *the shame of having stripped herself bare on purpose to be looked at, and it crossed into wanting almost before it was shame at all.* I had already felt it. I'd felt it in the mirror two paragraphs earlier with *floozy* flaring and her staying anyway, which was perfect and needed nothing after it. The book keeps explaining the transit it just performed. It's the error-correcting-code thing — say it more than once so the noise can't kill it — and I noticed twenty chapters ago that it's his method, and I'm now fairly sure it's the author's method too, and I'm less sure that's on purpose.
