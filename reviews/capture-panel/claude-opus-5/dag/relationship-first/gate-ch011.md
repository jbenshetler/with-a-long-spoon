REACTION

Okay so I finally got my lunch and the book was cleverer about it than I was. I asked for Vee and Randi alone in a room with no man in it, and what I got was two women alone in a room where the man is the entire content — and I don't think that's the book failing to deliver, I think that's the answer. Randi cannot sit down across from this girl without Pace on the table, because she's the one who put him there, and the only intimacy on offer between them is the intimacy of talking about him. That's an awful little shape and it's the right one.

The thing I can't stop turning over is the bad date. Randi opens with a *catastrophe* — tall, money, Switzerland, lacrosse numbers, didn't notice she'd gone quiet — and it's a fiction, because she wasn't on a date Friday, she was with Pace, and she has to be single in this room for the rest of her life. But look what the lie *does*. "A man who can't be bothered to listen to you across a dinner table, honey, is not going to bother taking care of you anywhere else." She hands Vee a criterion. She hands her the one criterion Pace has already met, forty minutes after Vee sat down with it still warm on her, and she does it before Vee has said a single word, so that when Vee tells it, she's not describing an evening, she's confirming a rule she was given. My God. That's not even manipulation, it's pedagogy. And it *worked on me too* — I sat there nodding at the lacrosse boy like he was a real man who'd wasted a real evening of hers.

And then the extraction. "You let him choose for you. And you liked it." Held up to the light, not a question, and then — this is the part that got me — "Mm, warm, and let her have it, and went no further." She stops. She doesn't push. She lets the girl feel let off. Randi edges in *conversation*. She does to Vee across a smoothie table exactly what Pace does to Randi on the bench: takes her to the edge of an admission, holds her there, backs off, and the girl thanks her for the mercy and hands over more. Twice Vee had the dignified version sitting right there ready — *I made an exception* — and twice it came out as the smaller truer thing instead. "Because of him." That's the whole book in three syllables and Vee spent them without noticing.

But here's what I didn't see coming and what I'll be carrying: Randi slipped. "Ask and then ravish." A beat late, low, and the book tells me flat out it's the first thing she'd said all day at Vee's own volume. Everything else out of her mouth is projected — the throne, the wattage, the *you little minx* — and for one line she drops to where Vee actually lives, and Vee, halfway up an imagined mountain with her hands running down her own thighs, "felt her there in it beside her." That's the chapter's real heat and neither of them is allowed to know it happened. Two women getting warm over a scenario they've each cast a different person into. I'd text a friend at 1 a.m. about exactly that sentence.

The Cassie thing is the knife and the book didn't underline it, which is why it landed. Cassie's look goes *past* Vee to Randi and gets folded away before it comes back — that's a girl learning where she is in the order and deciding not to say so. And then Randi, of all people, is the one who offers: *we could have dragged her.* Randi offers and **Vee** shuts the door. "It isn't really her place." Vee — who goes thin in that doorway, who's underdressed before she looks at a single face, who is about to be measured top to bottom by two finished women — pre-emptively excludes her best friend from the room on the grounds that she wouldn't fit in it. First genuinely ugly thing Vee has done and the book let her do it in half a page and then let her feel *comfortably settled* about it. "Underneath it something turned over, small, and she let it lie." Good. That's the first time this book has let Vee be a little bit awful and I trust it more for it. *Goodbye, Vee* is starting to look less like an alarm bell and more like a girl who already knew.

The high table in the window again. Feet not reaching the floor again. "Set up on a shelf," "a thing the room could turn and look at" — same furniture as Chi Latte and I don't think that's the author running out of cafés, I think Randi seats her that way. Displayed. And then Brooke arrives and the display gets its audience, and the *we missed you Thursday* drops a whole private week on the table that Vee was never in, and Vee goes "very slightly the wrong size for the room," which is the best line in the chapter.

The tall one who never speaks is the thing I'm suspicious of. Everyone in this book gets a name — Sloane, Kinsey, Meg, Christine, the woman gets a name if she runs a restaurant — and this one gets a coffee, a slow easy circuit of Vee, and "the look you turn on a thing once you are done turning it over," and no name at all. Something was being *shown* to someone in that ninety seconds and Randi was the one holding it up. *We should all get dinner sometime.*

"Vee's my dear friend," and the cool damp hand taken on the white table in plain view. That's the turn, and it's genuinely lovely, and it cost Randi something or it cost her nothing and the chapter is careful not to tell me which — Vee is certain it cost something and Vee is the last person whose read I'd take. What I noticed: it does the same work the *dear friend* line does in the other direction. It doesn't just claim Vee in front of Brooke; it puts Vee somewhere she can only be kept by Randi. She walks home not feeling looked at but feeling *picked*, and "she had a place now, and the place was here, next to Randi." That's a girl who has just been given a seat at a table she'll do anything not to lose. Which is the point. Which is horrible.

One complaint, my usual one: the three sentences after the hand. "She did not know what it had cost Randi to say it. She knew only, without being able to say how, that it had cost something." The cool damp fingers closing on hers in front of those two women *already told me*. The book then explained the feeling it had just given me, and it does that twice in this chapter — the deep-water-and-the-boat line is doing the same job, and it's also cashing the pool chapter again. When this book trusts its own objects (the apple, the bottle cap, the hand) it's better than nearly anything on my shelf; when it gets nervous it annotates itself.

Also: "No bra is not an option for me. I'd black my own eye." I laughed out loud, and then noticed it's the only moment in eleven chapters where Vee's body is just a practical fact and not a verdict, and that it happens in a room full of girls who all got dressed in the same room, and that Randi laughed so hard she turned a head. Whatever else is being done to this girl, somebody made her funny out loud in public. Both things are true and the book keeps making me hold them.
