REACTION

Four pages and I'm sitting here with my hand over my mouth. The zipper. That's the whole chapter and it's one of the best small things this book has done. She can tell Cassie the dinner, she can tell her the apron and the garlic and the Chianti opened early, she can hand her *he's a good kisser* like a coin — and then her own body walks her into a corner the story can't cover, because the next inch of that zipper is evidence and evidence doesn't have a version. *It would be on the floor, and there would be nothing left to say.* She zipped it back up. She stood three feet from her best friend and re-dressed herself to protect a secret from a girl who wasn't even asking.

And she'd told herself all morning the withholding was a kindness, and the book lets her have that — *It was a kindness* — and then the zipper proves what it actually is, which is that she wants to keep it. It's hers. She's hoarding. I noticed the book couldn't resist saying it too (*she believed it the way you believe the thing that lets you keep what you want to keep*) right after the ladle had already done it perfectly, and after the zipper does it perfectly again a page later. Three passes at one idea. I don't need to be walked to the thing I'm already standing on.

The no-bag. God. She couldn't pack a bag because packing a bag would have been *saying it to herself, plainly* — so she drove out there with nothing, and woke with no brush and no face and her hair its own wild country, and laughed in his mirror. That's the most honest thing about her appetite in twenty-one chapters. She'd rather arrive unequipped than admit in advance what she was driving toward. And I note that the smile came back at every red light and she still gave Cassie *sweet* and *a good kisser* — the exact vocabulary she used to spend on the fine men. She's filing him in the old drawer on purpose, out loud, to keep the real file shut.

Cassie. *For half a second something crossed her face and was gone before it found a name.* That's the first time this book has given Cassie an inside and then refused to open it, and it's the only thing I've wanted for her. She clocked the toenails — in October, I have eyes — which is more than Randi ever got, and she got it from the floor with a cereal bowl on her knees and then *let it stand.* She didn't pull at the seam. Whether she felt it there, Vee couldn't have said. I think she felt it. I think she has felt it for weeks and has decided, with a generosity nobody in this book is going to notice, not to make her friend lie out loud.

And then she has a game. She has cleats and a bag and somewhere to be and *don't wait up, lover* — kindly, certainly, wrong, which is the most quietly devastating three-word list I've read in a while, because that's how everyone's going to be wrong about Vee from here on: kindly and certainly. But I'll take the cleats. The girl has a Saturday of her own.

What I'm sitting with is the last line. *She had never in her life been so happy.* Flat, unhedged, no irony in the sentence at all — and I'm holding the jacket in my other hand. She's bare under the skirt, the green is in a drawer in a house forty minutes away, her toenails are a color she still can't account for, and she's happy, and two people arranged this. I keep waiting for the book to flinch on her behalf and it won't, and that's why I'm still here.

What I want now is Randi. It's Saturday morning. Vee is going to want to tell somebody the middle, and there is exactly one woman in her life she's allowed to tell it to, and that woman said *you don't have to tell me* and meant *please don't.* Text me when she calls.
