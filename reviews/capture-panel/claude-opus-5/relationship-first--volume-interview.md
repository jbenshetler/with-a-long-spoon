**T1.**

*Vee.* The book leaves her under a quilt with her hand on a scar he hasn't explained, holding a word down under her tongue instead of saying it — which is the first time in twenty-seven chapters she's chosen to keep something on purpose rather than out of fear. That's where she is. How I feel is harder. Protective, and then immediately embarrassed about being protective, because that's exactly the response the book has been farming out of me since the quad, and I don't like being handled. She's the only one paying and she's the only one who's actually moved. The green satin folded in thirds and *trade*. Taking the underwear off so the line ran clean. Those aren't a girl being educated by a man, which is what I braced for at Gate 3 when he was narrating her reactions to her from inside his own head — those are somebody arriving at her own appetite and then spending it. So: fond, and uneasy about the fondness, and a little angry at how much the volume's pleasure depends on her not knowing. Not angry enough to have stopped. That's the honest number.

*Pace.* I like him and I don't trust the liking, and I've decided that's the book's doing and not my failure. Everything he does in a room is real — the pencil handed back at the exact line, the silk matched to her and not to the magazine, an hour with a tape measure and he never once looked down. But he's been outside that room the whole time too, and outside it he has never said Randi's name to Vee. He's a man who has substituted competence for disclosure and hasn't noticed, because competence keeps working. Chapter fourteen is where I stopped being able to like him cleanly: *her stomach is soft*, and no account of how he knows, and then she won't stay and he stands in the door not understanding and eats twice. The book put that crack in and walked away from it without pointing, which is the single best thing it did for my trust in it and the worst thing it did for my trust in him. Where he's left: warm, shaking, glad, and carrying an unsaid sentence he's converted into a pile of extremely good meals. I feel about him roughly what I feel about a person at a party who is genuinely the kindest one there and also the only one with a reason to be.

*Randi.* She's the one I can't put down. She came in as the operator — the bench, the thing behind her face policing the food, the cup going twice to her mouth without a sip, the lunches that were extractions dressed as friendship. By twenty-two I could see the whole apparatus running and it had gone grim on me. And then twenty-three: one line drawn through the top of the sauce, and she pours her own wine in that house for the first time, and *it's selfish, Pace*, true and unsayable, and he answers a charge nobody made. And then she eats. In his undershirt, feet up, everything on the plate, after twenty-five chapters of that withholding — and *she got past me before I could help it*, and the stillness when he describes Vee lighting a room. So what I feel is the thing I least wanted to feel about her, which is sympathy, arriving at exactly the moment I stopped trusting her with anything including herself. She is running a con she has fallen out the bottom of. I ache for her and I would not let her near anyone I loved.

(And Cassie, who isn't on your list: the zipper going half down and back up, the ladle out of the soup and the surface closing over. The loss I actually feel at the end of this book is that one, and nobody in it has noticed yet.)

**T2.**

I expect Book Two to be about the cost of the silence rather than the silence breaking — at least for a long while. I expect Randi to keep choosing Vee in small ways she can't explain to herself and to keep feeding on her at the same table, because nineteen established that the harvest and the tenderness are the same gesture. I expect Pace to be brought to the edge of the sentence two or three times and to hand over a meal instead. I expect Cassie to find out first, from the outside, and to say about nine words about it.

The one thing that would put me down: if the book keeps its secret by making Vee stupider than she's been for twenty-seven chapters. She has done the arithmetic on everything else in her life; the moment the plot requires her not to notice something a reasonably awake person would notice, the people have stopped moving the plot and the plot has started moving the people, and I'll set it down mid-scene and not come back. A near relative of that, same verdict: if she finds out by dropped phone, overheard call, misdelivered text — discovery happening *to* her instead of somebody choosing to speak or choosing not to. This book has been about people choosing what to hand over. Let a device do it and it's a different, lesser book.

**T3.**

Yes. Full price, day of, and I've already said so to my own notes — I didn't check the page count and I won't.

What I'm buying: forty pages of two people under a quilt talking about saffron and jet engines and coastlines. That's it, that's the purchase. Everything else is the frame that makes those pages cost something. Secondarily I'm buying the author's nerve — a fully dressed man with a tape measure as the hottest chapter in an explicit book is an argument, not an accident, and I want to see what else they think they can do. And I'm buying one specific question: does the book know what it did in nineteen? *You don't have to tell me*, said at the precise second the thing was about to be handed over — if that was engineered, this writer can do anything and I'll follow them for four volumes. If it was luck, Book Two will show me within eighty pages.

I am not buying the reveal. I want to be clear about that, because I think the marketing will assume I am.

The hypothetical — I'll take it. If Book Two ended and Vee still didn't know, whether I buy Three depends entirely on *why* she doesn't know, and that's not a dodge, it's the whole test. If the not-knowing has become the subject — if volume two is about what each of them is buying with their silence, and I can name the purchase for all three, and the prices have gone up — then yes, immediately, and that's a better book than the one where she finds out. Withholding as a structure is fine by me; I've loved books that ended on the question.

But it has a half-life, and the tell is whether the silence is still costing anyone anything. Two volumes of nobody saying it because saying it would end them — that's a novel. Three volumes of nobody saying it because the author needs the furniture where it is — that's a serial, and I'd know the difference by the end of Two, and I'd put it down without malice and go back to my own shelf.
