REACTION

Okay, I came in braced for Vee — the blurb's girl, the mark — and the book hands me Randi instead, on a bench, begging. That recalibration alone rearranged my whole head. The woman the jacket cast as one of the two *planners* is on her face in a man's furniture, being taken somewhere she did not plan to go, and she is twenty-one herself, with a mother who lives on egg whites and a sorority that weighs its girls at the door. So the game Vee is being walked into was built by two people, at least one of whom is herself mid-dismantling. That's a much more interesting book than the one I thought I bought, and I mean that as a compliment.

The consent scaffolding is meticulous and I clocked every piece of it, because I always do: the safeword asked for twice, "tell me again if you forget, I won't be upset," the check-ins at her face, the shoes taken off, the water left out in advance, the blanket, the not-interpreting. And the book *knows* the scaffolding isn't the whole story — that's the thing. Because the most important moment in the chapter is after all of it, when he says "you're beautiful" and she flinches, and then locks a bathroom door she's never locked, and stands at the mirror reassembling the face. The scene was fully consensual and she is leaving it more defended than she arrived. He thinks it was a remarkably good evening. She has decided, "quiet as a deadbolt going over," that the woman he found is never coming out again. The book sees both of those things at once and holds them without resolving them. That is exactly the awareness I read for.

And it's why the apple undid me a little. He slices it thin because she'll only eat thin. He has watched her police herself against two institutions of thinness and never once said anything about the counters *she* won't mention. The tenderness is real and it is also a scalpel. "All he had done was find it, and answer it" — that's Pace's narration of himself, and the book lets it sit there looking benign while the bench sits damp across the room "waiting for the next time." Chilling sentence. Intentionally chilling, I think — that object gets the last word of the chapter like a held breath.

The one thing I'm carrying warily: Pace's "small warm pleasure of having taken her further than he had thought she would go." There's an acquisitional note in it, and the book doesn't flag it — but the Daphne backstory (a door he didn't respect, a cost he learned from) tells me the book knows about that note too, and is letting him have it uncorrected *on purpose*, the way it lets him not-notice her lock the door. He's good. He may be the most careful man I've read in this genre. And careful men who are also keeping score of how far they've taken someone are the ones the blurb warned me about. "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." Yes. The chapter is that sentence, dramatized.

Also — the prose itself. The long sentences, the furniture-building exactitude, the sex built out of hands and waiting rather than acrobatics. The begging sequence earned its length; when the *please* finally changed registers I actually exhaled. And her line reading at the mirror — "The face was lying. The face was good at lying. The face had been lying for years" — I would text someone about that at 1 a.m. if anyone I know were awake.
