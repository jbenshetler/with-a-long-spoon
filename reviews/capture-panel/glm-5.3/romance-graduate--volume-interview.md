**T1 — Where the book leaves me with each of them**

**Vee.** Vee I feel tender toward, and slightly protective, the way you'd feel about a friend you watched walk into something with her eyes open but her map drawn by someone else. What saves her from being a victim-prototype for me is that the book gives her an actual inner life the *whole* way down — "the transcript was blank," the shame that runs the whole length of her wanting and never closes it. She's not a naive girl being fooled; she's a hungry woman being fed, and the book is honest that some of what's happening to her she'd have signed up for anyway. But she doesn't know the shape of what she's in. I love her, and I'm worried for her, and — this is the uncomfortable part — my worry is partly for who she'll be when she finds out. She's been telling Randi everything. Every lunch was a confession she thought was a friendship. So right now: enormous affection, and a low hum of dread on her behalf.

**Pace.** Pace is the most complicated feeling I've had about a hero in a long time, and I've had a lot of feelings about heroes. I trust his tenderness completely and his innocence not at all — I wrote that in my first journal and it held all thirty-one chapters. The courtly stuff is real. "May I choose," the water left out, the chin craned up so he wouldn't look at what she was giving him — none of that is performance, or it's performance that stopped being only performance. Chapter twenty-seven, the desk in the hallway, "Peter" said once into the dark — that's a man who showed me the actual scar tissue. So what I feel is genuine love tangled with genuine unease, and the book *refuses to sort those for me*, which is why I can't stop thinking about him. He's the best-written character in a book full of good ones. He's also a man who helped choose a girl on a quad like a purchase.

**Randi.** Randi unsettles me most and moves me most, some days. Every warmth she spends on Vee has an owner's hand under it — I stand by that — and the interrogation in chapter nineteen, Vee led down "one small yeah at a time," is the most skillful and most cold-blooded thing in the book. But chapter fourteen — wanting too big for her face — and the robe with no face on, "So you fix it," and "she got past me before I could help it." She's further gone than her own plan accounts for. She's the one running the con and the one the con is quietly being run *on*. I feel for her the way you feel for someone you can see is going to lose the thing they're most afraid of losing, and I don't fully trust a syllable out of her mouth. Both are true.

**T2 — What the book refused to give me**

Honestly? Less than you'd think, and what it withheld, it withheld on purpose, and it was right to. I wanted to know whether Pace *would* have told her eventually — "I want her to get there on her own" is doing sleight-of-hand, and I couldn't tell if it was respect or a connoisseur's patience, and the book wouldn't tell me. At first that frustrated me. Then I understood the frustration is the product. Same with the panty trade at the end of chapter twenty — I said in my record I don't fully understand it, and I still don't, and I've decided that's the best feeling a book can leave me with. A book that hands you everything is a book you're done with.

The two things I'd call actual debts — things I think Volume Two *owes* me, not just dangles: the reckoning and Cassie. The reckoning: Vee has to be allowed to be *furious* about the con, at full volume, without the narrative softening it into "but it was real love, so." I said in my verdict that if Book Two flinches from that, I'm out, and I meant it — the whole architecture is built for that detonation, and dodging it would curdle everything retroactively. And Cassie: she's the only clean ledger in the book, she said "Goodbye" flat back in chapter four and has been accruing moral authority every chapter since by *not asking*, and Vee keeps editing her down to the tidy version. Cassie is owed the truth, by Vee and by the book. If Volume Two sidelines her into the funny friend, that's a debt defaulted on.

Also, small and petty: I turned the page expecting fifty chapters and got the back cover at thirty-one, and I gasped out loud. I wanted *more book*. That's not a refusal, that's just me being greedy. I'll own it.

**T3 — Pace gets away clean, and does that change whether I buy Book Two?**

Here's the thing: I don't think he ends the book unexposed at all. He ends it unexposed *to Vee*. The book exposes him to *me* on roughly every third page, and it does it deliberately — that's the whole engine. I watched the pointing game from Randi's side. I saw the collision staged so it looked like the redhead walked into her. I know the courtship is real *and* designed, simultaneously, and the scariest and most honest thing this book does is refuse to sort those two apart. Pace deceiving Vee for thirty-one chapters and ending the volume loved and trusted isn't a flaw or a moral failure of the text — it's the *premise*. It's on the tin. The book never once pretended he was innocent; it just made me fall for him anyway, alongside her, and then let me catch myself doing it. That's not the genre betraying me. That's the genre doing the one thing I've been begging it to do: trust me enough to hold an ugly thing without flinching.

Where I'd get angry is if that were the *ending*. Volume One as a complete work where the con never costs anyone anything — yeah, I'd have a problem, and a big one. But it's not the ending, it's the *setup*. Nothing landing on him yet just means the debt is outstanding, and the book knows it — it keeps slipping its own knife in quietly, "I keep waiting to get bored of her and it doesn't come," the two of them cataloguing her in bed like collectors. The author is keeping meticulous books. Pace doesn't get away with anything; he gets a very expensive tab run up in his name.

Does it change whether I buy Book Two? No — it's the *reason* I'm buying Book Two, at full price, on the mailing list, first time in two years. But I'll tell you exactly what my line is, because I wrote it down before I finished: the payoff has to cost what it's been promising. Vee finds out, and she gets to be as furious as a woman with that much interiority has earned the right to be, and the book doesn't fold that fury into the romance within two chapters. If Book Two handles the reveal with the same manipulative architecture and frames it as pure romance — if her anger gets groomed away the way everything else got groomed — I'll put it down mid-scene, and I'll be bitter about it, because this is the best thing I've read in the genre in years. But "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." A book that can write that line knows exactly what it owes me. I'm betting it pays.
