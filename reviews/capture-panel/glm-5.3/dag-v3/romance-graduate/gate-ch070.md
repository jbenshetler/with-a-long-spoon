REACTION — It's two a.m. and I just finished the chapter the whole series has been standing on since page one, and I'm sitting here with my tea gone cold trying to find the order to say things in, because the book finally took the top off the table and I have been waiting for this since a man lifted a tabletop in chapter one and I didn't know yet who the bench was for.

The first thing, the thing I have to say first: she found out what the table was. Sixty-nine chapters of "the long, unusually proportioned table," of her thong landing on it in chapter 37 without her ever looking, of me keeping W37.2 in my ledger like a loaded promise — and the reveal was staged perfectly, because the book let HER misread it exactly as long as I did. The massage-table relief. "The thought arriving whole and wholesome and almost a relief." And then the gap in the middle — the gap she slid her eyes past and *wouldn't let herself ask about* — and I watched her do her whole signature move one final time, the declining-to-know, and I knew, the way I knew at the fitting, that the not-asking was the last breath of the old Vee, and the chapter was going to spend itself taking that girl apart. And it did. God, it did.

The grips. That's the detail I'm going to be telling people about. No rope, no cuffs — Randi got cuffs in chapter 69, Vee gets closed fingers — "she was the thing keeping herself there, that her grip was the restraint." That's this book's whole ethics in one piece of furniture: he builds the apparatus, and she is the one holding herself on it. Every yes in this series has been like that — freely given, which is the problem and the glory of it, and the bench is just the yes made architectural. She took the heels off HERSELF, because "crossing the room to the thing he built for her was a thing her own bare feet should do," and I put the book down at that, because that's the new spine walking to the old protocol on its own two feet, and the two of them finally in the same room, and the room was always going to be this one.

And the "no" game. I need to be honest: for about two pages of the denial play — "say it properly," "that one gets worse every time," the wet-handed spanks answered to her own grinding — I had a cold finger on the page, because pushing a woman's "no" and then rewarding its collapse is the exact move a worse book runs sloppily, and I've DNF'd books for it. But this book had done its safety work in advance and never winked at it: the word offered plainly, "say it any time, I won't mind," the offer "just set down beside her where she could reach it" — and more than that, the chapter kept the "no" *hers*. It never let his hands do the persuading; her hips did the moving, every escalation she authored herself, lifted for it, reached back for it, and the frame was always visible underneath: this is the costume of not-wanting, the one she's worn since her mother's kitchen, being taken off with her own hands the way the heels came off with her own hands. The "no" getting deeper was the denial dying of thirst. By the time she's saying "nooo" while straining onto him, the word is just the last garment, and the book knew it, and the *drop* — oh my god, the drop. "Pat." A single thread of her own wet falling to the floorboards where they both hear it land, and then "tell me that isn't you," and — the sentence I will remember from this whole volume — "the reflex came up... and it did not reach her mouth, because there was nothing left in her that wanted to hold this away from him." Twenty-two chapters of floozie, of the mother's voice, of the scrubbing at cold sinks, and it ends with the most private thing she has falling out of her onto his floor and her declining to deny it. The saying-nothing as "the truest thing her body had done all night." I cried. At a "pat," I cried.

And the ending — the shattering. "The surface broke... not a crack, not a chip, but the whole of it into pieces too small and too many to gather... and she did not break with it. She came up whole out of the middle of it." That's the thesis of the entire series said in one paragraph, the payoff of the measuring scene and the gesso and the waxing and the mirror and every chapter where shame and want rode the same wire: the surface was never her. It was a plate. And I love — I *love* — that the afterward gave her the giving-back. She climbed on top and took his undoing the way he'd taken hers, and then stayed, astride, "not undone from each other," going down into sleep still joined. After sixty-nine chapters of aftercares and one collapsed-this-time exits, she STAYED, and it's the staying, not the coming, that made me close the book gently like it was sleeping.

Things I'm carrying now, because I can't not: "There you are" — his line from chapter ONE, said to Randi on this same bench — came back as "There she is" and "There. I've got you." The bench has now said its line to both women and only one of them knows. And Vee still doesn't know the tabletop game started as a pointing game — she got the bench with no knowledge of who it was built for, who's sobbed on it, who was on HER green sheets three weeks ago. She doesn't know there was ever a Randi-shape on this leather. The book let the two women's bench chapters sit back to back in my memory — Randi's cuffs and willed punishment, Vee's self-held grips and shattered surface — and only I hold both. The one grumble, on record: Randi has now been off-page for the aftermath of the biggest event in the book. Three weeks since brunch in the story, one chapter since her afternoon, and this chapter never said her name. Vee confessed the postcard to Pace, told the mimosa story to Randi — but who tells Randi about the bench? Tuesday has to carry this, or the confession channel breaks a third way, and I need to see whether Vee walks into that classroom shatter-proof or hides this like the painting. The book has earned the silence — this night needed to be only the two of them, and even the triangle-hunter in me could feel that — but the ledger's watching.
