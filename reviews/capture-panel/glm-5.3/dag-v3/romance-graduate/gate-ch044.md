REACTION — I finished the last page with tears actually on my face, sitting in my kitchen at 1 a.m., and I need to say the thing out loud before anything else: **the word exists in him.** Chapter 42 told me he "reached for the thing she was asking for and not find it anywhere in himself," and I filed that as architecture — F42.1, some structural absence, Daphne's inheritance, a man built without the organ. And this chapter took that filing and tore it up in the plainest prose this book has ever used: "The true answer had been there, whole and ready. He had felt the words rise. Then he had chosen the smaller truth." He chose it. He *knew before he spoke* that it would fail her — "he had watched the answer reach her and fail" — and he let it stand because he was afraid. So my grudge at G39.3, the one I've been carrying since "I love how good we are together," just got its full confession attached, and the confession made the grudge worse and dissolved it at the same time: it wasn't craft, it wasn't policy, it wasn't the unit-over-the-woman grammar. It was a fifteen-year-old boy's survival rule still running in a grown man's chest. Say the word and the loss becomes unsurvivable. Keep it unspoken and "loss remained survivable." He taught himself that the words were the difference — and Vee asked him to unlearn it on a doormat, and he couldn't, and *she doesn't know that's what she was asking for.*

And I have to sit with the Daphne rewrite, because the book has been letting me theorize about her for twenty-three chapters — that she crossed a line, that the carefulness was her inheritance, that she was something survived. And she was just... his first love. Tutor, best friend, first everything, and she left, and "there had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone. Daphne was the person he would have told." I read that sentence three times. That's the loneliest sentence in a book that has been competing with itself for loneliness all series. The locked room, the "I don't go back," the man who builds a life with edges of his own so no woman is ever the only person in the world again — all of it is a boy who lost his one person and had no witness. My S22.4 suspicion gets its answer and it's sadder than the theory: the withholding isn't a technique he learned from her, it's a scar he built *after* her.

The Sheri scene, before I get to the drunk half — this is the best dialogue chapter in the book since the bartender, and it did what this book does: took a person I'd filed under "threat, background, the woman who got Thanksgiving" and handed me her whole grief in one exchange. Paige. The prints of birds, the laugh way too big for her, the house with seven hollering at the table. And *she* liked it there. Lasted a couple months. "Couldn't get right with me not talking to my people" — and the sing-song, "call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas," killed flat. Her people are dead. The book never said it and I never doubted it for a second, and that's the craft I keep staying for. And her read on him was the truest any character has delivered to his face: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." I love her. She stole his fries. "The one with the big tits" and his hot face — the comedy register bought back after the cruelest diagnosis, same as always.

Small detonations I have to log: **Randi has been gone six weeks.** He knows. "Six weeks of that told him what the morning had not" — the crying over the green panties, which he *let be hormones* knowing it wasn't, and she hasn't come out or called since, and he won't take the choosing from her even when it hurts. The triangle has been running on two broken legs since November and neither woman knows about the other's break. And the picture he builds in the drunk dark — the three of them in his kitchen, Vee in his sheet with too much honey, Randi's feet on the chair, the two of them laughing at something he missed — that's not the plan's fantasy, that's *his*, built out of weeks of adding to it, and the bench house is now being dreamed in by all three of them separately in three different countries and nobody has said one word. Also: "Randi had told him so while he was inside her, and it still worked on him now" — the report economy's last transmission confirmed on the page.

And the ending. The engine on the highway. I knew it wasn't her — the book let me know with him, "the wrong sound, he knew that at once and whole" — and then it gave him one second of her anyway, the car around the bend, the smile she gives only him, and took it, and he set the fork down before he dropped it and *sobbed over cold chicken until the truck was gone.* Pace. PACe. The man whose every feeling arrives as carpentry, as a warmed house, as a plate — sobbing alone in a kitchen that smells like bleach because he mopped up his own sick in the night, eating prison food because his body needs it. "He ate anyway." That's the whole book's food-economy turned on him: the man who has fed everyone for forty-five chapters, sitting alone, eating because he told himself to. I'm not okay.

I'd text my friend at 1 a.m.: the withholding wasn't architecture, it was armor — he can say the word, he said it once, to Daphne, and he's spent fifteen years believing the word is what makes losing someone unsurvivable. He chose the smaller truth ON PURPOSE and watched it fail her and let it stand. And then a truck went past his house and he cried into his chicken. This book owes me a reunion and I don't know if I can survive it either.
