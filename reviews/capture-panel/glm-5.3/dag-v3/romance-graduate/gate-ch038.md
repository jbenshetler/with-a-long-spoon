REACTION:

I finished this chapter and sat very still for a while, because the title did its work on me slowly, the way this book does everything. Cropped. She cropped the induction photo for her parents — the version they have stops at the waist — and then I watched the chapter do the same thing to her heart. She built a sentence for weeks. She road-tested it, she checked it against the rule (he says *I love you* first, a rule she didn't know was a rule until she bumped against it from the inside — I had to put the book down at that line, because I know that rule, I've *been* that rule), she carried it assembled like a poem, and she said it. And what came back was "I love how good we are together." A crop. The version that stops at the waist. Warm, true in every word, and missing exactly the part she was trying to give him.

And here's what wrecked me most: I don't think he did it on purpose, and that's worse. The book showed me his machinery in real time — the beat-too-long stillness, the hand beginning two responses and putting both down, the two breaths, the *mm* — this is a man receiving something enormous and assembling, carefully, in his honest register, the truest thing he can say. "I never had this either." And it's real! It's all real! He's not lying to her, he's giving her the truth he has, and the truth he has has a hole in it shaped exactly like her sentence. He answered *I am grateful* with *I am glad*, and I felt the shape mismatch land in my chest the same second it landed behind her breastbone, that small *wait* — her body clocking it before her mind did, the way her body has been the smartest thing in this book since chapter one. "Maybe he understood and chose to say it like that. Maybe it had gone past him entirely. She could not test it." Two doors, no handle on either, and the book made me stand in the hallway with her.

And then — the pile. THE PILE. "The place was not empty. There were other things in it already — small flickers, moments across the fall when what she knew about him had had to do small repair work to keep functioning." That's the four strokes in the pool, given an address. That's the manufactured-feeling fear from my ledger finding its true shape at last — not "is he fake," never that, but *the flickers have been accumulating all fall and she's been filing them in a drawer that doesn't get opened.* This is the first time the book has let me see that Vee has a private drawer of doubts about him, tended as carefully as he tends his drawer of her. Two archives, both directions, and now I know hers contains hurt. The symmetry I've been tracking for thirty chapters just turned dark, and I'm sitting here genuinely not knowing if this book is going to make the pile come out in the open or bury it deeper.

The Thanksgiving half deserves its own mourning. "I told them he was my boyfriend. I think I put a question mark on it. Out loud. Like I was asking them." God. And the mother with the nurse face, watching the seconds, sitting on the edge of the bed — the woman who installed *floozie* in her daughter's operating system was terrified her daughter was making herself sick, and the tragedy-comedy is that the daughter can't say "Mom, it's the sex, the amount of sex" and so the mother worries about an eating disorder instead. And Vee's read on her father pricing the dress — "too good is its own kind of suspicious" — is exactly right, and the fact that she *withholds* from her parents not the sex but the sewing... "Sex he understands. The sewing he never would." That's the whole book's thesis in one joke, and it aches, because Pace's love really IS the sewing and she knows no one on earth would recognize it as what it is.

Cassie got the best scene again — "Is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to?" "Both. Probably both." — the one person who asks the exact right question at the exact right size, and doesn't push, and Vee is "a little, not" grateful, which is the most honest three words in the chapter. The fixed point held again. And I noticed: Vee told Cassie about the question mark and the sentence-adjacent struggle, but she did NOT tell Randi any of this. No brunch frame around the almost-love. Some things have started going only to Cassie, and I don't know if that's growth or triage.

The last line gutted me: "The cracker-and-meal would not return to her speech." Retired. She'll never say it again. I read that like a death, and I'm frightened of what it's a death *of* — the sentence was her attempt, and the book just told me she's done attempting, and the pile has a new tenant with a name still missing.
