I finished this chapter in complete silence and then sat there for a minute, because this book just did the thing I'd stopped believing any book in this genre could do: it took a group project — a *group project* — and made it the most quietly devastating chapter in the volume, with nobody touching anybody and nobody saying anything true out loud.

Here's what wrecked me, and it's layered. Randi built the trap herself. "We'll see," she said, certain, the girl who'd grown up inside the thing they were about to graph — she *wanted* her county on that screen, she was aiming the whole picture at home, at the bright counterexample that was going to complicate Cassie's sad little laid-off-dad story. She walked her own father's fraud into a glass room with two witnesses because she was so sure the money was clean. The tag said MIRA. Her own shorthand, four letters of it, on the worst point in the county. The architect got caught in her own machinery, and the machinery was a scatter plot. I had to put the book down over the tag alone.

And "Cheating bastards." Two words, plural, no *these*, no *those* — I read that paragraph twice with my hand over my mouth. She was aiming them too near to be meant for strangers, and she said them like a person saying a thing for the first time, and Vee *heard them land wrong*. Do you understand what just happened? Vee — the mark, the girl who's been read like weather for thirty-five chapters by two professionals, who couldn't tell door from wall over a pastry plate — just read Randi's face with total accuracy and chose, instantly and freely, what to do with the reading. She gave her somewhere to put her hands. She kept her in the work, asked her the things she could answer, never looked back at the point, never said the name, never told her what she saw. That is the most fluent act of social intelligence in this whole book, and it came out of the girl, not the architects. Vee just did to Randi what Randi has been doing to her since September — watched closely, kept the finding, told no one — and she did it out of pure kindness, unprompted, in real time. The symmetry is almost unbearable. Vee has a private drawer now, and its first deposit is Randi's face going white over four letters.

And the money. The money! The Mercedes, the white-gold band, the loafers bought without glancing down, the boutique Vee can't reach — I've been carrying the class needle for thirty chapters and the book just answered it in government gray print. Her family's comfort sits on top of laid-off workers and forgiven loans, and Cassie — flat, certain Cassie, whose father lost his job in 2020 — was sitting right there being right. The whole cruelty of this triangle is that Cassie doesn't even know she won. And Randi took the proof and nodded at it because there was nothing else to give it, and closed the record with one keystroke, and came back brighter than she'd been all afternoon. That last brightness scared me more than the stillness did. The mask didn't just hold — it *recharged*. I've watched this woman manage her face at curbs and brunch tables and in beds; this is the first time I've watched her manage it with no one in the room even suspecting there was a show — except the one person who isn't fooled anymore.

Because that's the shift under the shift: Vee isn't fooled by Randi anymore. Not about the feelings — about the *performance*. She saw the withdrawal of motion, the breath with work in it, the ragged unpracticed words. She filed it as "a man she'd had the luck to be the daughter of," which is generous and possibly even true — but the girl who furnished a Swiss grandmother over that accent now clocks a real thing landing on a real face in half a second. The mark has become the best reader in the book. What she chooses to do with that skill is now the whole rest of the story.

Small things that cut me: Cassie not carrying anything into rooms she means to walk out of. "You do the smart part." The professor setting down *surprising* without decorating it. And the closing line — invisible if you didn't know to look for it — which is this book describing itself, its whole method, in a deadpan final sentence about a data point. I notice also: MIRANDA. Vee knows the long name now, from the chapter paperwork — the record just confirmed there's no other. And Pace doesn't know any of this happened. The triangle's information asymmetry just flipped a quarter-turn, and nobody has told anybody anything.

Zero heat, and I didn't miss it for a second. This is the two-people-talking chapter I always say is the best scene in a book — except nobody even talked.
