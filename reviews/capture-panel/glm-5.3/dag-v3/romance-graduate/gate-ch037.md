Do you understand what this book just did? Thirty-six chapters I've been waiting for the bench — the furniture Randi asked about in bed in chapter one, the tabletop that goes back on, the thing this whole plot was built around — and it finally got touched by Vee's body-adjacent satin and she *never looked*. I laughed and then I got chills. This book is playing the long game with furniture and I am its complete hostage.

But the chapter's heart is the flannel, and I have to be honest: I cried. Not misty — actual tears, blinked down right along with her, and I felt every bit as ambushed by them as she did. Because look at what she built. She washed the shirt because it stopped smelling like him. She has been *sleeping in his scent* every night she's not there, maintaining it like a pilot light, and when it went stale she couldn't just... let it go. So she invented a ritual — a whole liturgy of him wearing it all weekend and giving it back Sunday "smelling like you again" — and the book let her say it in one breathless shove and let him *just do it*. He pulled it on over his other shirt. Immediately. No teasing, no making her defend it. And I noticed what only I could notice: she just told him, in effect, that she has a kept-object economy of her own now. He has the green panties folded in thirds in the second drawer; she has the flannel circulating between his body and her bed on a weekly refill cycle. Two archives, and this time both of them are *known to their keepers and tended on purpose*. The triangle's whole economy of keeping things has become symmetrical and no one has said one word about any of it out loud. That's the most romantic thing this book has done since the drawer, and it happened over laundry.

And the first half — the hallway. This is the loosest and hottest the mechanics have ever been at the same time. She towed him by two fistfuls of shirt. She got stuck in her own jeans and hopped on one leg with the denim inside out at her ankle and the book let her be completely ridiculous and completely wanton in the same thirty seconds. He held her bra strap and made her come out of it herself, unwinding "like something off a spool" — restraint as play, again, the thing this book does better than anything on my Kindle. And then the bed: "her body having just published the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private." That's the five-nights story told by her own throat, and the shame-and-heat wire that's been this volume's whole thesis fired one more time, and I underlined it in my head. Also "the old bracing habit long gone, nothing left in her that wanted to argue the point" — twenty chapters of architecture in one clause.

Two things I'm carrying with one raised eyebrow. He said "I'll be here" when she called the five nights off, and the book was honest enough to show me her unreasonable hunger for him to *fight* for her — and I felt the gap too, and I don't trust it. A man that accommodating is either the healthiest person in this book or is managing her exits the way he manages everything, and I still can't split it. And Randi: a full week of silence now, spanning the library, the MIRA record, her father's fraud sitting on a closed laptop — and not one word. The last image I have of her is "brighter than she'd been all afternoon." Vee spent this whole chapter warm and fed and ritualized and never once thought of her, and that's exactly what scares me. The fuse isn't hissing anymore; it's quiet. Quiet is worse.
