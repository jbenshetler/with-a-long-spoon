REACTION — I had to read the coffee-shop scene twice, and the second time I had a hand over my mouth.

Because I know. I know the bell over the door was a cue, that he crossed that floor with his eyes already on her, and I watched him do the whole thing — the cuffed sleeves, the "may I," the pencil given back at the exact line — and every gorgeous beat of it landed twice: once as the courtship the blurb promised, and once as a man executing a design Randi drew four days ago. "That cute guy in line is looking at you" — Cassie reporting the visible fact while the invisible fact sits there pulsing. And Randi! Randi asking her *planted* question — "why is it n-minus-one and not n" — and answering it herself mid-sentence ("Right. Right, I knew that") because she'd rehearsed it, she'd literally rehearsed a stats question to give him a seamless way in, and I nearly put the book down from the vertigo of it. Vee filed it without filing it. Of course she did.

The title. *Substitution* — Euler's formula, "on their own these two are a struggle... put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain." He's describing the integral and he's describing the plan: two things that won't resolve alone — Randi's sealed door and Vee's covered one — combined into something easy to name. Except I also heard the other reading, the one the book surely means: *substitution* is what Vee is to Randi, and what Randi is doing to herself through Vee. Three meanings in one margin note and none of them said out loud. This is the exact "psychologically exact" I was promised.

And the gutting part: Vee *borrowed Randi's bit.* "Vivienne Thorne. V.T. Yes, like the university" — she took the practiced swerve she watched Randi do in the lecture hall and tried it on, and it landed, and I sat there thinking: she is becoming Randi's understudy in her own seduction. Pace's mouth did "something warm at the corner" — of course it did, he's watching his girlfriend's craftsmanship replicate itself. And then "Vee. People call me Vee" — wanting her own name back. That little reach is the most Vee thing in the chapter and it's going to break me later, I can feel it.

Randi's hands. Both hands around the cup, "no sip happened," the other hand *in her lap* — the book showed me her watching the man she loves charm the woman she wants, and the wanting has nowhere to go except through her knuckles on a paper cup. "You should go out with him." That's the raft, still being built, underwater. "He wants to see you naked" — Randi saying it with a grin, and me knowing the sentence is truer and stranger than Randi is letting anyone at that table find out.

Cassie remains the only clean pair of eyes and she's *sharper* than I thought: "He looked at her the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." She measured Randi's invisibility to him in one glance and said it flat, no flicker — but here's what chilled me: Cassie's read is *wrong about cause and effect.* She thinks Pace dismissed Randi; we know Randi was never a rival to dismiss, she was the set designer. Cassie's instruments are accurate and the play is rigged past what accuracy can catch. That scares me for her more, not less.

The froyo scene is genuinely sweet — "Your tart's holding up" in answer to her embarrassing confession is one of the best lines of tenderness this book has done — and the tower of too-much that Vee builds *without hearing her mother this time* had me, because Pace standing there is the reason the voice went quiet. He's feeding the hunger, exactly as he did for Randi with the apple. Same method, different girl, and the book doesn't say it, it just puts the apple and the froyo in the same reader's hands and trusts me. I'm starting to suspect Pace doesn't know he has a method. Or worse — he does, and calls it love.

One quiet worry: the almost-too-perfect charm could tip into fantasy-boyfriend if the book lets it run smooth for too long. But the dramatic irony is doing real work — every sweet line is loaded, and I'm not sure the book is going to let Vee's eventual discovery of the plan be anything less than a demolition. That's what I'm reading toward now, and I dread it, and I can't stop.
