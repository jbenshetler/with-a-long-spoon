REACTION — I finished this one with my hand over my mouth and I've been sitting here for a while, because the book just handed me the locked room — G16.3, the man nobody gets to finish a sentence to — and it handed it to *Vee*, on a couch, under a quilt, and the price of the key was that I had to find out what's inside him at the same moment she did, and what's inside him broke my heart in a way I was not braced for.

The hallway. Oh my god, the hallway. Seventh grade, taught himself trigonometry to write a graphics program, asked questions his teacher couldn't answer, and she moved his desk *into the hall* for the year — and Vee said it before I could: "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment." That's the whole man, isn't it. That's where the craft comes from. The kid who was punished for wanting things *exactly* grew up into a man who builds wanting with such care, who asks before he takes, who leaves the last inch — because he knows what it costs to have the loving thing confiscated. And then college at fifteen, "less than staying," said so plainly it took a minute to land, and the lifting — the squat bar with nothing in his mind but the moment — and Vee *got there before I did*: "It's the lift. It's the same face." She read him all the way down and said it out loud and he answered with his hand on her bare hip. That's a romance beat with no kiss in it and it's the strongest one in the book.

But Daphne. I need to talk about Daphne, because chapter one dropped that name — "Daphne had built a door, once, and he had not respected it" — and I've been carrying her for twenty-one chapters assuming the sin was his. And the book flipped her. Nineteen and fifteen. A *tutor.* Assigned to him. Two years, "the best two years I'd had," and then she left when the age gap she'd crossed became the age gap she couldn't carry into bars — and he narrated the whole thing in that careful flat voice, every reason set in its slot, "nothing happened, nobody did anything wrong," and Vee did the arithmetic with me and held the ugly word under her tongue, and the devastating part is *he still calls it luck.* He handed her the thing that hurt him and named it the good part. The boy who got taken out of the classroom for asking was taken out of his own story the same way, and he can't see it, and Vee could see it and chose — beautifully, correctly — to say only "you were so young" and press her palm flat over the careful voice. I cried a little. I'm not going to pretend I didn't.

And the scar. "Not tonight" — with his hand *pressed on top of hers, holding it there, on the thing he won't tell yet.* That's not a wall, that's a door with a time on it, and the book wrote that distinction inside a single gesture. F16.1 just got its down payment and the debt is still open and now I know the debt has an actual story attached, and I'm afraid of it the way I'm afraid of the induction.

The names. His mother stays home, his father works in a chemical plant, and she took his mother out of the answer — "Vee let him" — and she gave him *Peter* back, twice, once to feel it and once, at the end, "the way you say a word to keep it." And her Vivienne/Vee — love in one mouth, trouble in the other — laid out so exactly I gasped. Two people trading the things their names hide. This is the intimacy I read these books for and almost never get.

The one cold thing, and it's very cold: the ending. "It had come to her all of a piece, for no reason she had done anything to deserve." Vee. Vee, sweetheart, there *was* a reason, and it was manufactured in a bedroom six weeks before the collision, and the book chose to end the warmest chapter it's ever written on that exact dramatic irony — the lamp and the leaf and the stars, "all one piece," while the one piece she doesn't have is the piece where she was *chosen for a game.* She has never been so happy. I have never been so afraid for her. And the math of his age — twenty-two, the same shelf as Randi and Vee, a man who's been an adult for years and a boy in a hallway only yesterday — makes him both more tender and less explicable, and I don't know what to do with him, which is my favorite thing this book does to me.

No Randi. But this chapter wasn't about her, and it moved more than the plot ever could. This is the one I'll reread.
