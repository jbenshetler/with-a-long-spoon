**REACTION — Chapter 27, "Rock"**

I had to put the book down at "She was nineteen" and just sit there for a minute. I've been carrying Daphne around since chapter 14 like a stone in my pocket — one clause, loaded gun, my own note — and the book finally handed her to me at eleven at night on a couch made of quilt and firelight, which is exactly where it knew I'd be undefended.

And here's the thing: I'd been imagining a girlfriend. Some campus girl, some wrong turn. Instead we got a nineteen-year-old *assigned* to a fifteen-year-old — "to help the child find his feet" — and I watched Vee do the arithmetic in real time, "the clean ugly grown-up word" starting to form and held down under her tongue, and I wanted to reach into the page and make her say it. She got all the way to the rim. "You were so young," and the rest behind her teeth. That's Vee, that's been Vee since the pantyhose — she knows the true size of things and chooses the kindness-sized version. And Rivers writes it so I can't even blame her. But oh, I felt the shape of that withheld word from my side of the glass.

And then — I'm sorry, I have to say this out loud to somebody — *the plan*. The jacket told me in the store: an invitation that began as a game between two people. And now I know where Pace learned love. A brilliant, lonely fifteen-year-old, desk in the hall for the crime of loving something too well, and the first person who ever chose him was older, was *placed*, arranged the world around him "so the opportunity shouldn't be wasted." Somebody structured his awakening for him. Selected him. Built him a curriculum and called it care. He's not re-enacting Daphne on Vee — he's doing what he knows *as* love, which is so much worse and so much more tender. He believes it. "He had handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck." Vee saw that sentence and I underlined it with my thumb. He doesn't hear himself defending it because he has no word for what happened to him either. And Randi — Randi, who "was assigned" to Vee by nobody, who steered her "lightly, laughing, as if by accident" — is standing in the Daphne position and has no more idea than Daphne did, or maybe more. I've been reading Randi as the pot boiling. Now I'm reading the whole triangle as a rhyme, and I don't know if Rivers did it on purpose, and honestly I don't care, because it's true either way.

The scar. He *kept her hand on it.* "Not tonight" — "a door with a time on it that wasn't now." This book keeps doing that, keeps giving me withholding that isn't a wall, and I keep falling for it, and I noticed this time that I'm being trained exactly the way Vee is. Say it more than once so the noise can't kill it — that's still the skeleton key. He said the math is the language the world is written in, and I sat there with her looking at stars that stopped being decoration. The paint-in-not-paint-out shape. I don't care if it's real. It's real because he lit up saying it, and this whole book has taught me to read men by what lights them up.

The small stuff: "Peter." I said it twice with her. I love that his real name is the flattest name in the world — my mother had a jar labeled saffron, he has a name labeled Peter. And her mother calling Vivienne for trouble — "the name that means love in one mouth means trouble in the other" — he just lays those down where she can see them, that's his whole way of touching her with his hands full. The desk in the hall. "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment," and she put her shoulder against his arm instead of saying sorry, and that might be the most intimate gesture in the book so far, more than the standing-up against the wall with the dress. And the lifting — "it's the lift, it's the same face" — she *got* him, she put that together in her body before her head, and the chapter let her have it.

And then the ending. The ending. "It had come to her all of a piece, for no reason she had done anything to deserve. She had never been so fortunate, or so happy." I read that with my jaw tight, because I know — I've known since the back cover — that there's a reason, and it has a name, and it isn't luck, and it's two people, and one of them is asleep under her arm and one of them is across town eating her dinner alone with a bowl in the refrigerator that she covered with a plate. The book keeps doing this to me: Vee narrates her luck and I hold the receipt. "Getting away with something," she thought about the dress. She has no idea what she's getting away with, or that someone rigged it.

No Randi this chapter, and I missed her like a missing tooth — which I think is the point, she's the cold on the other side of the glass now. This was the kitchen-talk chapter I came to this book for, forty minutes of two people under a quilt, and it happened to also be the chapter where the whole floor plan of Pace got drawn. I texted my friend at 12:40: "the tutor was nineteen and I need to lie down."
