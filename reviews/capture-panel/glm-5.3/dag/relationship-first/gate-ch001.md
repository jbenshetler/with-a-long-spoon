Wait. Wait wait wait. "There you are, Randi."

I had to put the book down for a minute. The whole blurb — the girl being steered into Pace's path, Randi the glamorous friend who draws her out — and then chapter one opens mid-affair, kitchen, apple, bench, and I spent the whole scene assuming this was Vee. The sorority, the blue eyes, the face — I was building Vee in my head the entire time. And then the name lands like a slap. This isn't the seduction of the girl; this is the *before*. This is the game the two of them were already playing before Vee ever walked in. Or — and this is what's got me slightly cold, in the good way — it's Randi already in over her head, already having the real thing happen to her, which means whatever plan she and Pace made about Vee is going to be built on top of *this*. A woman who just decided, in a bathroom mirror, that the one he found is never coming out again — that woman goes and helps recruit a college girl for her boyfriend. God.

And the bathroom scene is the best writing in the chapter. The smile she practices, the smile that "sat on top of the face that had begged" — that's exactly the disjunction I read for. The whole passage about the face as a cost-free surface, the being-looked-at that never gets past the face — that's a psychology, that's a real person with a real architecture, not a genre body. And then the mirror scene ends with her *choosing the mask*, and him in the bedroom thinking it was a remarkably good evening, thinking he found her hunger and answered it — and he has no idea she just sealed the door. He respected the bathroom door because of Daphne, and he's proud of himself for it, and she used that respect to reassemble the lie. That's merciless. I love it.

The sex itself: hot, yes — the apple scene on his lap, the slow hands, the edging — but what actually got me was the *please*. The way he waits through the polite please, the waiter please, until the real one comes from below. That's the erotic center and it's psychological, exactly as promised. And him staying hard all night and setting it aside — "that's not what this night was for" — that's a choice the book made and it told me something about him. Controlling. Interesting-controlling, but controlling. The water bottle cap under her thumbnail that she doesn't know she's doing — that little detail is doing so much work. She punishes herself with edges of things. I clocked that immediately and I don't think Pace did.

What I'm suspicious of: him. The narration lives mostly behind his eyes, and his eyes are benevolent, patient, exact — and he's a man who builds furniture with a secret purpose and doesn't correct you when you call it a table. "You said it was a table. I didn't correct you." That's charming on the page and it's also the whole ethics of this book in one line. I don't trust the narration's warmth toward him yet, and I think I'm not supposed to, quite.

One flag: the prose occasionally tells me twice. "The settling was good." "The leaning was genuine." Sentences vouching for moments that had already carried themselves. Small thing, once a chapter I'll forgive.

But Randi. Randi is the reason I'm turning the page. Not Vee — I haven't met Vee. I want to watch a woman walk out of a bathroom with a rebuilt face and go find something to do with the fact that she was seen.
