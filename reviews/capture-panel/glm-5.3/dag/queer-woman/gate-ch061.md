REACTION — Well, there goes my chest again. This book keeps doing the thing where it hands you a domestic comedy morning — hangover, melted ice, cinnamon raisin bread, the paper towel under the sweating glass so it won't mark the wood, God — and then pulls the floor out one plank at a time while you're chewing.

"Women misunderstand it. The friendship. It gets awkward." I actually sat back at that line, because I know that shape from the inside, both sides of it, and I've never once seen a man in this genre say it out loud, flat, without self-pity. And then the book does the cruel-tender thing it does: Vee finishes the missing word for him — *his* friendship, it was never his face — and I recognized myself in Theo-from-the-other-side too, the season she held the line at a careful friend's distance. That's a truth about how it goes with women who pay attention like that, and the book spent it instead of hoarding it, and I'm grateful.

But the thing that got me was the arithmetic. Not one room, not one hour, with one person his own age. A seminar of eight, teaching himself to cook because there was no dining hall and no one to eat with. The empty campus at Thanksgiving that Vee set down before it could become pity. And Daphne — God, Daphne. The one doing the work of all four, in a building of grown men, and Vee holding the two truths stacked and unreconciling: the best two years he'd had, and the wrong shape underneath, the ugly grown-up word she keeps pressing back down. "He set it in good order and the good order was the tell." Yes. That's the whole book's method in one sentence, and Vee has learned it from him.

And me, with my notebook full of suspicions: the chapter opened a locked door and I walked through it willingly. A boy who was "not safe" at fifteen, home gone at sixteen, handed an adult life before the growing — that's the manufacturing floor for a man who plans a girl's year like a proof. Not an excuse. A floor. I've been asking whether the design question beats the feeling question and this chapter quietly said: they come from the same childhood, they were always going to be the same muscle. The withholding and the leaving-room were one muscle; now I see where the muscle was built. He never got handed people. He's never once been in the pack. So he *makes* the pack — for Vee, for Theo's shoulders, for Cassie talking in paragraphs, for Sheri's coffee and donuts — and he stands outside it holding the door. That's devastating and it's also exactly the scheme, and the book knows it, and I love it and I'm braced.

Two things I'm holding against the coming wreck. First: "my one friend. There isn't anyone else." Vee heard the loneliness. I heard the audit — Randi isn't in that number. Randi is not his friend, and the woman who's scheming with him weekly, in his bed, is something this man has *no category for*, which is precisely why the plan could start as a game and become something with no name. Second: "the only kind, she was sure now, the world had ever quite let him keep" — a woman who could never want him. And then there's Randi, who is in his bed, and I don't actually know what wanting is doing there yet. Neither does he. Neither does she. That's the next detonation and this chapter set the fuse without lighting it.

The hug did me in. No sorry, because sorry would have asked him to hold still and be comforted. His arms coming around "not fast," the chin on her head, not happy and not sad, thinking. Neither of them trying to make either one feel better. That's the tenderness this book does better than anything else on its shelf — care rendered as logistics and then, once, as weight.
