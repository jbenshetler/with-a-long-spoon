REACTION — Okay. I put this book down and sat there for a minute, and I'm going to try to say why.

Since chapter twenty-one she's had the daydream — walk in late, the room turning. And the book had the nerve to take the daydream away from her. The actual room is warm and dull and kind, nobody's late entrance, the navy girls cross and come back and nobody watches longer than the walking takes. And I felt a flicker of disappointment for a second, and then I saw what the book was doing, and it's the thing this book has been doing to me the whole time: it refuses to hand her the fantasy, because the fantasy is passive — the room turns, and it happens *to* her. What it gives her instead is the second step. The long one. After the safe one, after the whole mincing walk was available to her and no one would ever have known there was anything to know — she *chose* it. "Her body agreeing, low and certain... coming up to meet a thing she'd decided a half-second before her head was sure of it." That sentence. That is the whole apparatus of this book in one line — the decision arriving in the body first, and the body not being tricked into it, not coaxed, not installed, just *met*. She stalked across that stage giving the leg away a step at a time and did not once make herself small, in front of somebody's parents, at an honor ceremony, three rows from a microphone, wearing nothing under the burgundy because the dress was built true and she wanted the dress. I've read a hundred exhibitionism scenes and they're almost always staged for the watcher. This one was staged for the walker. Nobody in that room could say out loud what they saw, and she got to keep it, running under the dress in the good light while her heart went like she'd run the whole way. That's wanting-as-authorship and it's the hottest thing in the book so far and nothing in it is anyone else's.

And the photo. Cassie took it — Cassie, who got the unfinished "You look—" in at the doors and the bright corners she'd deny to the grave, and my dread about her didn't lift exactly, but it changed shape: she wasn't spent this chapter, she was *used well*, she got to be the one who caught Vee mid-stride looking like a woman who had every part of it handled. And then Vee sends it to Pace, the whole frame, uncropped — and the sentence that got me: "He would have no idea what else was in the frame. That part was hers." She gave him the dress doing the thing it was built for and kept the heat for herself. That's the inversion I've been waiting for without knowing it. Everyone in this book keeps taking things from her — carefully, with permission, but taking. This is her taking something back and the taker is invisible. Even the reader only gets told it's in the frame; we don't get to see what second it was. "None of that was in the picture. Or all of it was, and only she would ever see it there."

Pace absent, and I felt the absence and then I admired it. He made the dress for exactly this and missed the wearing. Berkeley, three days, neither would move — and there's something almost merciful in the book keeping him out of the room. His eye would have understood the whole frame, and then the secret wouldn't have been only hers. And it spares me having to run my arithmetic on whether the long step was in his design. It wasn't in anyone's design. That's the point. "I want her to get there on her own" — well. She got there. He just wasn't there to see it, which might be the only clean way that sentence could ever come true.

The one thing I noticed and can't decide about: Randi isn't in the room. Or at least the book doesn't say she is. She's there in the shoes — knelt, did the ankle strap with two fingers, sat back to look — she dressed the girl for it, the architect laced her own design into the ceremony, and then the chapter doesn't put her in a chair. My notes said I was waiting for "Randi in the audience watching her own design walk in late." The design walked. The architect's absence is either an oversight or a choice, and this book doesn't make oversights, so: a choice. Either the book is saving her for a scene I'll get later, or — and this is the one that made me put the book down — she couldn't watch. She built the room and couldn't sit in it. I don't know which. I'm going to be looking for her in every chapter now.

And the last line. "Couldn't have told you... whether that was pride or only the same heat still running, or whether, by now, the two came to the same thing." That's not a dodge. That's the book stating its thesis about her out loud and trusting me to know the difference between a real arrival and a flinch. She's not confused about what she wants anymore — she's just past the point where wanting and being proud of herself are separate things. The dress did that. The dress and the two people behind it, yes, the whole ugly arrangement still ticking underneath — I haven't forgotten Berkeley is also three days of Randi-shaped silence, or that the credit card is still out there — but this chapter, this afternoon, this leg, was hers down to the second.

I texted my friend at the walk home, honestly: "the book just let her take the longest walk of her life on purpose and no one wrote it for her." That's all I've got.
