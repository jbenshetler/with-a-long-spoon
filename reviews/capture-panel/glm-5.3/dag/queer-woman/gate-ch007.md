REACTION —

A whole chapter with the machinery turned off, and it's the best thing the book has done with Vee so far — which I don't think is an accident. No Pace, no Randi, no plan anywhere in the water. The blurb told me this man "sees Vee completely," and here's the chapter where she's most completely seen and neither of them is in it. Vee is the most herself in the one place nobody is looking at her, and I suspect the book knows exactly what it just did with that irony.

The underwater passage got me. "She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem, and the water was the one part of it that had never been taken." I put the book down after that line. The suit she wore at sixteen "when there'd been less of her to fill it" — that's the whole tragedy of this girl in one wardrobe note. And the scull her hands still remember, "palms feathering the little figure-eight that keeps you down and turned and yours" — that's a writer who has actually been underwater, who knows the one place a body can be weightless and unobserved at the same time. "Nobody down here wanted anything." Every woman I know has some version of the deep end, and the book found hers and let her grieve in it without making a scene of the grief.

The gauntlet down the bleachers, too — the trained responses, the blink, the eyeroll "aimed at the ceiling," taking the walk "like an entrance" because there's no other way to the water. The book describes that technology of being looked at without editorializing about it, and it's accurate. This is the same girl who clocked the Chi Latte floozies in her mother's voice and then looked a beat too long. The book keeps her consistent: the body is a problem precisely because it's an event in public, and the water is where the event closes.

And Cassie. I'm all the way in on Cassie now, and nervous about it. "Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman" — pitched low, just for the two of them, when she had the lungs to hit the bleachers with it. She kept it for Vee. And then the splash: she sees Vee come up with her face on wrong, doesn't ask — because asking is for people who want the sad version to get bigger — and instead drives a wall of water at her, minimal water, maximum outrage, and yanks her friend back to the surface by pure ridiculousness. "You can't grieve and fight your best friend for a mouthful of pool at the same time; the splashing won." That is love rendered exactly. That's the truest attention anyone has paid Vee in this entire book, and it came from the girl in the old navy suit who does not go to pools to be admired. The shriek-laugh, "the ugly one" — that's the first unguarded sound Vee has made in seven chapters. Not with Randi's hand on her back. With a wave to the chest and Cassie not changing expression.

Here's what I'm feeling under the warmth, though: this is the before picture. I know from the jacket that there's a plan, that two people chose her, and the book just showed me the girl in her last unoccupied country — her body in the one place it was never taken — and let me watch her be happy there. That's tenderizing me on purpose. It wants me to fall for Vee in the water so that later, when the appetite the blurb promises arrives, I'll feel it arriving at *her*, not just at the plot. I notice the manipulation. I mind it a little. But it also wrote her from inside so cleanly that I let it. Chapter 1 spent most of its length on Randi's body from Pace's eyes — hot, but cold too, a specimen half the time, and I think that's deliberate. This chapter is the proof this writer knows the difference between a body being looked at and a body being lived in. That's the difference that will decide whether this book pays for its queer material or just collects it. So far I'm still betting it pays.

Cassie had better not turn out to be the collateral. If this book uses that plain, watching girl as the roadkill on the way to the threesome, I will never forgive it. That's my ledger now.
