REACTION — Okay. So this is the chapter where the book stops being about Randi and becomes a con, and I watched the whole thing through my fingers.

I knew this was coming — the jacket told me, and chapters one through three told me, the pointing game was the rehearsal — but knowing didn't protect me from the double exposure of watching it land on Vee anyway. "She felt, absurdly, chosen." I actually put the book down at that line. Because she *was* chosen — literally, at a distance, on a lawn, by two people drinking in the afternoon. The word means two opposite things in the same sentence and the book knows it and I know it and Vee doesn't, and that's the whole machinery of this novel in one line. That's the "psychologically merciless" the blurb promised, and I'll be honest, merciless is the right word. I'm not sure I've enjoyed being this uncomfortable in a while. I keep reading it as tenderness, the book keeps insisting it's tenderness, and some part of me keeps whispering *this is grooming with better grammar.*

And yet. The thing that keeps me from closing it: Vee's wanting is *hers* on the page. It's the best-written wanting in the book so far. The forearms — "stayed a half-second past where she would have chosen, before she made it move." The way her chest did the stairs-too-fast thing. The half-second lag where her body gets ahead of her mind, "the kind of beat she would not catch until later." That's the exact register I read this genre for, desire arriving sideways and in the flesh before the mind will sign off on it. So here's my problem, and I think it's the problem the book *wants* me to have: everything Pace did in that coffee shop is technique. "Given her back the pencil at the exact line where she could solve it herself" — Vee reads that as character, I read it as *the same thing he did on the bench*. He gave her back the pencil; he gave Randi her choices. The courtliness is the cage. But the wanting it produced in Vee is not fake. Both things are true at once and the book is holding them both without flinching, and God help me, that's the kind of book I stay up for.

Two other things I need to say at 1 a.m. to somebody. One: the math. Euler's identity as the chapter's whole thesis — two things that struggle alone, joined at right angles, becoming "a single thing that's suddenly plain." That's not a tutoring scene, that's the novel explaining itself. Vee-and-Randi, or the straight wanting and the queer wanting, or Randi's two selves. And "Substitution" as a title — the technique that turns the unsolvable thing into the known thing — while Randi sits there watching a girl be walked into the experience Randi just fled from behind her own face. Whether Randi is building a substitute she can love at arm's length because she can't survive being seen herself — I genuinely don't know yet, and that question is the reason I'm still holding this book. The cup that goes up to her mouth and comes back down with no sip. We were inside Randi's skull for three chapters and now we're locked out, and the lock is the suspense.
