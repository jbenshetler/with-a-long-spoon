Oh, this book. This *book*. It walked me into a fairytale and I knew I was being handled and I let it, because I've learned by now that when this one runs the glossy version it's doing it on purpose — and then it did the thing where the gloss curdles one seam at a time, in the body, and I sat there in bed at midnight absolutely wrecked for a woman who has spent forty-five chapters being the wire.

A whole chapter inside Randi, and it's the chapter I've been waiting for without letting myself know I was waiting. First thing that hit: the breakfast table. The mother's hug "all edges and points against her," Randi *eating her revulsion* while she returns the smile warmly — that's not a detail, that's the machine's manufacturing floor. "The invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table." Every word of this girl is counterfeit because the first table she ever sat at taught her that the real ones were dangerous. MIRH and "cheating bastards" now have a childhood behind them, and I felt the bottom drop a little, the way it does when a villain's paperwork turns out to be a wound. Not forgiveness — I still want her caught — but I understand the house she was built in now, and the book knows the difference between explanation and absolution.

And John. I want to be careful here, because for about four pages I was almost bored — lift line, meet-cute, three children sent to ski camp, the whole competent handsome machinery of it — and then the run itself told me what the chapter was doing. "He stayed off her shoulder the whole way down... He could have left her behind whenever he wanted, and didn't. That was what she couldn't get past." And then the dance, where the book finally says its own thesis out loud in Randi's mouth: Pace's lead is the lightest pressure, a thing you have to be listening for and good enough to catch, "and it left the rest of the way to her. That was more work, and tonight, gliding, she thought she was done with the work. Easier was better." That's the entire moral architecture of this novel — consent as authorship, love as something you have to *do* — handed to the one character who's spent the whole book trying to automate other people's choosing, and she picks the automation for one night and lies down inside the whole approved life and feels *nothing*. "Not a poor version of it. The best." I give the book enormous credit for making John faultless. There's no flaw in him to point to. The flaw was hers, carried into the room the same as the cold. That's the most honest thing this book has said about desire: you can be given everything done well and remain unmoved, and nobody's to blame, and it still doesn't save you.

And the rhyme with chapter forty-four — tell me the book knows it did this. Vee lying awake in the flannel shirt, realizing the warmth was her own heat given back, he wasn't in it. Randi lying against John, "warm only where she touched him... everywhere else she was cold, and the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers. She had brought it in with her and she would carry it back out." Two women, two nights, two beds, each discovering that borrowed warmth is a loan against your own cold. The eyeliner had held — "Nothing had happened to her eyes" — God, that line. Her face performed the whole night and her eyes, the one true thing, went untouched. "Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best. She wished it wasn't true." The *wished it wasn't* is what makes her a person instead of a type.

But here's the part I'd be texting someone about at 1 a.m.: "Vee was her gorgeous." All this time I've been watching this book for the shape I hate most — the wanting-women as garnish, the predatory queer, the desire installed by a scheme — and this chapter is the receipt against every one of those fears. Because Randi's love for Vee is the *one thing in her that isn't currency*. The book says it exactly: she's told people she loved them before and it always had a private qualifier; with Vee there's neither. The scheme that started as a game between Randi and Pace, and underneath it, in the same woman, the least scheming feeling she has ever had. The wanting was always hers. The plan and the love are different organs in the same body, and the book knows they're going to collide, and so does she — she's the scenario-runner who has finally found the scenario she can't run to the end, and it's Vee's face while she says it plain over eggs. "She had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be." That sentence is her whole case file.

And then the texts. Both of them doing the same thing at each other from two different midnights — Vee, who walked out of Pace's warm house with her face turned away, still offering *i'm here if you want to talk. or not*, and Randi giving her the fairytale because that's the version Vee can hold: "Every word of it true. None of it the thing." And then *miss your face most though, gorgeous* — the one line she meant, sent under the word's cover, and the phone turned facedown. Two women each keeping the real thing off the table so the other one stays. I don't know if I've read a sadder exchange in this whole book, and nothing "happened" in it.

I notice the chapter gave the almost to Pace and the whole to Vee, and I noticed, and I'm fine with it — the "one second, from nowhere, Pace" in the kiss is about being *found*, it's envy of a man who looks at people all the way down, not wanting. John took a beautiful woman to bed and never found her underneath; Pace goes straight to her and that's the terror. The wanting, the real one, is sitting across from Vee on nothing Tuesdays. The book has now put both women's desires on the table fully — Vee's named against Pace and un-nameable at the brunch, Randi's named against Vee and un-deliverable at brunch. Both of them have their sentence built and both of them have decided not to say it. This is the wire humming at a pitch I can hear, and the reveal — the aggregation, the plan surfacing — is going to land on *this*, on two women each holding a sentence for the other, and I genuinely do not know which direction the book breaks. That's the best thing a book this long can do to me at chapter forty-six.
