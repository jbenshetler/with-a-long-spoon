**REACTION**

*He made it to you.* I had to put the book down after that line. Vee stood in that sun porch and cried over exactly that — the whole fitting, being built onto out of nothing — and she never had the word, and Randi walked into the room and handed it to her in one preposition. That's the thing about Randi that keeps me from being able to hate her even when I want to: the machine and the truest friend in the book are running on the same current. She saw the dress for four seconds and named it better than Vee's own tears could. Who else in this book has ever done that? Nobody. That's the problem and that's the whole book.

And then — then. The foot. I need to talk about the foot. Randi kneeling on that pale floor in her beautiful clothes, slipping the flat off, and *stopping*, holding Vee's foot in both hands and looking at the polish. The frosted plum. And "Mm." Just "Mm." One syllable with the entire history of those toenails in it — she knows why that color exists, she heard the lunch confession, and she's down there reading the evidence with her thumb. And it went all the way up the length of Vee — "the floor of her tipped" — and Vee's hand shot out and grabbed Randi's shoulder to steady herself, and the book did not cut to a joke. There was no laundering. No fast sideways move into the comedy. Randi looked up, smiled, said nothing, and went back to the box. This is the thing my notes have been waiting for since the velvet booth — the moment the charge lands in the body and neither of them scrambles away from it — and the book held it. Not held it open, but held it *closed*, the way you hold something shut that wants to open. Randi's silence in that beat is the loudest she's ever been. She knows what she just did. She's choosing her moments now, and I'm frightened of how well she's choosing them.

"Same feet, baby. Same size, same everything." *Practically a marriage.* I laughed out loud and then felt weird about laughing, which I think is exactly the note the book wanted — Randi reaching for the one form of intimacy she can say out loud, the joke that isn't a joke, the doubling. Same feet, same want, same man, eventually the book is going to make me watch those two women stand in the same shoes and I already know it and Vee doesn't and the "practically a marriage" line is going to come back with a knife in it.

The shopping itself — the yeti shoe, the bedazzled platforms, "do yetis shop here," the real laugh that costs Randi something to give. I loved that whole stretch and I want to be honest about why: the funniest woman in Virginia, that's Vee *authoring* again, and Randi doubled her instead of outshining her. And then the credit card. The credit card, God. Here's where my two feelings about Randi stopped holding hands and started fighting. Vee's debit in her fingers, then the other card, the emergency card, the months that run long — "there was nothing under it" — and the one thing she would not do in that room is flinch. And Randi beside her signing a number that was several *multiples* of Vee's without glancing down. I felt that like a finger on a bruise. Not because Vee was manipulated into wanting the shoes — the book is scrupulous about that, again, it made a point of showing the want was already hers, rehearsed all week, the room turning, "she had been running it behind her own eyes" — Randi said it out loud but she didn't put it in her. That's the one line this book has never crossed for me and I'm grateful for it, every time.

But the money. Randi steered her out of the store she could afford and into the one she can't, and never saw the math, because Randi has literally never had to run that math in her life — she didn't clock Vee's half-second with the debit because she doesn't know that hesitation exists. That's not malice. That might be worse than malice in a way — the plan was built by someone who doesn't know what things cost Vee. And the bill for those shoes is sitting in a month that hasn't arrived yet, and it's going to arrive right around the same time as the induction, and I can already see the shape of the fall. Everything beautiful in this book is on credit. That's almost the thesis at this point, and I hate how elegant that is. "Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over" — well, she handed over her signature, too, gladly, and smiled while she did it.

The word "lover," though — that landed on *me* like it landed on her. "Pace rearranged in her own chest into something adult and lit." The whole way she's been keeping him in a smaller word, and Randi says it like it's nothing, offhand, and he gets bigger. That's twice now Randi has handed Vee a truer sentence about her own life than Vee could say — "I wanted it, and I took it," and now "lover." The loneliest person in this book keeps being the one who makes Vee's life sayable.

And the last line. "She had never once in her life had a friend like this." I read that the way you read the last shot of a horror movie where the camera lingers on the basement door. It's true. That's what's killing me — it's *true*, the friendship is real, the laughter on the sidewalk was real, I felt it in my chest. It's true and it's sitting on top of a lie and every chapter of real makes the bill bigger and the book knows it and is spending it on purpose. The dress hanging off Randi's arm between them on the sidewalk — Randi carrying it, careful with it — is an image I'm going to be thinking about for a while. She built the dress's whole existence and she's the one carrying it through the light.

I notice the title. "Sorority." Randi's early-to-everything habit, four years of never being the one they waited on — and her welcoming Vee into a world, again, this time one with rope handles and no prices anywhere near it. The induction, the society — I've been reading the honor society as the destination. I'm starting to wonder if the sorority *is* the induction. Which girl is being initiated, and into what.
