REACTION — I had to put the book down twice in this chapter, once just to breathe and once because I was so angry at Pace's hands and so grateful to them at the same time that I needed a second to figure out which one I was.

The middle section is the best thing this book has done. That's not a sex scene — it's an interrogation staged as sex, and the genius of it is that the inches he's doling out aren't the currency, the *truing* is. Every stall makes her hear her own tidied version — "she moved me around" — and the stop is worse than any question because it turns her around to look at what she smoothed over. "I didn't try that hard to get my hands back." She pays in accuracy and the arousal and the confession are braided so tight neither of them could tell you which bought which. And I know — I *know* — that Pace is one of the two people who planned her, that he's getting off on the material as much as the method, and the book knows I know, and it still worked on me completely. That's the whole trick of this novel: I'm Pace in that bed, greedy for the true version, and I felt implicated in my own want and kept reading anyway.

But the last third is where it cut me. "It's not the doing that makes you a cheat, it's the keeping of it" — she builds her own exoneration out of a rule, and then she breaks the rule in the same breath: the goodbye kisses, her own hand in the dark with a name in her head, and "No. Nothing," level as a countertop. She's become Randi. That's the sentence I'd underline for someone: Vee now narrates by audience, and this is the first lie she's told *to his face, naked, in his arms*, and the book doesn't caption it — it just lets her sort it "before it reached her mouth" and my stomach dropped. And the "I'm not gay" reflex — I've heard that exact half-second of nothing-after-the-no before, in my own twenties, in friends' mouths, and Rivers writes the drop under it perfectly. The floor she grabs for is him: "You're the one who wants this. You want Randi." Blame swung onto the nearest load-bearing man. I winced in recognition, not all of it mine.

And him — "You keep saying her name, Vee. Not me." The one time in the whole book he's defended himself, over *this*, and she reads his need to be seen as innocent as the sweetest thing he's ever done. The dramatic irony in that room is at pressure. He can't say *I want this because I'm in bed with her every week* — he can only say "I didn't say Randi," stepping around the name like a puddle, and the puddle is a woman he's inside on Tuesdays. I almost laughed at the craftsmanship. Almost.

The ending is the cruelest tender thing yet: warm, held, safe, "gotten away with it," and then Tuesday sitting circled at the end of the dark. "I'm not gay" — "I didn't say gay." He left the door open and she built a wall in it. Five, six times since Friday and her mind drives itself back to campus and she can't make it come back. *What is wrong with me,* no answer, and the reader who's been this woman knows the answer and knows it wouldn't have helped. That last page hurt in the way I read this genre for.

Also — small thing, big thing — she came to him *in Randi's skirt*, left the thong stuck to her on purpose, "wouldn't ask herself why," and I noted the book keeps doing this: the wanting arrives in clothing and objects before it arrives in words. The scrap of iridescent knit is doing the work of a whole confession.

One reservation, held and surviving: the confessional sex scene runs on Pace's control at its most engineered, and I felt the wire — the man with the plan, buying her truth in strokes. But she named it herself ("that's cheating," and "it felt right"), the coin was her own want, and the chapter ends with her lying to him and him being lied to and neither of them plain. It's not garnish and it's not a scheme done *to* her desire; the desire is hers, the lie is hers, and the book is honest about both. I'll take it.
