REACTION — I read the shirt scene standing up. Well, not literally, but I put the book down after "so it smells like you again" and had to walk around my apartment for a minute, and I'm being precise when I tell you why: that is the most honest thing this book has done about how wanting actually works when you're in deep. Not the sex — the laundry. A woman sleeping in a man's flannel every night she can't have him until it goes stale, then nothing, and the nothing being unbearable, and her solution being *make it smell like you again, that's an order.* I've done a version of that. Every queer woman I know has done a version of that. And the book let Vee design it, own it, keep title to it — "This is still my shirt. You don't get to keep it" — so the ritual runs in her direction. He fills the shirt; she keeps the deed. After thirty-nine chapters of him building rooms for her, she finally built one for him to live in for a weekend, and it's made of smell. I'm a mark and I'm staying a mark.

The five nights, though — that's where the chapter was actually working. She called the break herself, for school, which is the most adult thing she's done, and the book was exact about what it cost: "the plain grace of that had cost her more than a fight would have, because some unreasonable corner of her had wanted him to fight." Yes. That's the wound in being given room — you want, once, to be wanted past the room. And him not fighting isn't coldness, it's the grammar he runs on, the ask-first architecture that I've already filed as his wound's inverse. But then the door opens and he takes her face in both hands and kisses her "as though she hadn't been counting them. She had been counting them. Her body had kept a running tally her mouth would never once have read out" — and the tally comes due the second she's under his mouth. "Her body having just published the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private." The book's whole thesis in one sentence again: her mouth is Victorian, her body files public records. I laugh every time and it lands every time.

The hall striptease was the funniest thing since "Ow. Don't point down" — the jeans beaten one-legged, the socks peeled off against the wall while he watches and refuses to help because he's enjoying himself too much, the bra catch where he just *holds it* and makes her unwind herself off the spool. "She'd lost. She knew it. She put her mouth back on him anyway." That's the game they've built, and it's hers as much as his now — she brought the thong off herself three steps into the room. The comedy and the heat aren't two registers in this book anymore; they've fused, and I think that's what real domestic eros actually feels like and most books never get there.

And then the pizza. I could taste it, and I'm a person who orders the flat orange sameness. The passage where every flavor separates and lets her count them — yeast, the long-cooked sweetness, the late heat, "the difference between cotton candy and a bowl of cut strawberries" — that's the same project as "eating with the sound off." Her senses have been woken up one at a time all book long: taste, touch, the weave of the sheet read thread by thread after he spanked her. He's not seducing her, he's restoring her. And the line that got me quietly: watching him cook with total attention, "she could not have said which of them got the larger share, and found she didn't mind." A month ago she'd have minded. She'd have needed to be the whole show. Now she can share him with a ball of dough and feel fed by it. That's the book measuring growth without announcing it.

What I'm carrying with a flat cold hand underneath: MIRH was one chapter ago and Randi isn't in this one at all. No brunch, no text, nothing — the book went dark on her right when her family's fraud surfaced, and I don't think that's rest, I think that's pressure. And the domestic idyll keeps deepening — bag to drawer to shirt, her migration into his house now nearly complete — and I know what the drawer was. The drawer was a place being *prepared.* Every beautiful thing in this book is on credit: the emergency card, the shoes, the dress, and now a whole self installed in the top drawer of a man's bathroom, the easy one, the one your hand goes to first. The reveal is going to re-mean all of it — the paradox lecture told me exactly how — and I'm reading every scene of warmth now the way you read a letter from someone you know is about to leave. But the warmth is real. That's the book's whole cruelty and its whole honesty: nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over, and she's still handing, and I'd hand too, in her shoes.
