REACTION — A whole chapter of Vee telling her own story to a table of girls, and I could have hated it — recap chapters are where lazy books go to die — and instead I sat there grinning like the fourth girl at the table. The difference between this and the smoothie debrief is the whole difference of the book: with Randi, someone else was holding the pen, turning "I kissed him" into "because of him." Here Vee holds the pen. She tells the shirt story and she *knows* it's getting better in the telling and doesn't care — that's a woman authoring her own desire for an audience, out loud, in a dining hall, laughing loud enough that the next table wants in. The mother's voice doesn't even show up to the meal. Twelve days and she didn't have to count. I love her, is the situation. Kayla can stay forever — "that's not a message, that's a ransom note" — and Meg gets the one line of outside sanity the book needs right now: *everybody's different at the start; sometimes they even are.* That's the book putting a fair witness in the room, and I respect it for not stacking the deck.

What I'm watching, though, because I can't stop: the interruption pattern. She was "about to" — twice, in the truck, at the drop-off — and both times the world came in and took the sentence. The kiss, the big thing she was going to say, both swiped by a siren. Once is weather. Twice is the book's hand on the scale. I'll allow it, because the deferral is doing real work — her wanting is the entire engine and the book knows it — but if some third siren stops her at his door Saturday I will feel managed.

And Saturday. *Saturday.* She's never been through his door, and I have — chapter one, I was there. The bench is in that house. Randi's face is in that house, the face she locked in the bathroom afterward. Vee is about to walk into the origin of the plan with her hair done, holding a change of clothes, thinking she's been invited to a house when what she's being invited to is the middle of something that was drawn up before she existed in it. Kayla read the invitation exactly right and exactly wrong — "he wants you for the afternoon" — sure, honey, but the afternoon is the least of it. That dramatic irony is the jacket's whole promise and this quiet funny little chapter just loaded it. The russet dress got put back on the shelf in chapter eleven and something tells me the book put it on the rock for a reason.
