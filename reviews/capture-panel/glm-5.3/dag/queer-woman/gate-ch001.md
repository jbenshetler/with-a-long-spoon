REACTION — Okay, so the woman on the bench is *Randi*. I clocked that about a third of the way in — the jacket told me who Randi is, the one who's going to steer Vee toward this man — and the whole time I was reading I had one eyebrow up, because the blurb basically promises me that this woman is going to be the corrupter figure, the one with the plan, and I have read that book so many times I could recite it. The seductive queer girl with the appetite that ruins the nice girl. I was ready to be tired.

And then the bathroom happened, and I'm not tired.

Because the mirror scene is the truth. That's a woman who has survived twenty-one years by being a surface — the face takes the being-looked-at, "and none of it reached any further than the face, because the face was not her, and what was not her could be given away all day and cost nothing." That's not a type talking, that's a coping structure talking, and I know that structure. I've dated women wearing it. The moment she tries the photo half-smile on top of the face that begged and sees the seam — that's the best writing in the chapter and I reread it twice. And the decision she makes in there, that he can have the face and the found one is going back behind it — that's the engine of everything the blurb promises. The plan, the game. Two people who are going to play at intimacy by proxy because the direct thing burned them. He's got his Daphne door; she's got the face. It's symmetrical and I don't think that's an accident.

Was I turned on? Yes, genuinely — the apple sequence in the kitchen is doing real work, the wanting that arrives in the body before the mind signs off, the way she keeps touching him "with the small marveling concentration." The edging itself I've read a hundred times, but "he heard himself want to hear it again" landed, and the waiting-instead-of-the-strike, the refusal to be in a hurry when every man before him was — that's desire rendered as *policy*, which is more interesting than most of what this genre manages. The detail of her pressing the water bottle cap into her thumbnail without knowing she's doing it — that's the kind of thing that tells me the author is actually paying attention to what happens to a body after it's been taken apart.

The reservation, and I want to name it honestly: most of this chapter is his gaze. She's rendered through his connoisseurship — the expensive bag, the unpainted nails as cost, the "gloss" and the "warmth under it that no one else is given" — and for a long stretch I was reading a very well-made version of a book where the woman is the artwork. The bathroom is what pays that off, retroactively, because the book turns around and says: yes, the surface, she built it, here's what it costs her to be seen through. The flip from him deciding it was "a remarkably good evening" while she's deciding to never be real in front of him again — that's the book being merciless exactly the way the blurb said it would be. He thinks he found her. He got the face performing sincerity. He doesn't know. That's genuinely unsettling and I mean it as a compliment.

No Vee at all, which I notice, and I'm fine with — I read the jacket, I know she's coming, and I'd rather the book spend this much care establishing what Randi and Pace actually are to each other before the third person walks in. If Randi were thin, the plan would just be a plot device. She's not thin. I'm in.

One thing I'm watching: when the queer wanting starts — Randi wanting Vee — this book has to let it be *hers*, Randi's, and not just an instrument of the game, or another thing the face does while the real one stays locked in the bathroom. The blurb swears it gets real for all three of them. I'll take that on credit for now, because chapter one paid its debts.
