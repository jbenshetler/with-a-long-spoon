REACTION — Okay. So chapter one is not about Vee at all — it's Randi, the glamorous friend from the jacket, facedown on a bench. I sat with that for a minute and decided I don't mind; the blurb told me the plan predates Vee, and this is the plan's other engine, and honestly I'd rather meet Randi here first than have her arrive later as a sketch.

What worked on me, hard: the bathroom mirror. That's the best thing in the chapter and I don't think it's close. The whole bench sequence is well done — the pacing of it, the way he keeps not taking what she keeps almost handing him — but it's the mirror that got me. "The face was lying. The face had been lying for years." A woman rebuilding her mask in real time while the mask watches itself do it — that's desire and terror from the inside, that's the thing I read for. The detail of the water bottle cap pressed under her thumbnail, *and she did not seem to know she was doing it* — that's the kind of small true cruelty of observation that makes me trust a writer. And "not ever. He could have the face" landing "quiet as a deadbolt going over" — and she's the woman who locks his front door behind her first thing, every time. That echo is doing real work and I noticed it and I admired it.

What I noticed with more wariness: Pace. He's written as the fantasy of attentiveness — the apple sliced thin with the cut sides up, the water left out in advance, the "he had learned not to interpret things for her." He never once missteps. That's a choice, and it's a seductive one, but I've read this man before in books that wanted me to trust him so the rug-pull would land harder — and the jacket has already told me there's a rug. "Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over. That was the problem." So I'm reading Pace with one eyebrow up. The Daphne paragraph — the door he didn't respect, the thing it cost him — is the first honest crack in him, the first time the book admits this man has a history of deciding what's good for a woman, and it cost *him*. I'd flag that to a friend at 1 a.m.: "the book thinks his lesson is 'respect closed doors' but the actual pattern is 'he finds the hunger and answers it,' and Randi just spent a chapter having hers answered without her consent to the answering." The aliveness came *at* her. "It had not come from her." She says that herself, in the mirror, and calls it ecstasy anyway. That's the book being smarter than Pace, or at least I hope it is. If it's not smarter than Pace, we're going to have a problem.

No Vee yet, no charge between women yet — the blurb's promise about Randi wanting Randi's side of it is all still ahead. That's deferral, but it's deferral the jacket explicitly structured ("Book One," the plan, the reader ahead of her from the start), and this chapter paid its own way, so I'm not charging it to the complaint column. Yet.

Also, low notes: the second-person-flavored line about the sorority — "he had seen them" — made me laugh, darkly, the one-liner economy of it. And "blue toenails, the one small place she let herself step out of line" — I liked that more than I expected to.
