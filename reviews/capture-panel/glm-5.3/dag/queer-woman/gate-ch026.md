REACTION — Okay, this one got me in the body and then made me sit with something uncomfortable, and I want to be honest about both halves.

The lesson on the pad is the best pure courtship scene the book has done since the truck in the rain. "Give me something to push back against. Being jelly doesn't work" — that's the whole thesis of the book said in a dance instruction, and it said it *to her*, as technique, not as philosophy. And the moment it gives — "the decision pulled from her somehow" — that's the most dangerous sentence in the chapter and the book knows it, because "He's making me" arrives in her head at the bar in exactly the same words. The lead arriving before her feet decide. The book is letting her feel both edges of that — the ecstasy of being led and the vertigo of where her own authorship went — and she's the one who thinks both thoughts, unprompted, which is why I trust the book with it. Nobody installed this suspicion. It rose out of her body, out of the dance itself. That's the honest version of my allergy: a book that stages "he makes girls do just what he wants" and makes me sit in how *hot and how frightening* that is simultaneously, without pretending only one of those is true.

The blonde. I clocked her instantly and I want it on the record: "Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to" is not small talk. Nobody says that to a stranger. Somebody was stinging her on purpose, and the bartender's "Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey" only half-soothes it for me. Either the blonde is just a local girl with a crush taking her shot, or — and my paranoid reader brain is wide awake at 2 a.m. — this book has a *plan* in it, and I've spent twenty-five chapters knowing there are exactly two people who run operations on Vee's evenings and I don't recognize this one. Chekhov's blonde. I'm watching that drawer too now, alongside the scar.

And Vee's spiral. "Three dates, and she'd spread her legs like a slut" — that's her mother's voice doing its work, and the fact that she goes from jealousy straight to self-slut-shaming is so painfully accurate I flinched. "Floozies. She watched them." She turned the shame outward at the other girls before she could feel it as her own — I know that move, I did that move at twenty-one. And then the 4 a.m. reckoning where she gets it right — "she'd punished him for being the man she wanted him to be" — but the sting, the thing she can't put down, is the sensation of the lead. Both things true at once, and the book refuses to resolve it into either "he's a predator" or "she was silly." That refusal is why I'm still here.

The end terrifies me in the good way. She's going to Randi. She's going to walk her fresh fight — her jealousy, her shame, her wanting — straight into the mouth of the person who co-authored the whole thing, because Randi is the person she tells everything, and Randi is going to receive it, and I have no idea what Randi does with this one. That lunch is either going to be the loneliest thing Randi's ever had to sit through or the moment the machine tips over. I'd text a friend right now: "the dramatic irony in this book is a loaded gun on every page."

One reservation, small but real: the bartender's exculpation was a little too tidy, a little too much the wise barkeep dispensing the truth. The book earns its conveniences most of the time; that one it was handed for free.
