**REACTION —**

The dance lesson is the filthiest thing this book has done in weeks and nobody so much as took a shoe off. "Give me something to push back against — being jelly doesn't work." Do you understand what that sentence is. That's the entire grammar of this book — the frame, the push-back, the information traveling through the point of contact — taught to me on a cold concrete pad with a boom box held together with electrical tape. And the thing where the frame finally works and she realizes the closeness she fought is the thing that makes her free: *it held her in the one place the next step could come from, so there was nothing to do but take it.* I've read whole novels about submission that never got within a county of saying it that clean. I read that page twice with my hand over my mouth like Vee in front of the dress.

And then the bar. Okay. The blonde. I need to talk about the blonde, because for about one page I was genuinely frightened — I've seen that exact scene before, in worse books, where the woman at the rail is the book's own sour mouth, where "knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to" is the thesis the author actually holds and is about to punish the heroine for. "All us girls love him. He's so good about getting a girl home safe." I sat there going *don't, don't, don't* — and then the book did the thing that keeps me: it let Vee get ugly anyway. The venom she heard coming out of her own mouth "the way you hear a glass go past the point where you can still catch it" — that's shame and jealousy rendered from the inside, no villain required. And Pace didn't defend himself. "Yes. Sometimes. When it's required." Four words, no charm, no case for the defense, beer untouched, gone. That's the man who stopped the kiss at the front door. He has never once told her what to believe about him and I don't think he's going to start.

But here's what I can't stop chewing at one in the morning: the blonde is wrong about Pace and right about the book. "Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to." I know about the coffee shop. I know about Randi's Fridays. This woman at the rail, doing three drinks' worth of damage with a smile, accidentally said the truest sentence anyone's said in this novel — aimed it at the wrong crime and missed the real one by a room's width. Vee stood there being played by a jealous stranger and couldn't taste that she's being played by the two people she loves, and *I* had to sit there watching her not know. The bartender saying "you're the one the girls were all talking about" — Vee's a subject in rooms she doesn't know she's a subject in. That's the whole book in one line of bar talk.

The night after. The shirt. She washed it and it came back "smelling of fabric softener and no one," and she put it on anyway, and I had to put the book down for a second because I've done that. Rationed the smell of somebody until it went stale, hated the machine, wore it anyway. That detail is aimed and it landed. And then the audit she does in the dark — "she'd punished him for being the man she wanted him to be. She'd done it to a good man" — I love that the book lets her be wrong AND lets the blonde be poison at the same time, no either/or. The shame voice came back ("spread her legs like a slut" — that's her mother's arithmetic running, and three dates, the way she shrinks it when she's bleeding) but this time the shame did work by morning instead of closing her. "Cool embarrassment is just information." Vee, baby, you're going to be dangerous when you're forty.

And then the last line, where she decides the fix is Randi. Oh no. Oh *yes.* She's running to the co-architect to repair a breach in the design she doesn't know has a designer. And I know something she doesn't about that house Randi keeps visiting — Sade on the playlist, "she's not ready, you'll want to take your time," Randi wanting out loud what she can't stop wanting. Is the fight a problem for the plan or a gift to it? Does Randi patch her toward him, or does something in Randi's hands shake while she does it? I genuinely don't know, and I'm not sleeping until I find out.
