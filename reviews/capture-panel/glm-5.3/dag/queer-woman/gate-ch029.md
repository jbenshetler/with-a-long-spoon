She changed her shoes in the car and I put my hand over my mouth, because I know something she doesn't: everything she's wearing is Randi's. The shoes, the scarf, the knot tied the way Randi taught her, "flung over one shoulder" per instruction. She thinks she's arriving at Pace's door as herself, contrite and sure, and she's arriving dressed head to collarbone by the woman who's sleeping with him, built like a costume for this exact doorstep — and the book never says one word about it. After chapter 24 I can't unsee it. Randi stood in front of her in that little dark room in a robe with no makeup on and ran the fitting and said "he doesn't stand a chance," and Randi knew — knows — what those heels do, knows the man, knows the inch. And Vee walks up the steps thinking the shoes are just "the right thing to wear to apologize." The blurb promised I'd be ahead of her, falling for it exactly as she does, and this is the first chapter where I felt the full weight of that arrangement. It's not garnish. It's load-bearing.

But here's the thing, and I need to say it to whoever's listening at 1 a.m.: the heat is still real. That's what's killing me about this book. The plan is real, the loneliness inside the plan is real (Randi saying "she got past me before I could help it" while lying on Pace's chest — the loneliest sentence in a book full of sex), and the door-sill scene is real on its own terms. When the heels put her eyes above his and it goes through her "like a missed stair" — that's such exact body-writing, the body knowing a fact the mind hadn't filed. And his answer. "Beauty like yours made for the gods and loaned out." Loaned out. He said *loaned* — the book put the word in his mouth, borrowed clothes, borrowed shoes, a girl dressed out of another woman's closet, and he compliments the *loan*. I don't know if he knows. I don't know if the book knows it knows. I think it knows.

The apology itself undid me more than I expected. "She handed me a lie and I picked it up because some part of me was just standing there waiting for one." That's the ugliest and truest thing she's said in the book, and it's the mother's voice finally named as something with a *place waiting in her for it* — the shame isn't just installed, it's a hook the world can hang things on, and she's just now seeing the hook. And his "thank you for telling me what she said" — strange, precise, him thanking her for the exact piece of it that cost the most to hand over. That's his grammar. He keeps thanking her for the things other men would wave off.

And then the kitchen. Okay. The slap. I went still when it landed, because this is the fork in the road where a hundred books have gone wrong on me — the woman's body says a thing "she had not agreed to have it say" and I brace for the book to make that a project, something done to her, a curriculum. And it didn't. It was a genuine accident — she swung into his hand mid-tickle, the book takes visible care to make it no one's design — and then everything after is *hers*, and the book writes the wanting from the inside with the door shut: the nipples, the soundless oh, and then her whole strategy of talking, the pan, the soak, the running commentary to keep the quiet out of the room because "a quiet room was a room with a question standing in it." That's the most accurate description of how unsayable desire behaves that I've read in this genre in years — desire as something you launder through chatter about dishes while your own hips keep lighting the match. "She could have gone slower. She did not go slower." That's her authoring it without letting herself know she's authoring it, which is this book's whole subject.

And his restraint again. The palm comes down and *covers*. No strike. Nothing new. "This?" — and she says "please," twice, "attached to nothing," and the book leaves it unattached. He gives her pressure where he hit her and not the thing itself. The man asks before he takes even when the asking is just a flat hand and a one-word question. I'm a willing mark, I've said it before, I'll say it again.

The end. "She left her face where it was and let it sit there, unnamed, hers." *Hers.* The book hands her the ownership of the unsaid thing in the final clause of the scene. That's the sentence I'd underline. Whatever this kink becomes — and the title, "A Recognized Method," is laughing at me a little; soaking, apologizing, seducing, everything here is method, the only person without one is Vee — the first taste of it is hers, uninstalled, unclaimed, sitting in the room with both of them and neither of them reaching for it. Not *you're beautiful*, which she'd have argued. *Always.*" He found the tense she can't fight, because it isn't contingent on the disaster her hair currently is. I'm gone. I'm fully gone.
