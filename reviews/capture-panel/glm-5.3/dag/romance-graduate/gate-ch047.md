REACTION

Okay. I've been waiting chapters for the book to open Randi all the way, and instead it handed me this — a whole glittering evening built to show me exactly what she is, by showing me the best the "acceptable world" can offer her. And it worked on me completely, because the fairytale is *good*. John is genuinely lovely. That's the trick of the chapter and the trick of her life: there's no flaw in him to point to. The sleigh with the grey horses, the kids sent to a ski camp that doesn't exist, "She walks in beauty" and he couldn't finish it — I was charmed right alongside her, and then she gets into that bed and the charm costs *her*, not me, and I lay there in the borrowed warmth with her feeling nothing.

The ski run was the thesis. He could have left her behind whenever he wanted, and didn't. That's what she couldn't get past. And then later — "He was a better dancer than Pace. She turned the thought over and found it true" — and I actually gasped, because the book just said the quiet thing: easier is better, she thought, tonight, gliding. That's not the villain's chapter, that's the chapter of a woman who has been working her whole life and got one night of being sent, and her body went where it was pointed and it was the easiest thing in the world. "Pace made her say it, out loud, every time. She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked." That sentence is doing so much work I could cry.

And the cold. "The cold did not belong to the room. It was hers. She had brought it in with her and she would carry it back out." Compare that to Vee two chapters ago, lying bare in the cold refusing the shirt. The two of them doing the same unbearable thing alone, on the same night of the year, and neither knows it. Vee's text arriving from the warm kitchen in Ohio — "Vee was her gorgeous" — and Randi typing the fairytale back with every word true and none of it the thing. I had to put the book down. "She was not brave enough to lose her. She had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be." That's the most honest sentence about a certain kind of beautiful, managed woman I've read in years.

But — and I noticed this and I'm not letting it go — this is also the con artist narrating her own myth. The invented boyfriends at breakfast, safer than the real ones. "Vee heard it one way; Randi meant it two ways." And the plan at the end is *explicitly the con continuing*: let Vee arrive at it on her own, keep the brunches exactly as they are, keep the word under its cover. The book has me aching for her and watching her rebuild the trap in the same paragraphs, and I don't think that's an accident, I think that's the whole point of her. The mirror chapter, MIRA on the screen, the father whose fraud just surfaced in the data — and now this, the chalet, the label on the belt, "eating her revulsion" at breakfast. Her whole world is a beautiful forgery and Vee is the one true thing she owns and she's choosing to hold it the fraudulent way.

Also — MIRA. That chapter hits different now. She flew to Gstaad and lay down in the middle of the exact life that money bought with laid-off workers, and felt nothing there either. The flaw was hers, carried into the room same as the cold. God.

No Vee, no Pace on screen, zero plot forward on the surface — and I didn't care for one second. This was the chapter I've been waiting for since the mirror.
