REACTION:

I had to put the Kindle down. Not because it was bad — because it was *too good*, the exact kind of too good where the book reaches into your chest and rearranges something you'd filed under "my own history." Vee building that sentence for weeks — running it in different orders, heat coming up the back of her neck at the imagining, carrying it "assembled" — I know that sentence. Every woman who's ever been in love with a man who communicates in joinery knows that sentence. The crackers-and-meal thing is devastating because it's TRUE and because she checked it so carefully that it wasn't *I love you* in any shape he could take it for — she built a heat shield around the payload and he still heard only what he chose to hear. Or didn't hear it at all. And the book won't tell us which, and *she* can't test it because "the words go through him first." God. That's the cruelest mechanic in the whole book and it's the truest: you cannot ask a man whether he understood your almost without converting the almost into the whole.

"I love how good we are together." I read that line twice with my hand over my mouth. It's such a *well-made* non-answer — sincere, warm, he even adds "I never had this either," which is a real gift, and that's exactly why it hurts. If it were cold she could grieve it. Instead it's a beautiful door that isn't a door. Her body filing the *wait* before her mind catches up — that's this book all over, the body always files first, and I love that the dryer-room scene trusts me to sit in "I do not know what just happened" without resolving it. No explaining-the-subtext. The pile. "The place was not empty. There were other things in it already." CHILLS. That's the blurb's "he gives her everything but a word" arriving in the text with its teeth out, and the chapter title — *Cropped* — cutting twice: the photo she cropped at the waist for her parents, and this, her love cropped at the "I am grateful" line, everything below the waist of the sentence hidden. This writer is not accidentally good.

The cafeteria half first, because it earned its space: "weird is worse than wrong" is the single most accurate thing anyone's said in this book about Pace's parents and about half the men I've dated. And her dad pricing the dress, picturing grey at the temples — I laughed and then felt caught, because she's doing to her parents exactly what Pace does to her: feeding them a cropped version and calling it protection. "I put a question mark on it. Like I was asking them." She's asking EVERYONE except him. Also — Mom's nurse face, watching the seconds. The bras going loose, the weight going — my stomach is in my shoes. Mom is the only person counting what Vee stopped counting, and Vee's answer is "I'm happier than I've ever been," and the horrible thing is it's TRUE, both things are true at once.

I noticed what this chapter did structurally and I'm not going to pretend I didn't: the flannel went back Sunday smelling like him — I assume, we skipped it — and this is the first chapter where Vee's mouth finally got near the yes and got handed warmth instead of a word. Cassie's "is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to" — "Both." That girl deserves better than what this triangle is going to do to her, and she's the only one asking the real questions.

I'm a wreck and I want the next one immediately. The book has stopped being the spice-plus-depth thing I picked it up for and become the thing where I dread Tuesdays.
