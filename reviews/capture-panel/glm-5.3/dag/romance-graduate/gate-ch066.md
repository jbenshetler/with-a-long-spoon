REACTION — I need a minute.

That chapter didn't hurt the way I expected it to. I came in ready for the hangover-cute — bread baked while she slept, water on the nightstand on a folded paper towel so it wouldn't mark the wood, and honestly the paper towel detail nearly got me on its own, that's a whole man in one square of paper — and then the book just... turned the floor to glass and let me stand there.

"Sheri's my friend. My one friend. There isn't anyone else." I put the book down. I actually put it down. Because I'd been riding the party high for two chapters — watching him hand people first lines, watching Cassie say "you can stay" — and I'd filed him the way Vee filed him: the comfortable one, the social one, the man who makes rooms easier. And the book let me have that pleasure fully and THEN went "by the way, none of it ever came back to him. Nobody is his Kayla. Nobody is his Cassie. He has one friend and she's the one woman in Virginia guaranteed never to want him, which is the only reason the friendship survived." Sixty-six chapters and I'd never once asked the obvious question. Vee never asked it either. That's not me being dumb, that's the book being patient.

The window-that-opens-once stuff — men make friends by losing to each other in the middle of the pack, and he was never once in the pack — I believed it so hard it made my chest hurt, because I've met that man. I've *dated* that man, the prodigy with the beautiful kitchen and the empty calendar. And then "the ones already there, when I was small among them, either weren't safe or weren't welcoming." He didn't say a name. He didn't have to. We know. Vee knows — and I watched her do the arithmetic she "wasn't asked to do," stacking "less than staying" and "not home since sixteen" and Sheri's flat little report, and land on the thing neither of them will name. The book keeps refusing to say the ugly word out loud and keeps making me say it in my own head, and it works every single time, and I resent how well it works.

And the Christmas questions. "What are you doing for Christmas? Your folks coming up?" — every one of those has a gap under it, for me. That's the loneliest specific I've ever read. Friendship's doorway is small talk, and he can't do the small talk, and he knows exactly why, and he calls it "not tragic" — and Vee nailed it: the good order is the tell. Two years he called Daphne luck. A childhood he calls a door that didn't open. He files his wounds as weather.

The hug at the end. She copied Sheri's hug — whole weight committed, cheek flat to his chest — and the book had already taught me the grammar of that hug so I'd recognize it when she spent it. And his arms coming "after a moment. Not fast." And his chin on her head while he's somewhere else with it. He doesn't get comforted, he gets *held*, briefly, by arrangement. God.

Also: "she'd had that much of him to begin with only because of the thing about it that was wrong" — the book finally let Vee look at the Daphne shape head-on, both truths stacked and unreconciling, and let her NOT resolve it. That's the grown-up move. That's the thing I'm always begging these books for.

One small quiet thing: next Saturday is the dance hall. Brooks and Dunn, Sheri commanding, "leave them shoes home." The book left that door standing open on its way out and I'm walking toward it.
