The drawer. I actually had to put the Kindle down at the drawer. This book has a whole vocabulary of these gestures by now — the covered bowl, the folded undershirt, "you're always beautiful" — and I should be getting inoculated against them, but this one got past everything. He didn't say it didn't matter. He went and made her a place instead. And then the detail that wrecked me: HIS stuff crammed too tight into the one drawer that was built to hold half of it. He folded his whole ordered self down to make room and never mentioned it, never will. That's not romance-hero behavior, that's a specific man's grammar of love, and the book knows it and doesn't underline it, just leaves her standing with a hand on each drawer and me sitting in the dark doing the math on which of them is more gone on the other.

And the counter beat before it — I love that the book let the worry be ambiguous. She spilled herself across his clean surface and he smiled into the mirror and said nothing, and she carried a small tail of *did he mind* out of the room, and the answer turned out to be the drawer. The whole chapter works like that: a question laid down, left overnight, answered in hardware.

But the part I keep turning over is section one, and it's darker than it looks. "Peter," she said, to the sleeping shape of him. Barely a breath. She's saying the name nobody uses — the boy from the hallway — to the unconscious man, and she's saying *I love you* only inside her own chest, "where she couldn't take it back," and I noticed the book's exact phrasing: he says it in everything and *nothing*, and she "could live clear inside the everything-he-did and not go hungry for the saying." That is either the most grown-up thing this book has said or the most elaborate denial, and I genuinely can't tell, and I don't think she can either. Because I'm still holding chapter thirty's Randi problem: nobody in this house says the real words out loud, and Vee has now formally joined them. Meanwhile her body is burning down weekends of him — she's LOSING WEIGHT, her clothes going loose, the bras keeping the secret — and the book slid that in between the salmon and the sauce like it was nothing. It's not nothing. She's physically shrinking inside the happiest stretch of her life and calling it happiness, and I don't think that's an accident of prose.

Also, small thing, big to me: "He never went straight at his own finish... it always came around to her first." She's cataloguing what's normal about him against every man before him, building the case for love out of evidence, the way she builds everything — the clean machine, the induction walk, the drawer inventory. This girl processes the world like a stats problem and keeps getting the answer wrong on purpose. That's why I can't quit her.
