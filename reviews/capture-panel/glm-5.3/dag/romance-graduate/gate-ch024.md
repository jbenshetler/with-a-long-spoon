REACTION

I have been carrying three open files on Pace since chapter one — the Daphne door, the parents door, the scar — and this chapter opened every one of them in a single night by the fire, and I'm sitting here in the dark with my tea gone cold like theirs, not okay.

Peter. His name is Peter. The book gave me the flattest, plainest name in the world for the man I've been reading as an operator for twenty-two chapters, and it landed like a key turning. "Vivienne" means trouble in one mouth and love in the other, and Peter means nothing, means a boy from a chemical-plant town who taught himself trigonometry in seventh grade and got his desk moved into the hall for it. THE HALL. For the whole year. They took the thing he loved and made it the punishment — Vee said it before I could, and I watched him look down at her "as if she had put her hand on a place he had not offered her and had, by accident, found the right one." That's the whole book's method described from the inside. That's what he does to Randi on the bench and to Vee in the sunroom — he finds the place not offered. Because somebody did it to him first, with long-division worksheets, and the technique was born in a hallway. I had the origin story of the method and I never once guessed it was a wound.

And Daphne. I've been waiting for Daphne since chapter one — "she had built a door, once, and he had not respected it" — and I built her up in my head as the reason he asks permission, as the thing he broke. And the truth is so much worse and so much sadder: she was nineteen and he was FIFTEEN. His tutor. Two years. And he handed it to Vee as luck — "he was defending it, setting himself between the memory and the word she hadn't said, and he did not hear himself doing it. He believed every part of what he'd told her." Vee did the arithmetic and held the clean ugly word under her tongue and I was holding it too, and the book let BOTH of us not say it, and that restraint is the most grown-up thing it's ever done. That word is not hers to say and it's not mine either and the book knows it. What the book gave me instead is worse: the man who learned about love at fifteen from a nineteen-year-old who was assigned to him like a course requirement, and who left at twenty-one because the bars wouldn't let him in — of course this is a man who asks before he takes. Of course he counts her please-syllables and waits for the real one. Somebody taught him the exact weight of an uneven arrangement and he has spent his whole adulthood making sure he's never on the heavy end. I have to sit down. The "may I" wasn't courtliness. It was the scar tissue.

The scar. He pressed her hand DOWN onto it — didn't move her away, held her there, on the thing he wasn't going to tell her, "not tonight" with no wall in it, only a door with a time on it. The man is rationing his own past out on a schedule, the same schedule as the oyster yardage, and I noticed my ledger has the same entry in two columns and I don't know if that's the design or just who he is. I think it's who he is. This chapter did to my grudge file what the flannel did in chapter ten — emptied it. A fifteen-year-old in a dorm full of grown men, lifting so there'd be nothing in his mind but the bar. "It was a useful incentive." Oh, Peter.

And the math. I need to confess about the math, because I'm the person who skips the math. He gave her the paint and the coastline and Newton's three sentences, and "the ball and the leaf and the stars are all doing the one thing, and once you can read it you can't unsee it" — and I read every word, and then Vee looked at the stars "because he was looking at them" and they stopped being decoration, and the book had done it to me too. It's the language the world is written in, delivered by a man who was moved into the hall for asking questions, said "with almost grief." I fell in love with him this chapter, fully, and I'd already mostly done it, and now I know what the laughter in the front rooms of his life was worth to him — "the first year of my life I wasn't in the hall."

But here's where the chapter turned cold on me, and I need to say it plain: the ending. The induction — he can't come. Baby came early, Berkeley, the same three days, he looked, and I believe him, I do. But the timing! The book has loaded that ceremony for five chapters — Randi's inexcusable absence, the shoes on credit, "stop the room cold," and now BOTH of them will be missing from the audience. Vee will walk into that room in a dress with a hidden seam and four-inch debt and neither of the two people who built her for that night will be there to see it. And the very last paragraphs — "she had stumbled into her," the collision re-narrated from inside Vee's head as luck, as fortune, "for no reason she had done anything to deserve" — the book made her say the dramatic irony OUT LOUD, lying in his arms, one piece, and I actually put the Kindle on my chest and lay there. Six weeks. She thinks it's one piece. It is two people. And the second one is spending Tuesday at a sorority officers' meeting she could get out of, and I still don't know why, and Tuesday is the day after tomorrow.

The fire, the quilt, the cold on its own side of the glass — this is the quietest chapter in the book and I didn't skim a line, because the whole thing was the talk-two-people scene I told someone this book does better than anything, and then the scar underneath it. Also, small ledger note: she made the connection — the lift, "you're all the way here, it's the same face" — she keeps seeing the technique and naming it love and being RIGHT that it's love, and I've given up splitting it because so has the book.

One thing I'm watching, uneasy: "Not tonight" is the first thing he's ever refused her. It was gentle and it was a refusal, and the fact that it thrilled me a little — the withholding still working on me — tells me I'm inside the machine with everyone else.
