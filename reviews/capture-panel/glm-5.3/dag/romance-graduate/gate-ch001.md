REACTION — Okay. I need to talk about this.

First — I clocked it at "There you are, Randi." The *Randi*. Blurb-Randi, the glamorous friend who steers Vee into Pace's path, and this book just opened by handing me the scene that made her. I had to put the Kindle down for a second at that reveal, not because it was shocking but because of what it instantly did to the whole blurb. The woman on that bench — the one who got seen all the way through for one night and then stood at that mirror and decided *not ever, he can have the face* — that's the woman who's going to build a game out of another girl. She's going to hand Pace someone she can watch him want, keep her own mask bolted on, and call it control. I already understand her and I already ache for Vee and I already ache for *her*, and it's chapter one. That's not nothing. That's the thing I've been begging these books for.

And the heat — this is the real stuff. The spanking scene did something I actually can't remember the last time a scene did, which is make the *edging* the psychological event instead of the physical one. Him listening for the difference between her waiter-please and her real please — "the voice she said please in when she asked a waiter for a glass of water" — that's a filthy line and a devastating one at the same time. The apple. Him slicing it thin because she eats thin slices, cut sides up because she eats faster when they show. I'm a hundred books deep in "he remembers her coffee order" and this man noticed which *orientation of apple slice* gets eaten faster. And then feeding becomes the game, bite-kiss-bite, and it's doing double duty the whole time — the girl who portions herself, finally letting someone feed her. I'm not going to pretend I wasn't affected.

What almost lost me: the early stretch, where his narration catalogs her — the monogrammed bag, "the money in the grain and the weight of it," the sorority built to one scale. It's a man narrating a woman like an appraisal, and it's *intended* — this is his POV and he sees everything, that's literally the book's engine — but for about a page I felt that familiar tightness, like, is this book going to be one long perceptive man decoding a pretty girl? And the omniscience teetered once or twice: "he watched the thing behind her face that policed her relationship to food run through whatever calculation it was running." Careful, book. You're at the edge of explaining her to me.

But then the bathroom scene. And here's what saved it and then some: the bathroom scene is where the book *stops letting him be right*. He's sitting on the bed thinking it was a remarkable evening, gently smug, planning to answer her hunger "as often as she wanted to bring it to him" — while behind the locked door she's deciding he can never have the real one again. The book cut him off at the knees exactly when I needed it to. He read every apple slice and missed the whole woman. Even the Daphne paragraph — him patting himself on the back for learning to respect a closed door — lands differently once you realize the door he's respecting is the one she's using to reassemble the mask. The dramatic irony isn't a trick; it's the tragedy, and it's clearly the series.

The cap edge under her thumbnail. The lock clicking "quiet as a deadbolt going over." Him noticing he's still hard and setting it down — "that's not what this night was for" — a hero who doesn't get his ending and the book doesn't pretend he minds. And the last image, the bench becoming a table again, "full of what it was, doing what it would continue to do, waiting for the next time" — that's the whole blurb in a piece of furniture.

One real reservation I'm carrying forward: almost all of the interiority so far is *his*. Randi got one scene of her own, a great one, but I know the blurb promises me Vee, and this book has now shown me it can do the seeing-man and the seen-woman — it has one more woman to prove it can do. If Vee turns out to be just another face for Pace to read, I'm out. But the writing is real — actually written, feeding me, not performing — and the heat is real, and the tenderness is *merciless* exactly like the jacket promised. I texted my friend at a reasonable hour for once: "new book, the dom sliced the apple thin, I'm ruined."
