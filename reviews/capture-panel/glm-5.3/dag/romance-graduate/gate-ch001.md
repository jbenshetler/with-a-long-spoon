REACTION — Okay. I did not expect *that* from chapter one of a book whose blurb is about a college junior named Vee. The woman on the bench is Randi — the glamorous friend, the schemer from the jacket — and Pace is already deep in something with her before Vee even enters the story. So the blurb's "game between the two people now closest to her" is apparently already a real, consuming, months-long thing, and I'm being shown that *before* I'm shown the girl it gets played on. That's either going to be brilliant or ruthless, and honestly both prospects are why I turned the page in the first place.

And the chapter itself — look, I've read a hundred spanking scenes. I could recite them. This is the first one in years where I actually cared what happened between the strikes. The edging-denial structure is familiar, but the way he *waits* for the please to change registers — he won't accept the sorority please, he wants the one from underneath — that's psychological writing doing the work that choreography usually does. The "Good girl" landed because it was earned across pages, not dropped in as a stock phrase. And then the sex ends and the chapter keeps going, and the bathroom scene is the best part of the whole thing. Her standing there watching herself fail to put the mask back on, trying the photo smile and seeing it sit "on top of the face that had begged" — that gave me an actual chill. "The face had been lying for years." I got goosebumps. That's not a romance-novel beat; that's a character being built in real time.

The detail work is feeding me, too — the apple slices laid cut-side up because she eats them faster that way, the water bottle staged in advance, the cashmere blanket she brought after week two because she gets cold in his too-warm house. He knows her. But here's what's making me itchy, in a good way: the POV is his, and the POV *likes* him, and I caught myself trusting him — and then I remember the jacket. This man is planning a seduction of a third person as a game. The bench scene reads as tenderness and mastery, and somewhere in this same house is a plan for a girl who doesn't know there's a plan. The chapter even plants it: "Daphne had built a door, once, and he had not respected it." So his courtly respect was *learned*, at cost, from a woman I haven't met. That's the hook under the hook.

What unnerves me a little: Randi deciding, mid-aftercare, "not ever" — that he can have the face, and the one he found goes back behind it. He thinks the evening was a triumph of intimacy; she's already building the wall back. He has no idea. The book let me see through him in the last few pages, and that slight vertigo — the narration itself gently deceived — is exactly the kind of thing I've been starving for.

Small caveats: it's long, and the middle of the spanking section gets a touch *exact* about itself — the prose occasionally lapses into cataloguing skin temperature like an inventory. And "the small breath of a laugh" appeared about four times; I noticed, and I'll notice again if it keeps happening. But those are quibbles against a chapter that made a denial scene feel like an emotional event and ended with a woman deciding to disappear from a man who thinks he just found her.
