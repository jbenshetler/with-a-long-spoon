REACTION — The dinner conversation nearly killed me. She's sitting at his stove telling him about Randi — "she does this funny goodbye kiss, it's French, I guess" — and he's miming cheek kisses with a spatula while the reader knows exactly who Randi is and what that kiss is and what he's been doing with the mouth that's doing it. I had to put the book down. Not because it was bad — because it was so cruelly, perfectly done. He asked "Short brunette?" like he had to check. And "Only good things, I hope" — that grin. He's enjoying this. There's something almost filthy about how much he's enjoying this, his girl handing him the picture of her weekends with his lover, innocently, over salmon. And the dark current under it: everything she confides travels. She told him "your name might come up" — meaning she's telling Randi everything about him, meaning Randi is getting fed Vee's sex life as material, and here Vee is confirming it to the man without knowing she's confirming it. The dramatic irony isn't a trick anymore, it's a weight on every line.

But the chapter earned its landing somewhere I didn't expect. The bathroom counter. God. Her whole disordered self flung across his one clean surface, and her waiting, braced, the guest's ring on good wood — and he does nothing, says nothing, and she carries the worry out with her. And I thought, okay, that's the note, that's his silence costing her something small. A week of that unease. And then the drawer. I actually got misty. He didn't reassure her. He didn't say it didn't matter — he folded his entire ordered self down into one cramped drawer and gave her the top one, the easy one, the one your hand goes to first. Made her a place instead of a sentence. For a man whose whole damage is that nobody ever made room for him — the desk in the hall — that is the loudest thing he's ever said. He gave her the opposite of the hall.

And it lands right after she says "Peter" to his sleeping shape. She's let herself love him, out loud to the dark, and decided she can live in the everything-he-does — and then the everything-he-does does THIS. The book is feeding her (and me) the word in every language except the word. The blurb promised "everything but a word for what they are" and it's keeping that promise with terrible tenderness.

The "never been so fortunate, or so happy" pattern — this chapter has its sibling: "happier than she'd known a person was let to be." The cliff is being climbed on purpose again. Also: she's LOSING WEIGHT. Bras looser. She was finally eating — the field hand appetite — and now the note about the tomato she ate anyway, "there was still a tomato on her plate. She ate that too" — that's not nothing. That's the book planting a flag I don't like. Is the happiness making her forget meals, or is something else going on? And Randi took ONE bite of pastry and set it down. The two of them are converging from opposite directions on the same broken table and I'm scared.
