REACTION

I need to talk about the shoe store, but I can't get there until I talk about Randi kneeling on that pale floor.

Because here's what the book did, and it took me two reads to catch it: Randi asked about the dress for weeks. She's had every secondhand account — brunch, lunch, the campaign, the toenails. And now the actual object is hanging on a brass hook six feet from her, and she asks to SEE it, and then she asks Vee to PUT IT ON, and then she touches it. Two fingers on the fold of the skirt, following the bias seam up Vee's body to the bare shoulder. She's not feeling the charmeuse. She's reading the dress. She knows the fabric by name — charmeuse, good weight — because she knows clothes, and she's reading the cut the way a tailor reads it, which means she is reading HIM through it, his hands, his weeks of work, and she says "He didn't make this for you. He made it to you." And I sat there knowing what she's holding in her hand is the thing she was in bed with him about. The dress is the child of the bench conversation and the fitting and the oyster yardage, and she's running her knuckle up its seam with Vee warm underneath it. The scene is played as girlfriend-shopping and it's the most physical contact Randi and Vee have had since the curb kiss, and neither of them names it. The thumb across the toenail — "Mm." — that's Randi touching the plum, the polish she cross-examined into existence, the polish that was on Pace's chest, and she doesn't know that last part, and I DO, and I had to put the book down. "Felt it go all the way up the length of her." Randi does it and says NOTHING. That's the woman who can't ask. That's the whole book.

The stats class first, though. Both of them turned toward her and not the professor — I love the book for making the literal structure of the chapter the structure of her life: Vee in the middle, flanked. And Simpson's paradox sitting there like a landmine. "Each part on its own says one thing; put together, the conclusion reverses." That's not a cereal-box lecture, that's the book telling me Vee's whole read of her life is about to reverse when the parts get aggregated — every individual fact she's collected about Pace (kind, attentive, asks first) and Randi (warm, generous, loves me) is true, and the aggregate is the trap. The professor "letting them go" after handing her the key to her own future. If the book doesn't pay that off I'll eat the Kindle.

And the shopping. The shopping! This is the lightest the book has been since the water fight, and it's doing the darkest work. Vee in her own store, on her own ground, being funny — the yeti shoe, the one-brisk-walk-from-a-sandal — and getting Randi's real laugh, the one that costs her something. I fell for both of them all over again. And then the same-feet thing. "Same size, same everything." Randi DELIGHTED by it. The intimacy of shared shoes — "it's practically a marriage" — is Randi getting a piece of Vee she can actually own: Vee's literal footprint matches hers. I felt that land in my chest and then felt sick two beats later when the boutique scene started, because I knew what was coming for that debit card.

The credit card scene is the most quietly frightening thing this book has done to Vee's body since the truck. She knows she doesn't have "the paying-it-back part." She signs anyway, and lets none of it reach her face — the girl who ran the TJMaxx math her whole life performing solvency in an expensive hush, and the book writes the whole class wound into one gesture: "she stood there holding a quiet the exact shape of what was in her account." That's chapter fourteen's shame machinery running on money instead of sex. And Randi beside her ringing up a multiple of it without glancing down — the gulf on full display, and Randi either not seeing the flinch or choosing not to. And Randi steering her there. "Which is exactly why you're not buying that." Randi killed the sensible shoe. Randi is the one who said "walk in late and stop the room cold" — she's authoring the induction fantasy, feeding the daydream I was already afraid of. The shoes are the first big debt of Vee's adult life and Randi poured the concrete.

"I watched him sleep." She said that to Randi. The most intimate sentence she owns, handed to the one person in the world who is sleeping with him. And Randi's hand stayed at the shoulder seam and "for a breath Randi was somewhere else, behind her own face" — and Vee read it as jealousy OF him, and felt pity, and the pity is real tenderness aimed at the wrong target entirely. The dramatic irony in this book keeps finding new floors. "Love you, girl." Oh, Vee. Oh no.
