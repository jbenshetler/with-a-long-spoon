REACTION — Oh, this chapter. This is the one where the book proved Sheri was never a rival — she was the antidote. I spent the first half of it with my stomach in my shoes right alongside Vee: the blonde she built out of her own jealousy, the armory buckled on piece by piece, the induction heels on a January taproom floor. And then Sheri is five foot nothing with crooked makeup and roots coming in, and the look she gives Vee isn't the pricing look or the placing look — it's *enjoyment*, pure, unhurried, and Vee's laugh comes out of her before she's voted on it. I actually put the book down for a second at "the look had been doing the whole unhurried trip down. It was enjoying her." Because that's the book's whole thesis in one gesture, isn't it — being *seen* completely, and the shame turning into something else. Except this time the seeing came free. No plan underneath it. Nobody steered Vee into Sheri's path. Sheri just liked her on sight, legs first, and there is something almost violent about how much relief that gave me — one relationship in this whole novel with no handler behind it.

And the blush. THE BLUSH. I have watched this man be easy in every room for sixty chapters and "you get to motorboat them things" turned his ears red. That's the most disarming single beat in the book for me, because it's the first time I've seen him *be the object* — Sheri has some license issued to nobody else on earth, and I love that Vee got to watch it.

Then Sheri's folder. "Whole tablecloth come off the table and nobody caught a dish." I wasn't ready. The whole coming-out story told flat, road-condition voice, "don't you start either" — and then Pace on the porch with coffee and donuts, fixing a lock that had been promised since August, no false words, no *they'll come around*. And then she hands Vee the Daphne drawer without knowing it: went bad with his daddy, hasn't been home since he was sixteen. TWO LINES. That's all anybody gets, and Sheri's had two years. That's not withholding for drama's sake — that's exactly how much of him there is to have, and it recontextualizes the whole book: this man learned to *show up* because nobody showed up for him. The headlight thing at the end — sitting on a dark road until the porch light comes on — Vee watching him do the thing he does for everyone he keeps, and him never once mentioning it. I was a little bit in love with him by the last page and I've been keeping him at finger's-length all book.

The truck ride home wrecked me gently: filthy versions of two radio songs, Vee laughing until the seatbelt got involved, "bacon cheeseburgers," to the window, to nobody. And her drunk observation that with him affection and ballast were never two different things — that's the truest sentence about their whole dynamic the book has said out loud.

What I'm chewing on, though, sitting here at 1 a.m.: Pace did this on purpose. "There's somebody I'd like you to meet" — the man who curated her entire year just introduced her to the one person in his life who wants nothing off him. Was that an offering or a test? And do I care, if the result is this? Also: Sheri is now the only unmanaged person in Vee's orbit, and Randi doesn't manage her — that collision with the hall dance next Saturday, all three women possibly in one room, is loaded. And the perfume of dread that never landed — "the heat stayed exactly where it was. It had never needed the blonde." Vee's jealousy found no purchase because Sheri gave it nowhere to stand. The book keeps doing this: every door Vee's afraid of turns out to be warm on the other side. Which makes me dread the one that isn't.
