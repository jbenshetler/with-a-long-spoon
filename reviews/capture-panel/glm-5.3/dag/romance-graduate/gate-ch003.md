REACTION — Okay. I need a minute.

I spent chapter one falling for Pace a little, honestly — the apple slices cut thin because she eats them faster that way, the water bottle left in advance, *I point, you pick* — a man who pays attention as a love language, finally, instead of another brooding billionaire with a tragic eyebrow. And the bench scene was genuinely hot; the edging-until-the-performance-breaks thing did something to me. Then chapter two flipped the lens to Randi and I got the vertigo the book was clearly selling: the bathroom mirror, "the face was lying," her putting the real girl back in the box forever. That was the moment I texted my friend "this book is actually *about* something."

And now chapter three comes for my throat.

Because here's the thing — I know this girl. I was *surrounded* by this girl in college. The lush one who laughs with her whole throat to the sky, the one Randi's whole standards-board eye "would find nothing in it to grade." That's Vee. That's the heroine from the blurb. And I watched her get *selected* like a dish Randi hadn't ordered, and I felt the exact same split the book is running on me: the walk itself was charged and fun — his mouth at Randi's ear, "what about her?", Randi getting wetter on the quadrangle than she ever lets herself be in daylight, the moment she stops being a good sport and starts *shopping*. As erotica, the pointing game is one of the better seduction devices I've read in years; it's a threesome-interest book where the wanting is actually *in* the woman, not performed for the man, and Pace clocking Randi's "only in a threesome, with you" as a gorgeous act of self-protection — handing the appetite to him so she doesn't have to own it — that's the psychological exactness the jacket promised. I underlined it.

But I have the blurb. I know Vee doesn't know there's a plan.

So the last line — "*Do you think she'll like the bench?*" — landed like cold water down my back. The bench from chapter one. The bench that broke Randi open and left her crying facedown in leather and locking bathroom doors. And Randi, lit up, grinning, half in love with a girl she collided with *on purpose*, is asking about it with her daylight voice while I'm standing there holding the knowledge of what happened the last time that question got answered. The dramatic irony is doing exactly what the blurb said it would: I'm falling for it as Vee is going to, ahead of her, complicit, thrilled, and a little sick about it.

The one thing I'll say against the chapter: the pacing of the game itself — point, verdict, point, verdict — went maybe two women long for me. I get why; it's training the reader in Randi's taste before Vee detonates it. But the beanie girl and the blazer girl and the matched athletes all blurred a touch on me.

Doesn't matter. That ending bought it all back. This is the first book in a year where I put the Kindle down and just sat there.
