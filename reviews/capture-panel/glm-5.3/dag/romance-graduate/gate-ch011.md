REACTION — Okay. I sat with this one for a minute before I could even text anyone, and then I texted my friend Dana at like 11:40: "I just read a sex scene that was two buttons and a flannel and I need to lie down."

Because here's the thing — nothing "happened." Nobody touched anybody. He didn't lay a finger on her, she got five buttons into a wet shirt and then put his flannel ON, and it's the most charged thing I've read all year. I've read four hundred shirt-ripping scenes. This one undid me with a man standing outside his own truck in the rain so she could change with privacy, and the book KNEW I'd figure out why before she did — "Then she looked down at the flannel in her lap" — and it still landed like a little bell. He gave her the room and she used the room to put on a show for him. That's the whole book in one cab.

And the mother-voice as fuel. God. "The voice said *floozy* and her thighs pressed once, hard, on their own." I have never — I have genuinely never seen the shame-voice written as the thing that makes it HOT instead of an obstacle to be therapized away. She's not overcoming it, she's *coming with it*, it's part of the engine. That's the psychological exactness the blurb promised and it's actually delivering.

The moleskin scene had me from the first sentence. Her stomach clenching because he's about to see her sock. And him going *hmm* like she's a problem in an equation. And then — the pit. She spits the pit and he picks it up and doesn't look at her doing it, and she has it now, and "he hadn't made her small to give it to her." I actually had to put the Kindle down. That's teaching-as-tenderness and it's the same hand that broke Daphne, I know it is, and I don't care, I'm in.

The dress. The russet dress she sets back on the shelf, and he says "That sounds like a beautiful dress" with no weight on it at all, and doesn't pick it up again. A worse book has him buying it for her next chapter and her crying. I'm watching that spot like a hawk. The fact that I *don't* know what he'll do with it is why I trust the book.

And the end — the siren eating her sentence, her body going before she decided to go. The book keeps doing this: her body as the honest one, the voice as the liar, and now the world itself interrupting at the worst moment. He was shaking. He was shivering the whole time she was performing for him and neither of them knew, and she caught his hand mid-reach-to-her-mouth and put it on her belly instead — that re routing, want turning into care without losing any of the heat — that's the grown-up thing I keep begging these books for.

Still simmering underneath: this is all built on the pointing game. Every generous thing he does reads double to me — the moleskin, the sandwich made at 6 a.m., the flannel packed "for himself." Was it packed for himself or was it packed for her? The game was his idea as much as Randi's. But the shaking was real. The cold was real. The book keeps insisting both things are true and I keep believing it.
