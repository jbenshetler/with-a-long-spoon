REACTION — Oh, that hurt. That hurt in a way this book hasn't hurt yet, because I've been waiting for the "I love you" the way Vee has, and the book just made me feel exactly what it costs to hand someone your best sentence and get a beautiful answer that isn't the answer.

The cracker-and-meal sentence. Weeks of her assembling it — I *felt* the rehearsal, the heat coming up the back of her neck just imagining it, the way she'd checked it against the unsayable words to make sure it couldn't be mistaken for them. And it's such a perfect sentence. It's her whole self in one breath: the starving, the being fed, the waking up. And his hand does that thing — "two different responses... put both down before either had finished" — and the second breath she'd never heard him take, and I sat up straight right there with her, thinking *oh, it landed, it landed, he's going to—*

"I love how good we are together."

God. The precision of that dodge. It's *about her* — "what your body does, I've never" — it's warm, it's probably even true, and it is exactly one centimeter to the left of what she said. And the book does the cruelest, most honest thing: it doesn't tell me whether he understood and stepped aside on purpose, or whether it genuinely went past him. "Maybe he understood and chose to say it like that. Maybe it had gone past her entirely." Either end of it hurts. I flipped between both readings for the whole laundry room scene and I still can't settle, and I don't think I'm supposed to.

And here's the thing that has my stomach in knots: he's running a con. He and Randi planned her. Is "I love how good we are together" a man who can't say it because the plan is in his mouth? Or a man who's stopped believing the plan and can't say it because it would be true? The jacket says "how real it would get" — and this chapter is the first time I've felt the con and the love occupying the exact same sentence. He heard a love confession and gave her one back that a con artist could say safely. Or that a guarded man could say honestly. I can't tell, and that ambiguity is the whole book in a single exchange.

What broke me most, though, is her filing system. "She put it where she put things she did not know what to do with. The place was not empty." Other flickers across the fall, "small repair work." So there's a *pile* — a little drawer of doubts inside the woman who got the top drawer — and she doesn't look at it. That's the con's long game showing up in miniature: nothing taken from her she didn't hand over, and she keeps handing over her own warnings. The laundry room is the first time I've watched her *know* she's doing it — "she did not argue with it" — and choose the warm quilt over the question. That's not steamrolling; it's self-governed silence, which is somehow worse and more real.

Also — the echo with last chapter is so quiet I almost missed it: Cassie asked "what is he, to you," and Vee couldn't fit him in any frame. Two days later she can't even say love to his face. The people she trusts most are both owed a word she can't produce. The middle she keeps from Cassie and the sentence she can't give Pace are the same silence, aren't they.

This is the first chapter where Pace did something I can't redeem with "but look how he holds her." The holding was gorgeous — the held kiss on her hair, longer than his usual — but I felt the *wait* behind her breastbone and my body did the same unnamed thing hers did in the laundry room. I'm not mad at the book. This is the merciless tenderness the jacket promised, and I'd rather have this ache than ten easy confessions. But I'm watching him now in a way I wasn't on Sunday.
