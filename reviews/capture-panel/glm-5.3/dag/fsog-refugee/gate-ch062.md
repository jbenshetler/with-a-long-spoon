REACTION — I'm sitting here with my hand over my mouth. I thought this was going to be a hangover chapter — cute, domestic, him with the bread and the water and the paper towel under the glass so it wouldn't mark the wood (that paper towel, god, that's the whole man, he thinks of the *wood*) — and then it turned into the loneliest thing I've read in years.

"One friend. There isn't anyone else." I actually had to put the book down for a second. Because Vee's argument is the reader's argument — you *watched* him at that party, everyone glowing around him, Cassie saying "you can stay," and you thought the same thing she did: this man collects people effortlessly. And the answer is no. That was hosting. That was the invisible-hosting thing I've been calling his love language since the roadhouse, and it turns out it was never reciprocal because it *couldn't* be — the door was on a timer and it closed when he was fifteen. "You make a friend from the middle of the pack — you lose to him, and lose again, and come out the other side friends. I was never once in the pack." That's the most quietly devastating explanation of loneliness I've ever read, and he delivers it like a fact about soil conditions. "It isn't tragic." Yes it is, you enormous fool. He believes it the way he believed Daphne was luck — the good order is the tell. The book said that and I felt it like a hand on my own chest.

And the arithmetic Vee does about Daphne — I'd been holding that ugly word under my tongue too, and now the book has laid the shape of it right out where I can't not see it: a boy alone in a building of grown men, "weren't safe or weren't welcoming," and the one person who held all of him held it *because* of the wrong thing. And then she left, sensibly, and there was no one left to take the heartbreak to. I keep thinking about him on a porch with coffee and donuts and a screwdriver, fixing Sheri's lock — "that's all a body can be for you at a point like that. There." Because nobody was there for him. He knows exactly how it goes.

The hug at the end undid me. "She didn't say she was sorry, because sorry would have asked him to hold still and be comforted" — that's it, that's the exact right instinct, that's Vee learning his grammar and speaking it back. Cheek flat to his chest with her whole weight committed, the way Sheri did it, and him looking over her head at the middle distance, neither happy nor sad, just letting it happen. He doesn't try to make it better. Neither does the book. Nobody cries and nobody rescues anybody and it's the tenderest chapter so far.

Small worried note in the margin: his one friend is a woman who can never want him — "the only kind the world had ever quite let him keep." And now he has Vee, and Randi, and Sheri. The pack is being built around him whether he consents to it or not, and I love watching it. Also the blush, twice, last chapter — I'm carrying that forward. Pace blushing might be my favorite thing in the whole book.

No heat this chapter and I don't care. This is the chapter I'd have been furious to miss.
