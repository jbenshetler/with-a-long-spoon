REACTION — I need to just... sit here a second. My heart is going.

First things first — the beginning, where she gets herself off to the thought of Randi and stops herself and says "I have a boyfriend" like a girl grabbing a stair rail: that's the most honest internal writing this book has done. That's me at nineteen, that's everybody, the way you reach for the plain word because it's a *structure* and structures don't ask you questions. And then she gets in the car and doesn't turn the radio on because she doesn't want anything in the car that might start asking questions. God. That's the whole book in one line.

And here's the thing about that car ride: she drives to Pace specifically to crowd Randi out of her head. "She needed him the way you need a wall at your back." And it doesn't work — she comes with her face turned into his neck "so she wouldn't have to see him see her." Vee used him tonight, flat out, and the book KNOWS it and says it to my face: "she was not trying to have him. She was trying to get free of something." That's ugly and true and I actually gasped, because this is a heroine doing something genuinely unkind to a man she loves, and the book is not going to soften it. That's the opposite of the genre's problem — nobody's acted upon here, including him.

And he caught her. In the dark, mid-act, he felt the wrong engine in her body — "that was angry, Vee" — and he ASKED. That's my man. That's the thing I came back for: a dominant who reads her like weather and then opens a door instead of a window. "Did something happen at the mixer?" No accusation, no ego, just attention.

The confession scene — I'm almost embarrassed how it got me, because structurally it's a filth scene, but it's actually a therapy session with penetration. Him making her buy her own pleasure inch by inch in the coin of truth — and the TRUTHS. "I didn't try that hard to get my hands back." "There's no way I wasn't." "I wanted her mouth on me." Each one pulled out of her a half-second before she'd have tidied it, and the book tracks exactly which ones cost her. And the moment where he stops trading and just *takes her* — and then stops at the edge and waits — "You finish when you've finished." I had to put the book down and breathe. But here's what made it bearable, made it HOT instead of ugly: the coin she's paying in is HER OWN truth. He's not extracting something for himself — he already has her — he's extracting it for HER, from her, into the light. He's doing what she can't do for herself, which every good top in this genre is secretly supposed to be doing. And the proof is right there: her body trusts the rule completely, "it felt right, it only made her body needier." She consents to the game with her whole skin. Nothing hidden, nothing coerced. This is what I wanted and couldn't find for years — the control that SERVES her.

But the end. The end, the end, the end.

"Do you want to sleep with a woman, Vee?" — and he says it without the name, stepping around "Randi" like a puddle, and she's so grateful for the room he left her that she walks straight into it — the room, not the truth. And the book hands me that little narrative wink at his expense: she notices this is the first time he's ever defended himself in an argument, "the sweetest thing she had ever seen him do," and it settles over her "like a warm blanket" — and I'm sitting there with my teeth gritted going, no, honey, that's not sweet, that's a FLINCH. The man who never covers himself covered himself *once*, and it was about Randi, and you're both going to find out why eventually, and I'm dreading it on her behalf. And then — "she'd gotten away with it," and the reader knows exactly how much she hasn't gotten away with: he's hard again while she's confessing, "gone hard off her shame," and she clocks it and files it under *glad*. She's lying there holding two facts that don't touch: he asked "anything else about Randi" and she said "No. Nothing." — level as a countertop — AND she knows about the goodbye kisses drifting from cheek to open mouth, and her hand and the name in her head. She just performed a lie and narrated her own rule back at herself to justify it. My girl is in the drawer, and the drawer is closing on her own hand.

And the last two pages. "She'd had the best there was... five, six times since Friday... and she was lying in his arms... and her mind gone straight down the county road and back to campus and into a warm cluttered room with a girl in it, and she could not make it come back." Tuesday circled on the calendar like a fate. That's not a cliffhanger, that's a diagnosis, and it's hers, and she said it herself: *What is wrong with me?* — and the answer, which the reader already has and she doesn't, is sitting at brunch with perfect lipstick and called LOVE on her wrist.

I was going to say the heat was peak and the romance was a turn — both true — but the thing I'm actually going to carry around all week is how warm and doomed this chapter managed to be at the same time. The confession is real intimacy, the sex is the most charged in the book, the aftercare is water and the grey blanket and his mouth against her hair — and on top of all that comfort she's lying in the dark, held, warm, and her mind is in another room with another person. That's the most grown-up thing this book has done. Nothing taken from her that she hasn't handed over — except the things she's taking from herself.
