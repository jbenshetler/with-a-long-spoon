**REACTION**

Okay. I had to put the book down twice during this chapter and both times it was because of a pencil.

"Asked for it with his eyes first" — and then he gives the pencil *back* at the exact line where she can finish it herself, and the narration says she hadn't known she was allowed. I felt that in my sternum. That's the whole thing I read for, isn't it, and here it is doing its work in broad daylight in a coffee shop with nobody's clothes even off — a man who could solve it in two lines choosing not to, keeping himself out of it so the solving stays hers. And then he asks to call her — *may* I — and she hears the word the way you hear a word that doesn't belong to the room, and I heard it that way too, because I've read so much of this genre where the man just *takes*, where taking is the whole erotic charge, and this book keeps insisting the charge lives somewhere else. In the asking. In the nod arriving before her mouth catches up. I'm not made of stone; I sat there with my hand on the page like she had hers on it.

And of course — of course — the Euler's identity thing is the thesis of the entire book wearing a turtleneck. Two things that won't give on their own, that keep sending you in circles, and you put them together at right angles and they become a single thing that's suddenly plain. *You already know every piece of this.* He's describing Vee-and-Randi to Vee. He's describing all three of them. I don't know if I'm supposed to be clapping or terrified, honestly, I landed on both.

Because underneath all the sweetness I can't stop hearing chapter three. Randi texting someone with "the private, absorbed focus of a girl writing to someone who has her attention," and Vee thinking *some boy,* amused. And Randi — Randi who does a whole practiced bit with her name for every stranger she meets — handing this man her name as one bare word. No bit. No adjective. She gave Cassie's flat nothing more performance than she gave him. And the two fake sips, and the hand in her lap, and her coaching Vee afterward like the world's most devoted matchmaker — *a man doesn't forget his coffee over a girl he isn't going to call* — when she IS the mechanism, when the coffee shop scene was blocked out on a quad a week ago. The cruelty here is so quiet I keep double-checking I'm actually seeing it. She watched her friend get charmed by her own lover and her only tell was not doing her name-bit.

Cassie, though. Cassie is the one honest instrument in this whole orchestra. "Goodbye, Vee" last chapter — the full word, flat and finished — and now the patient flat look while Randi thumbs at her phone, and she's the one who *names* the look on Pace's face for Vee, the one who says out loud, he forgot there was anyone else in the room. Except the joke is Cassie's also wrong: the great-aunt glance at Randi is a performance too, two people who've done worse than kiss performing strangers at each other, and Cassie has no way to know she's standing in the middle of a play. Every time Cassie opens her mouth I get a little scared for her.

"Substitution." It took me most of the chapter to hear the title — the math move, turn the thing in front of you into something you already know — and then the other meaning arrived and I sat with it. There was no chance meeting. There was a substitution: a staged one slid into the place where a real one should be, so smoothly Vee will never feel the seam. And here's the thing that keeps me up about it: her joy is not fake. The elation, the stairs-too-fast chest, the "it was good" — all of it is genuinely hers, every yes she'll give will be genuinely hers, and the book told me on the jacket that that's the problem. I signed up knowing. Watching it happen to her is a specific ache — like watching someone walk down a beautiful hallway you know the floor plan of and they don't.

Do you think she'll like the bench. That's where every soft thing in this chapter is pointed. I keep whispering it at the page.
