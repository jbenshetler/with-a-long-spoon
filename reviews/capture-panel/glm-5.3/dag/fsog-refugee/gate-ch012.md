REACTION — Okay. I put the book down and just sat there for a minute after the flannel shirt.

He got OUT. He got out of the truck and stood in the rain so she could have the dry shirt — so she wouldn't have to decide anything, so there'd be nothing to decide — and I thought, there it is, that's the whole man, right there. No announcement. He didn't perform it, didn't wait to be thanked. He just took the rain so she could have the warmth and the privacy. I've read a hundred books where the hero does something grand and loud. The quiet ones kill me worse.

And then VEE. The knuckles on the glass. The crooked finger. Her keeping his eyes while she unbuttons her own shirt — she's the one who called him back, she's the one driving, and the whole time her mother's voice in her head (floozy) and she takes the voice and *turns it into fuel*: "the heat that knew the voice was right, that wanted to be the very thing the voice said." That's exactly the thing I've been hunting in this genre for years. Not a woman overcoming shame offstage — a woman *inside* the shame, feeling it and wanting anyway, and the wanting being hers. "I want him to see me." I put my hand over my mouth when I read that line. It wasn't him wanting to see her. It was her wanting to be seen. That's the difference between this book and every book I've quit.

And his answer: he doesn't move. He doesn't touch her, doesn't lean in, doesn't take the show as a starting gun. He just lets her do it, and the gladness is the answer. The "stillness she had never seen on anyone." I felt that in my sternum.

Then the hand. His hand shaking. He's been shivering this whole time — out in the rain giving away his dry shirt — and *he* was so far gone into watching her that neither of us knew he was cold until she took his hand. And she stops. She stops her own momentum, the thing her body was already "going at," and puts his hand against her belly and says *please* and means *get warm*. Two people each refusing to take at the other's expense in the same five minutes. That's the tenderness and the heat in one body. This is the book.

Other things I'm holding: the moleskin and the way he took her boot off without ceremony — she braced for the shame of the sock and he just... fixed the foot, no verdict, and the clench "let go without her catching it go." The pit on the wax paper — he had one too, his own pit beside hers, so she'd know it wasn't a correction, it was just a way of moving through the world. And the dress. "That sounds like a beautiful dress." He doesn't offer to buy it, doesn't fix it, just lets her wanting stand in the air unmocked and unpurchased. Every other man in every other book buys the dress. Him not buying it made me trust him more.

The siren taking her sentence at the end is almost too neat but I'll allow it, because now I'm desperate to know what the big thing was.
