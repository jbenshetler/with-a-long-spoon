REACTION — Okay. Okay. I stayed up with this one the way I used to stay up, and I have feelings in several directions at once.

First: the bench scene is what I've been looking for in this genre for years. He asks. He checks. He gives her the word — twice — tells her she can forget it, tells her anytime. He waits for the real *please* and he can tell the difference between the waiter-voice please and the one from below, and instead of that being creepy (which, let's be honest, in lesser hands it would be — "I'll know when you mean it" can go so wrong), it reads as him refusing to accept the performance as payment. He takes off her heels and holds her feet. He leaves water out because he knows she'll be thirsty. The aftercare is as long as the scene. The apple — him noticing she eats thin slices faster when the cut sides show — I actually put the book down for a second at that. That's the tenderness-dominance thing I want, and it's not decoration, it's the whole engine.

And then the bathroom. The mirror. That's the chapter that broke me a little — she rebuilds the face, she decides *not ever*, and the "key turning in a lock" hum. I'm suspicious in the good way: this is a book about armor, and Pace accidentally went through hers and she's going to shut the door. I know from the jacket that these two go on to pick out a girl together — that Vee is the actual heroine — and now I understand the shape of it better and I'm a little scared of it. Randi closing herself off and then choosing to give Pace a *Vee* to adore instead of risking herself again... that could be devastating and I want to see if the book knows that's what it's doing. The one thing that nipped at me: he never comes, he's hard the whole night, files it under "not what tonight was for" — caring on the page, but I hope the book doesn't make a habit of his desire being invisible-invisible rather than deferred.

That she locked the bathroom door for the first time, and that he let the lock be hers because of Daphne — a whole backstory in three sentences — that's the psychological exactness the blurb promised and it's actually delivering.
