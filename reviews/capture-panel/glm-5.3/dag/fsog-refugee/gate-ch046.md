**REACTION**

Oh, this book. *This book.* I said in my notes the Randi bill was coming and would be the cruelest chapter and the tenderest at once, and here it is, and it's worse and better than I braced for, because it's not the bill yet — it's Randi alone in Gstaad getting shown exactly what her whole armored life has on offer, and it's *nothing*, and she knows it, and she goes back to the nothing anyway.

The breakfast with the parents told me everything in one page. "Eating her revulsion." The invented boyfriends, "safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table." That's the machine that built her — a mother who's all edges and points, a father with the light at his back. Of course she runs cons. It's the only language that table speaks. And then the book does this devastating thing where John, the faultless man, is just... the final form of the con. He conjures the table, conjures the sleigh, and at the hotel door she *notices* — "he had never asked her where the night was going. He had assumed it" — and here's the line that cut me: **"Pace had never assumed anything in his life. Pace made her say it, out loud, every time. She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked."** And I sat there going — yes, and that's the trap, Randi. That's exactly the trap. She liked not being asked because not being asked costs nothing. And then the book pays it off in bed: the pleasure "real, complete, and gone before the plate was cleared," a good sandwich, and she's cold everywhere except the seam where she touches him. That's what not being asked gets you. Nothing put on the table, nothing won.

And the waltz — "Easier was better." Pace's lead as the lightest pressure she had to be *listening* for, leaving the rest of the way to her, "and that was more work, and tonight she was done with the work." That's the whole book in a dance comparison. The thing I read for — the man who makes room — is the thing Randi experiences as *work*, and tonight she opts out of it, and the opting out feels like a warm bath until she's lying awake in the cold she brought in with her. "The flaw was hers, carried into the room the same as the cold." God. I don't even know if that's true and neither does she, and the book isn't settling it, and I love it for not settling it.

Then the ending and I had to put the book down. "She could tell her." She's run every scenario her whole life and she cannot run the one where she tells Vee the truth over eggs, because Vee is the first person she's ever said "I love you" to *without a private qualifier*. "She was not brave enough to lose her. She had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be." I felt that in my chest. That's the most honest sentence this book has written about anyone. And then Vee's text — *i'm here if you want to talk. or not.* — that "or not" is so Vee, so Cassie-taught, the kindness that doesn't demand — and Randi answers with the fairytale, "every word of it true, none of it the thing," and *miss your face most though, gorgeous.* The cover word doing its cover work. The lipstick left on him and nothing on her mouth. "Nothing had happened to her eyes."

And I can't not notice the rhyme the book just built: Pace wouldn't give Vee the word, and now Randi won't give Vee the word, and Vee is home in Ohio holding a warm phone getting the fairytale from *both* of them. Everyone keeps one true thing from the person they trust most — my notes said that was the engine and here's the engine firing on all cylinders. The jacket's "every yes was freely given, that was the problem" — Randi choosing the con shape of her own love, choosing to let the word keep its cover — that's her yes, freely given, to the wrong arrangement, because the true one requires the bravery she's never stocked.

Also, filed under things I'm watching: the white-gold bracelet that never comes off, locked, kept out of the light at her mother's table. That bracelet is going to mean something. And John — cotillion-good, sureness "a person did not come by young," law school in New Haven — the book handed me a rich, practiced, assuming man and I don't trust him one inch, though honestly I think he's already served his purpose, which was to be the control group. He's the answer to the semester's experiment: the whole approved life, laid out and lit, and she felt nothing.

Still no wink, still no steamrolled no — John assumed and the book named the assumption and let her walk *into* it with her eyes open, which is its own kind of consent chapter, just a sad one. My line holds.
