REACTION:

I read this one with my heart in my throat the whole way, and I keep going back to the line about the word she wanted having "only ever been air." The key. He still won't say it — the book keeps finding him new ways to say everything *around* it — and this time Vee *watched herself* let it go, twice, and I could feel how practiced she's getting at not reaching. That's not resolution, that's a pressure valve she's learning to live on, and I don't trust it to hold.

The cabin opening was pure warmth — the tikka masala in the thermos, the cider pairing he "sat at home and planned," her being a little angry about the soup. "You don't have to understand it to eat it" is the most Pace sentence in seventy chapters. And the box: oiled wood, made by his hands, and the key inside — first key he's ever cut for anyone, and he spent it here instead of on the word. Her noting *house* and waiting half a beat for *home* and the other one not coming — the book did that to us on purpose, the little cadence of it, and it landed exactly where it wanted.

And then the blindfold scene. I have read a lot of body-worship scenes in this genre and I have never read one like this, because the whole engine of it was him narrating her *back* to herself — every shame she's carried since girlhood named and turned over in the light and wanted on purpose. The spill is the part I want most. "The most honest thing I've ever stood in front of." Her oldest shames — the wet, the answering body, "the kind of girl who" — and he takes each one and hands it back as a marvel instead of a verdict. The flannel blindfold made from the shirt she sleeps in: he knew what that would smell like to her, and so did I, and I had to put the book down for a second.

Consent discipline, checked line by line, because this chapter ran its hardest test since the stacked no: the blindfold, asked. The bows — "I want you to undo them. Not me. You. When you're ready. There's no hurry in this house." Her feet opening before her fear finished its sentence and that being *hers*, the book keeping the wanting unambiguously inside her. And then the "do you want relief / go ahead" turn — that moment where his hands didn't come and she had to find her own — I held my breath, because that's exactly where a lesser book would have steamrolled, and instead the whole thing was built on waiting. Gentle, total waiting, until the reaching was hers. And the "What are you thinking about?" breaking her open on "you, you, you" — that undid me. Sixty-something chapters of her mouth acting before her committees convene, and here it spent her whole evening's hoard in one syllable.

Then the ask at the end, in the dark, while she's warm and full of him and barely attending — "would you tell me, when you touch yourself thinking of me?" — and her "yes" coming out "the way you say yes to something small." That's the book doing something and I can feel it. Given what chapter 67 showed us — the hand she held flat on her stomach, the *name* she wouldn't let arrive with it — that promise is a tripwire with a timer on it. What happens the first time she has to tell him and the *you* in the dark isn't only him? The book knows. I know. She doesn't. It's December's nerve all over again, the Christmas-shape in a party dress, and I'm genuinely a little scared of it, which is the best thing a chapter can do to me at this point in a book.

Nothing here tripped my line. Not once. He asked, she answered, her body led and the book never once winked at me about what she "really" meant. Even the taking — him past grace, graceless and frustrated — was earned on the page as *her* power, and the book said so out loud. I'm warm, I'm wrung out, and I'm already turning the page, but I'm turning it with one eye on the promise she made with her hand already on him.
