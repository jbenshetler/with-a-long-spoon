REACTION:

Oh, this chapter. It undid me in a completely different way than the porch chapter did, and I wasn't ready.

Two chapters ago I watched him fail Vee in the worst way a good man can fail — "words are cheap" and then nothing, standing there with his arms folded while she cried. And I've been carrying my judgment of that. Forty chapters of him being the anti-Fifty-Shades, asking, waiting, folding his things into the bottom drawer — and then the one moment it counts, the wall came up. I was angry. And now the book takes me *inside* the wall, and it's not cold in there. It's just stuck.

Because here's the thing: he knows. Sheri says "not saying it matters more to you than having her" and he *looks down*. He has the whole diagnosis laid out in front of him by a five-foot woman stealing his fries, and he can't walk through the door. And the book still hasn't told me why. "I don't go back." Whatever's behind that door — the parents, Daphne, the scar — it's grown so heavy in my head that "I can't say love" now feels less like withholding and more like a man whose machinery for saying it broke somewhere I haven't been shown yet. That's the thing that keeps me: I don't think he's withholding to hold power. I think he's withholding because something in him genuinely can't, and the tragedy is that from where Vee stands it looks identical.

But — and this is where I get a little furious on Vee's behalf again — Sheri is right and she says it better than anyone: "That's a hell of a lot of reasons for a man who's already got the girl." He lays up his bricks — "I'm there, every weekend, I told her she was the most important thing in my life" — and every brick is true, and he's still answering a question she didn't ask. He does the same thing on the porch with "I love how good we are together." He hears the question and hands back evidence. The man who's made an entire love language out of construction can't, apparently, do the one verbal thing, and watching him fail it twice in three chapters is a special kind of ache.

Sheri, though. SHERI. I officially retract every suspicious eye I kept on her. She's the first person in this book who wants nothing from him — not even his attention, really — and she gave him the best and truest scene he's had all book. "Don't waste the good stuff on me" when he says the grief line! She can tell when he's spending real material. And the detail about her own Paige, "couldn't get right with me not talking to my people" — the irony she won't let him touch — the book just quietly confirmed the shape of Pace's wound by giving us its mirror: grief makes a person stop going back, and people who've never lost anything think it's a choice. Both of them have locked doors, and they like each other *because* of it, no repair attempted. That's a friendship drawn with real economy.

Also — the Elvis in the tabletop, the kid who gave the double to Sheri and the single to Pace, the chocolate shake she draws "like something owed." This book keeps doing that, the texture I'd never skip. And him thinking Vee "would either love this place or never see it at all, and he wanted very badly to know which" — the book is now hurting me with his interior, which it's been rationing for forty-seven chapters. I finally got his POV and it's a man who misses her, out loud, to a friend, admitting his face is hot over her.

And then that little dagger: Sheri telling him about hugging him hello at the dance hall *while Randi was on his arm*, Randi looking at her "like one more of your harem applicants." Randi watching women approach her co-conspirator and filing every one. He brought Randi to the dance as part of the game and Sheri saw her in the wild and I got a chill remembering the con has a whole social surface Vee never sees.

The structure is what's killing me: chapter 47, Vee alone at midnight, inventing a blonde. Chapter 48, Pace in a diner missing her, wanting to call, laying bricks. They're both starving and neither will send the text. Nobody is villain enough to leave and nobody is brave enough to break. That's the most honest picture of a stall between two people who love each other I've read in this genre, and it hurts in the good way.
