REACTION — Oh, this one *hurt*, and I mean that as praise. I read the whole last stretch of the bedroom scene with my hand literally over my mouth, because I know this girl now. I know exactly what she did. She built that sentence — crackers and a meal, the most beautiful thing she had in her — and said it, and he said "I love how good we are together," and she filed it, and it's been sitting in her like a splinter ever since, and tonight it came out sideways as "Is this what we are now?" in a voice too big for a dark bedroom. That's not a fight. That's a splinter working itself out wrong. I've been that woman. I've had that exact fight — the one where you're not angry about the restaurant, you're angry about the word, and you know it, and you say it anyway at the wrong size because the right size has been refused to you.

And here's what wrecked me: she said a cruel, untrue thing to him — "you only warm to me when you've got your way," which she *knows* is a lie, the book lets her know it ("some back room of her knew which of them had gone cold that night") — and he doesn't punish, doesn't get cold, doesn't perform wounded. He sets down two true things, quietly, and one of them is the whole man: "I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you." That's the TVs and the fork-squaring and the irritation he dropped his eyes to hide. This book has been showing me an autistic man through the food and the playlists and the careful hands for forty-eight chapters, and here it just *says* it, through his own mouth, to the woman who needed to hear it — and she can't take it in, because she's too busy needing the word. I want to shake her and I want to hold her, both at once.

The unforgivable-adjacent moment for me was almost the nachos — I felt the misery of "he had ruined it for her" as a *loss* before I felt it as character. But no, that's her, honestly rendered: you can't go back to billboards after paintings, and the book knows it cuts both ways.

The ending is the best and cruelest thing in the chapter. She takes him apart — his control, the last wall — and he still doesn't say it, and she lies in his arms "not able to take a bit of it in. It had no name." The chapter is called *Under the Rug* and it's about her pile of unspoken things, and mine too. Because here's my 1 a.m. text to my friend: the man has said "I don't go back," "I'd never share your photograph," "I'll be here," makes her a drawer, wears two shirts because she asked — and she's lying there counting the one word he won't say while the reader is sitting right there *drowning in all the words he does say*. The book is doing this on purpose. It's making me feel her blindness and his wordlessness at the same time, and I don't fully trust that he *can't* say it rather than that he's rationing it, and that ambiguity is the thing keeping me up. The kitchen chapter told me he says what's true and no more — so if he doesn't say it, either it isn't true yet, or it's true and sealed behind the same door as his parents. And I don't know which. That's the hook now.
