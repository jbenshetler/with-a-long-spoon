REACTION —

I had to put the book down twice during this chapter, and both times it was because of what Vee *didn't* say.

First: "You were so young." She does the arithmetic — nineteen and fifteen — and the ugly word starts forming in her and she holds it down under her tongue because "it was not hers to say." And the book lets her be right about that. She doesn't rescue him, doesn't therapize him, doesn't say sorry — she just fits herself against his side with her palm flat over the place the careful voice came from. That's the exact tenderness I read for. The whole scene where he lays out Daphne in "good order, every reason accounted for and set in its slot, and the good order was the tell" — I read that sentence twice. He handed her the thing that hurt him and named it luck, and *he doesn't hear himself doing it*, and she sees it and keeps her mouth closed and stays. That's love rendered as an act of restraint. That's the same discipline the man uses in bed, applied to a wound. God.

The scar. I've been waiting chapters for the scar and the book gives it to me like this — her fingers finding more of it than she knew, and his hand coming down over hers *not to move it* but to press it there and hold it. "Not tonight" with no wall in it, only a door with a time on it. I actually had to lie back and look at the ceiling at that. The asking and the answering contained in one hand over one hand. Nobody in this genre writes men like this — I've spent years wading through heroes who'd rather shove the heroine against a wall than let her touch a scar they haven't narrated yet.

Peter. His real name is Peter, and his mother has been surgically removed from every sentence about her, and he was fifteen in a dormitory full of grown men, and a teacher put his desk in the hall for a year because he asked questions she couldn't answer. "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment." Vee said that and he looked at her like she'd accidentally found the right place. I'm fully, hopelessly attached to this man now, and the book knows it, and the book also knows — and won't let me forget — that Randi was in his bed last chapter saying "she's not ready, you'll want to take your time," and that all of this openness, all of this earned intimacy, is happening inside a plan. That's the cruelty of this book. Chapter 24 and 25 back to back: the con, and the real thing, and the real thing IS real, I believe it completely when he says "I want her to get there on her own," and it was still Randi who walked her into his path.

Also: the math. The paint in the shape but never the outside of it. The coastline that gets longer the shorter your ruler. Newton's three sentences for the ball and the leaf and the stars. I don't care about math and I sat there *seeing* the stars as moving bodies because he did. When Vee says "it sounds a little lonely" and then realizes — "the same face" — that the way he's all-the-way-there with her is the lifter's face, no past no future, only this moment? That's the thesis of the whole romance in one observation. He wasn't taught presence by a guru; he built it out of survival, a fifteen-year-old in a hallway who found the one place with nothing in it but now.

The domestic frame kills me softly too — cold tea because they'd rather talk, her feet under his thigh, his palm just *holding* her breast under the quilt "as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm." That's not a sex scene and it's the most intimate thing in the book so far. The Ohio cumin exchange had me smiling — "affectionate" — he goes to the hand underneath the thing, every time.

My dread is unchanged and growing: the more real this gets, the higher the fall. But the line I'm holding the book to — every yes hers, no steamrolled no — is still clean as glass. The one flicker of unease is that "not tonight" was *his* no, and the book honored it, which honestly counts double in my ledger.
