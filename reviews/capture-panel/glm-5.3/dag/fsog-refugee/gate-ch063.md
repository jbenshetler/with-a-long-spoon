"Here was the shape of it with everything true scooped out of the middle." A stranger pressing her flat and saying "don't move" — the exact grammar Pace has taught her body, but with none of the love in it, none of the *want*, and her body can't tell the difference at first and then can, and she lies there aching for the right hand. And the detail that got me most wasn't the pain or the humiliation, it was that she *kept it back* — "kept the whole lit ruin of herself back behind her teeth where it belonged to him." She's developed a sense of what's his and what isn't, what's private and what's merely exposed, and she used it in a fluorescent room to protect something. That's not degradation. That's a woman who knows what she has.

The turn-over moment. "The part Randi hadn't said." I need to be honest — for about three paragraphs I was genuinely uneasy. Vee wanting to say no and *not saying it* because she'd already said yes three nights ago to a version of the thing that turned out to be smaller than the actual thing — that's the coercion shape I hate. And I watched the book very carefully to see if it was going to wink at me about it. It didn't. It named it: "she'd already said yes to the whole of it... without once asking what the whole of it was." The book isn't pretending this was a clean choice. It's showing me the exact mechanism of how a yes gets bigger than the yes-er intended — and it's doing it in a scene with no man in the room, no villain, just Sondra being professionally incurious. That's not the book endorsing it. That's the book telling me the con's grammar has now been *inherited by institutions* — the salon assumed, Randi omitted, and Vee rolled over because refusing felt like a thing for a girl with lines left. Randi didn't tell her about the back. Randi, who tells her everything, who walked her through it with a hand on hers, left that out. I don't think that's an oversight in the writing. I think that's the plan leaking again — Randi curated the experience the way she curates everything, and I noticed it before Vee did.

But the ending saved the whole thing from tipping, because the last beat is *hers*. Alone, no hands on her, she touches herself and it's not pain, and she's not thinking about the wax or the woman or what she endured — she's thinking of his hand discovering what she did for him. "She wanted it so badly it was its own kind of ache, a cleaner one than the other." The chapter began with her yes arriving before her fear (his asking, clean, and her mouth answering "yes, tell me when") and ends with her wanting the reveal. Whatever was taken in that room, the wanting is hers. The book kept its line: he asked. She chose. Even the part where it hurt her, she chose, and the book didn't pretend it was painless or pretend she loved it.

Also — the waxing itself as a scene of being made "smooth for him." She went and had herself made over, painfully, privately, and kept it as a gift. "The most expensive thing in the room, and no one in it knew but her." That's the pleasure-princess identity compounding, and there's something both hot and slightly alarming about how good she's getting at spending herself this way. I'm watching her. But I'm with her.
