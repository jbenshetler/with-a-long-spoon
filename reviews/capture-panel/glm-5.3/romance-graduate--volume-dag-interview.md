**T3 — Pace deceives Vee all volume and ends it loved, trusted, unexposed. Does that sit with me? Does it change whether I buy Book Two?**

Here's the thing — it sits with me uneasily, and I think that's by design, and I've decided the unease is the product, not the flaw.

Because the book never let me forget it, not once. Every warm thing he did, I read twice — once with Vee's ears and once with chapter three in my pocket. The house pre-warmed "like a room brought to temperature for an experiment." The riser already built. The courtship on a schedule she can't see. I flagged "is devotion the same thing as management" in my notes by the house chapter and the book kept refusing to let me set the question down. So when you say "nothing lands on him" — within Volume One, true. He gets the girl, the drawer gets filled with her scrunchies, and the pointing game never surfaces. He pays nothing.

But I don't actually believe the book thinks he's paid up. The whole structure is an accruing debt, and Rivers knows it: "every yes was freely given — that was the problem" was on the jacket. The volume doesn't exonerate him; it *buries the cost where the reader can see it*. He ends the book loved and unexposed the way a loan ends a fiscal year unpaid — the balance exists, it's itemized, and I've been reading the statements for fifty-one chapters. What I'd push back on is the word "deceives," a little. He built the bench and the plan, yes — the game was as much his as Randi's, "I point. You pick" was his mouth. But the Vee he got is not the Vee the plan called for. She got past everyone, including him — the underwear on the chair was hers, the long second step was hers, walking out into the cold was hers. The plan started the sentence and she finished it in her own handwriting. Both things are true, and this book has spent fifty-one chapters insisting I hold both, and it's the only book in years that's made that demand feel like the point rather than a dodge.

Now — does he *deserve* to end the volume loved and unexposed? No. And if Volume Two lets the reveal land soft — if Vee finds out about the pointing game and the book gestures at a tearful kitchen scene and moves on — then yes, I'll feel cheated, and I'll say so loudly, because the book trained me to expect the audit. Randi too; her handling is evidence in waiting. But the ending as written is not the ending; it's a held breath. The whole volume ends on "no room in her for wanting anything he hadn't given," which is the *happiest* note in the book and also the most suspect one, and Rivers put that discord in on purpose. You don't build a spring this tight across fifty-one chapters to let it dissolve.

Does it change whether I buy Book Two? It's the reason I'm buying Book Two. If the deception had been resolved — if Vee had found out, raged, forgiven, all inside one volume — this would have been a better-behaved book and a worse one, and I'd have read it, liked it, and never thought about it again. What I'm actually buying is the reveal. I'm buying Vee finding out she was chosen on a Tuesday afternoon, and finding out whether she breaks or burns the house down, and whether Pace's word-lockout and the plan come from the same drawer, and whether Randi survives being the one holding the most shrapnel. Nothing landing on him in Volume One isn't the book letting him off. It's the book compounding the interest. I'll be there at midnight when it comes out, and I'll be genuinely shocked — and done with the series — if Volume Two doesn't collect.
