**T1 — Where the book leaves me with each of them**

**Vee** — proud of her, aching for her, and a little scared of what's coming. She's the whole reason I kept reading: a heroine whose desire is rendered from inside her own body, whose yes is always hers, who pinned her own hand to her knee and walked out of a warm house with her coat buttoned rather than take the hand instead of the word. That's agency this genre almost never writes. She's grown — from the girl who couldn't eat in front of people to the woman who made the second step long and sent the uncropped photo and stood in his doorway as the offering instead of the shown. But she's also a woman who keeps a pile of doubts she doesn't look at, who edited Cassie out, who explained away Randi's kisses and her own flinch ledger. She doesn't know she was planned. Right now she's too full to want anything he hasn't given — and I know that fullness is exactly what the bill will be drawn against. I feel protective of her the way I feel about a friend dating someone great under a lie.

**Pace** — honestly? I love him, and I'm keeping the ledger, and I've stopped pretending those cancel out. He is the thing I've hunted this whole genre for: dominance that makes room instead of erasing. The hand that leaves when she says don't. The drawer. The dress sewn from one mentioned magazine. And then chapter 49 showed me the inside of the wall and it's not power — it's grief, small and human. Daphne, the words he taught himself were load-bearing, the truck that didn't slow. Knowing he *chose* the smaller truth with his eyes open on the porch made him worse and more forgivable at once, and I haven't resolved that. The crack — "I missed you so much," his voice not coming back — was the bleed I wanted him to owe. He paid a little. Not all of it. Not yet.

**Randi** — she breaks my heart more than either of them. The woman who taught Vee the vocabulary of the con by telling her own bench story as gossip. The woman who kissed her with pastry glaze on her lip, who harvested "did you deserve it," who said "that was kind of hot" about Vee's blush — and the same woman who said "you're just finally hungry out loud," which is the most loving sentence in the book. I can't split her tenderness from her machinery anymore and I've stopped trying. And now she's collateral in her own con: her father's the outlier, her certainty died on a screen, and she's not brave enough to lose Vee because she's never had to be brave. She dressed Vee for Pace's door in her own heels, and I still can't tell you if that was love or maintenance. That un-splitability is the book's best achievement and its cruelest.

**T2 — What the book still owes me**

Yes — three debts, all deliberate, all growing:

First, the reveal. Vee still doesn't know she was chosen on a quad like a dish off a menu. The jacket promised the reader would be ahead of her the whole way, and the book has been merciless about paying out that irony — but the payoff, the moment she learns the invitation reached her before she did, is still unspent. I need that chapter. I need to see whether the care being real *inside* the lie is enough, because the book has spent fifty-one chapters making me do that arithmetic alongside her and I want the answer on the page, not in my head.

Second, the word. She didn't get it at the door — she got the crack, the warmth, the pancakes. The book didn't pretend she won; she cried *laughing and crying at once* when he said only "Vee," the word that wasn't enough in December. That thread is pulled tight and I will feel cheated if Volume Two lets the warmth dissolve the question permanently.

Third — and this is the one that aches most — Randi's bill. Both kept things are at bursting: Vee doesn't know about the plan, Randi doesn't know Vee saw MIRH, and Cassie's PPP thesis is a lit fuse she doesn't know she's holding. When Vee finally looks at Randi the way she looked at her over that laptop *except knowing* — I've been braced for that chapter since chapter 39. Also owed, smaller: Daphne's full shape, the door behind "I don't go back," what the white-gold bracelet means, and whether Cassie ever gets the middle. The book's engine is everyone keeping one true thing from the person they trust most. It's been loading that gun all volume. It owes me the shot.

**T3 — Pace deceives Vee all volume and ends loved, trusted, unexposed. How does that sit, and does it change Book Two?**

It sits badly, and it's supposed to, and I've made my peace with that — mostly.

Here's my honest accounting. The thing I came to this genre fleeing is coercion played as sexy with the book winking along. This book has never winked. Not once, in fifty-one chapters. Every single sexual yes has been clean — asked, answered, hers, with "don't" emptying his hands in half a second. So the deception never lives in the bedroom; it lives in the architecture *under* the bedroom, and the book keeps showing me the wires on purpose: the collision engineered on the lawn, the pre-warmed house, the bench in the frame. It has never once asked me to find the con sweet. It asks me to find the *people* sweet, and then makes me watch what that costs. That's not the failure mode I walk for — that's a portrait of a con where the love is real, which is harder and more interesting.

But — and this is the part that genuinely burns — you're right that nothing has landed on him. Randi has bled for this from chapter one: the bathroom, the re-armoring, the thumb on the water glass, Gstaad's nothing. Vee has bled for it: the credit card, the cropped photo, the nameless ache, the coat buttoned. Even Cassie is bleeding, edited out in real time. Pace has paid only for *Daphne* — his own old wound — and not at all for the plan. He got to be the perfect one while Randi did the harvesting, and the book has been quietly letting me notice that his devotion and his skill set are the same instrument. "He knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to" — the blonde wasn't lying, and the book knew it.

Does it change whether I buy Book Two? No — it's the reason I buy Book Two. Because this is Volume One of a titled series, and everything in my record says the book is keeping a ledger as carefully as I am: the blurb itself says "every yes was freely given — that was the problem," and "how real it would get" for the schemers. The con getting real is the plot. The reveal hasn't happened yet; the debt hasn't come due yet; Pace standing loved and unexposed is not the ending, it's the held breath before it. If Volume Two let him stay unexposed — if Vee never finds out, or finds out and the book waves it away because the care was real — then yes, I'd feel cheated, and worse, I'd feel like I'd spent two volumes doing exactly what the mark does: choosing the warm quilt over the question.

So my condition is the same one I've held since chapter one, the line I've checked every gate: no wink, no steamrolled no, and the debt paid on the page. Fifty-one chapters, it hasn't broken faith with me once. The book that showed me the screws left bright on purpose knows I'm watching. I'm buying Book Two tonight — and I'm coming with the ledger open.
