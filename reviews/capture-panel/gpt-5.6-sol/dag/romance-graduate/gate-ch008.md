REACTION — Oh, that kiss. He accepts her no so completely that she has to cross the distance herself, and then, after she does, he still gives the kiss back to her instead of treating one exception as permission for everything he wants. That was the turn. “He wasn’t going to take it any further than she had. Whatever came next was hers too.” Yes. That is exactly what makes him so dangerous to me and to Vee: he makes choosing feel safe enough that she can discover how much she wants.

Her “but” got me more than a grand declaration could have. The rule comes out automatically, he respects it without sulking or bargaining, and suddenly she can tell the difference between a boundary she actually wants and a rule she has inherited for managing men. She is not swept away. She stops him, watches him stop, and then changes her own mind. The second kiss is genuinely hers. I loved the grin she let stay on her mouth. That may be the freest desire she has expressed yet.

And the dinner was deeply, quietly erotic. Not because he fed her by hand or made a production of her appetite—thank God he did neither—but because “May I choose?” gave her the relief of surrender without erasing her agency. He asks whether there is anything she will not eat, then takes the responsibility she has explicitly handed him seriously. That distinction matters. The bread, the steam, the smoke-dark dip on his hand, Vee losing the thread of his sentence because he licked it away: hot. Very hot. So was her watching his hands and copying what they did before she knew the names of anything.

But the feeding pattern is making my skin prickle now. Pace sliced Randi’s apple into the exact form she could accept. Vee accidentally built the enormous froyo cup in his company. Now she arrives having eaten nothing since breakfast, and he chooses an entire unfamiliar meal that lets her stop watching herself eat. He is genuinely giving both women refuge from the policing of appetite. He is also coming to understand that being relieved of choice can feel like mercy to Vee—and he already knows how powerfully choice can be staged.

That is the knot the book keeps tightening: Pace’s care is not counterfeit. The meal is lovely because he notices, because he chooses well, because he does not stare at her hunger or congratulate himself for feeding her. Yet Vee is trusting where he takes her because she believes their meeting was chance, believes the evening belongs only to the two of them, and has no idea he discussed her appetite with Randi the night before. She is handing him choices under false emotional weather. Every individual yes is hers. The structure containing them still is not.

I also noticed how closely Vee’s softness at dinner echoed Chi Latte. Again she loses the edges of time, stops retaining the actual talk, and keeps the total feeling of being somewhere with someone who has chosen her. This time it is more restful and less socially dazzled—Pace actually listens, actually answers, actually gives her room—but I am not entirely comforted by how completely she disappears into the experience. “She let him carry it” is romantic because she is exhausted from carrying every interaction. It is frightening because she has known him two days and already trusts him enough to set down the whole apparatus.

Cassie’s “That’s not a boy. That’s a man” made me smile and then wince. She is right about the behavior she can see. Pace does not extract an immediate answer, demand reassurance, or make Vee manage his disappointment. But Cassie, whose instincts I trust most, is endorsing him without the one fact that might change her verdict. That hidden relationship is now borrowing credibility from Pace’s very real decency.

And I need to know about the right hand. Vee notices the rule because she notices bodies, and the book would not repeat it that deliberately for nothing. There is a history or a practice in Pace that neither Vee nor I have been given yet. Christine knows him, feeds him, teases him at the door; this restaurant is part of a life he had before these women, and I want inside it.

This chapter was gentler than the last several, but it did not feel stalled. It made me want Pace and Vee together badly enough that the deception now feels less like a conceptual problem and more like a future injury. That is cruelly effective. At one in the morning I’d be texting: he respected her no so beautifully that she changed it herself, and I am swooning while screaming that she still does not know who sent him.
