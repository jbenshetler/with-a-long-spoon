REACTION — Oh, Cassie. I loved her before, but now I understand the shape of her care, and it may be the cleanest care anyone in this book is offering.

She sees Vee’s humiliation without pretending it did not happen, makes her laugh without making the men’s attention the center of the afternoon, then sees her come up from the bottom with grief still on her face and does not demand the sad story as payment for being a good friend. She shoots water at her. Barely enough water, precisely enough outrage. That absolutely got me. Everyone else is studying Vee’s hunger and arranging an answer to it; Cassie notices what hurts and gives her somewhere else to put her body for a minute.

“The body that was such a problem stayed up on the deck with the rolled towel” hurt, especially after the bleachers. Vee’s performance walking past those men is good enough that they will read her as confident, maybe even flattered, while she is holding her breath for fifty yards. Then underwater she belongs to herself again—not because nobody can see how desirable she is, but because seeing and being seen briefly stop mattering. The grief in “before she had a body to be a problem” is enormous. She loved swimming before her body became public property, competition, evidence, invitation, and danger all at once. I felt the loss of that younger self without the book making her explain it.

And Cassie keeping “They will never know the love of a woman” just between them may be my favorite thing she has done. She could have performed protection loudly and made Vee’s body into an incident. Instead she restores the private world between them. The men get nothing—not even the satisfaction of becoming the subject of the next five minutes. God, I love her.

Then Pace. “Don’t answer tonight” is so perfectly designed for Vee that I am impressed and suspicious in equal measure. Of course it works. Every other man has presumably wanted immediate reassurance, immediate access, immediate proof that she likes him. Pace puts the yes back in her mouth and tells her to keep it overnight. That creates room for choice—but it also makes the choice glow. He has made waiting feel courtly, unusual, and charged, and now she is sitting half-dressed in a locker room thinking about his hands.

I don’t think he is faking his respect for her choice. That is what keeps making this difficult. He genuinely wants her answer to be considered rather than reflexive. But he and Randi already know how badly Vee wants him, because they arranged the conditions that let the wanting emerge. His restraint is real and it is still part of a seduction whose larger terms she cannot evaluate. “Don’t answer tonight” would be lovely if it existed by itself. Inside the plan, it is another exquisitely placed hand guiding her toward the experience of choosing freely.

And yes, the locker-room bench got hot. Not because much happened, but because Vee’s attention finally moved from being chosen by Pace to wanting his body. She is cataloguing the way he sits, his ordinary face, the thick knuckles, the door held open with his back—and then her own imagination catches her sitting warm and nearly naked under a towel. She gets dressed fast because the wanting has become physical enough to embarrass her. That is the first moment Pace has existed in her mind as something she might actively want to touch, not merely the miraculous man who noticed her.

What I really loved is that this chapter let Vee have a life untouched by the plan before returning us to it. She has old bodily knowledge, grief, joy, a best friend who can reach her without steering her, and a place where she once belonged to herself. That makes me more frightened about what Randi and Pace are doing, because now I can see the self their attention is not creating. She was already here. Cassie knows how to call her back.

The thumb-hang over the text is a very good ending because I know the yes is there. The question is what Pace believes the waiting will make it mean.
