I spent the entire tour braced for the bedroom. I knew what was behind that closed door. I knew what the long oak table could become. Vee had chosen satin that morning, Pace stopped the kiss she initiated, and then he led her through his house while the want stayed lit and unanswered. And then the silk.

That got me so badly I had to sit in it. Not because expensive gifts automatically read as romance to me; usually they do the opposite. A billionaire buying the heroine a dress is barely an action. Pace heard Vee name one wanted thing she had already trained herself to surrender, remembered it after she forgot she had told him, found a color better suited to her than the one she had imagined, and offered weeks of his own skilled labor to make it exist. That is not money solving a problem. That is attention becoming time.

The burgundy nearly broke me. Her first disappointed *it’s wrong*, followed by the realization that the magazine color belonged to the magazine girl and this one had been chosen for Vee’s actual skin and hair—yes. And the shirt. Of course he made the shirt. Of course the thing that fit properly, lay flat, and had no label came from his hands. Furniture, food, clothes: Pace’s care keeps taking the form of making something exact enough that another person can inhabit it without fighting it. “I couldn’t find a shirt I liked, so I made one” is absurdly attractive because he says it as though competence is merely what happens after sustained irritation and patience.

But I am not floating past the intimacy of what he is asking. He wants to measure her in daylight, down to what she will wear beneath the dress. That is a blisteringly charged request, especially after he stopped the kiss and left the bedroom closed. Vee arrived prepared to show him satin in bed; instead he is asking whether she will stand in all that window light and let him look with purpose, closely enough to understand how silk will fall over every part of her. Her yes felt genuinely hers. He had already bought the silk, which applies pressure whether he intends it or not, but he never tells her the dress is inevitable or acts as though his labor purchases access to her body. He says what the looking will require before she agrees. He tells her what he will do, how long it will take, and leaves the rest open. Randi sleeps here. Randi has stood in that bathroom, locked its door, rebuilt her face in its mirror. Vee reads the shoes and knives and books and paper and furniture as evidence of Pace, while the most consequential evidence in the house is one extra towel she walks past without a question.
