REACTION

Oh, Vee. The instant she started counting the women, I knew exactly where she was going, and I still wanted to reach into the room and stop her. Not because the jealousy was ridiculous—the whole evening was built to make her feel the difference between being chosen and merely being the latest girl Pace has expertly made feel held—but because she already knew better than the story she told herself. She knew his attention. She knew his patience. She had just spent the night hearing about the fifteen-year-old boy who was made to feel out of place everywhere, and then she saw that he had built himself a community where women trust him, and she turned that trust into evidence against him.

The dancing worked on me terribly. “Give me something to push back against.” “Come all the way in.” Her discovering that the closeness she thought would get her stepped on was precisely what kept her safe—I felt all of that. It is the whole seduction between them in miniature: his structure, her resistance, the frightening pleasure of letting his signals arrive before her decisions do. And it is sexy, yes, but the thought she almost managed to hold—*how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd been building out of wanting him*—is the question I need her to keep asking. Of course the dance carries it away. His great gift is making thought feel unnecessary.

The blonde knew exactly where to put the knife. “Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to” is not an invented accusation; it is the live wire running through everything Pace does. She took something true and made it mean the ugliest possible version of itself. Vee did the same thing a minute later. Her “service” was cruel, and Pace’s face simply emptying hurt more than anger would have. But I am not letting him entirely off: he left. Yes, she said she could get herself home and walked away, and yes, respecting that statement is consistent with him. Still, he drove her there, knew she was inexperienced in this place, and departed without one more attempt at contact. That is a very exact respect for her words at a moment when both of them were wounded, and it feels a little like retreat dressed as principle.

The bartender nearly undid me. Not because she cleared up the misunderstanding—this could easily have become one of those maddening manufactured-jealousy plots—but because the truth was more interesting. Pace has not been collecting women. He has made himself useful and safe in a room full of them. They were jealous because he brought Vee. Vee was the exception she wanted to be, and her fear of not being exceptional made her incapable of recognizing it.

What hurts most is where she takes the shame afterward. “Three dates, and she'd spread her legs like a slut” is her mother speaking through her at full volume again, and then the word *floozies* gets turned on the women dancing. That freedom Vee was beginning to claim was apparently sturdy only while she felt singular. The moment other desiring women appeared, shame rebuilt the entire hierarchy: them, her, all girls Pace might have touched. I hate it because I understand it.

And then she lies three feet from Cassie—wearing Pace’s washed-clean shirt, unable to sleep, with Cassie right there—and decides in the morning to go to Randi. That is the detail that has me already reaching for the next chapter. Cassie would come in her pajamas and ask only what was necessary. Randi is the person who has trained Vee to bring her desire, her fear, and her interpretation of Pace to Randi for naming. Vee thinks she is going to fix a mistake. She is walking straight toward the woman who helped construct the entire situation and who is jealous of both sides of it. I am genuinely afraid of what Randi will do with this.
