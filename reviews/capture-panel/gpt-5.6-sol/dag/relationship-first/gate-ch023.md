Oh, Randi. The small bowl nearly broke me before she even arrived. He has a bowl that is hers, the one he slices her apples into; he knows the exact shape of her place in his house, and still cannot understand that she is starving there.

This is the first time I have felt the full cost of Pace’s patience as something other than danger to Vee. “As long as she needs” is, on its face, exactly right. Vee must choose every step. But Randi is asking how long she is expected to remain an instrument of that choosing—how long she must listen, encourage, translate, kiss Vee goodbye, then return to Pace carrying news of Vee’s happiness while he gets whole weekends and she gets the small bowl. His “How do I get her from where she is to us, on your schedule?” made me furious. *Get her.* *Us.* He answers Randi’s pain as a logistical objection to his ethical method. He can imagine Vee’s autonomy exquisitely and Randi’s need almost not at all.

And yet Randi cannot say the true thing either. She does not say: I want her. I kissed her again. She met me this time. I am no longer willing to be the corridor through which she reaches you. Instead she calls Pace selfish, which is true but radically incomplete, and then uses sex to say something he has never learned to hear without the familiar vocabulary. No pause. No eyes. No “tell me.” It is awful that the man who trusts bodies more than words can register every altered detail and still decide it is merely “the want, with an edge on it.” Her body is practically screaming *this is not our usual language*, and he keeps translating it into appetite because appetite is the answer he knows how to give.

The sex was hot and deeply unhappy. I felt her anger in it, but I also felt her trying to take back the power she has been surrendering in increments: she pours her own wine, takes him in one motion, refuses to look, refuses to ask him to speak. None of it gives her what she wants. Then she wraps herself in his blanket with her back to him, and he does not touch her because he cannot tell whether touch would be care or another imposition. That hesitation is tender, but it arrives terribly late.

And then he saves the untouched portion for her. God. That is Pace in miniature: attentive, practical, loving, and completely wrong about what hunger he is looking at. He searches the evening for what he “did” as though there must be one misplaced action, because he cannot yet conceive that the arrangement itself has become unbearable. He fed her the amount he has decided belongs to her, preserved it carefully, and never saw that she could not eat it.
