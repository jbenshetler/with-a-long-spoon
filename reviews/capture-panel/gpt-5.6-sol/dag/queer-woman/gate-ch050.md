REACTION — Oh, Randi. No. Absolutely not.

She knows Pace loves Vee. She knows he misses her. She knows the silence came from his terror, because she has access to the half of the relationship Vee has been denied. And she uses that secret knowledge to make Vee doubt her own perfectly reasonable hurt. “You backed him into a corner in his own house” made me furious. Vee asked the man who has effectively absorbed her life to name what she is to him. She did not threaten him. She did not manipulate him. She cried and left when he refused. Randi turns that into something Vee did *to Pace*, then presents her certainty about his feelings as feminine insight rather than inside information. That is the scheme doing damage in real time.

And of course some of what Randi gives her is genuinely good. She separates Sheri from the imaginary blonde Vee built out of self-loathing. She reminds Vee that fear lies. She gets her laughing after a day of crying. She puts the expensive new clothes on the floor without looking at them because Vee matters more. The hugs are real; the missing is real; the bitter-orange breath against Randi’s neck is real. That is what makes this hurt. Randi is not pretending to care while steering Vee. She cares desperately and steers her anyway.

But sending Vee back to Pace naked under a coat as an apology is grotesquely perfect. Vee came asking whether she mattered beyond sex, and Randi’s solution is for her to withdraw the question and offer more sex—offer her body as “the answer,” with “nothing kept back.” The book knows exactly what it is doing with that language, and I hate how hot the image is because I understand why it catches Vee: the cold, the coat, the heels, the terror of the doorstep, making herself into a gift so undeniable that he cannot refuse her. It is tuned exactly to her appetite and exactly to her wound. Randi even makes Vee generate the crucial detail herself. She leaves the sentence open, Vee says “nothing under it,” and therefore experiences the plan as her own desire. There is the whole machinery in miniature.

“She was going to do it. She’d known it since the coat, maybe.” Yes, the wanting belongs to Vee. That matters. It does not make Randi’s intervention clean. Vee’s desire is being used to route her around a demand she had every right to make.

And those heels. “Randi always knew the shoes.” I actually swore. Randi is dressing Vee to go reconcile with Randi’s boyfriend, using Vee’s shame and hunger to restore the arrangement Randi wants, while Vee believes she is receiving disinterested friendship. If these are the same black heels Randi buckled onto her before, the triangle is about to walk bodily onto Pace’s doorstep while only one of them doesn’t know it.

Then Vee asks for the real New Year, and Randi still cannot give her one. We finally know she consciously loves Vee and knows exactly what the charge between them is, but she edits John into a funny bedroom failure and lets Vee laugh. She cannot risk being seen even while persuading Vee that walking naked through the cold is bravery. That asymmetry is becoming unbearable. Vee tells the truth until she is raw; Randi selects truths that produce outcomes.

The second long hug and the goodbye kiss got me anyway. Randi missed her. Vee breathing her in got me. The want between them is no longer garnish—not remotely—and that keeps the book on the right side of my deepest suspicion. But Randi is now perilously close to making Vee’s desire something she can operate like a lever. I need the book to make her pay morally for knowing so much and disclosing so little.

What I’d text at one in the morning: Randi just convinced the girl she loves to apologize to their secret mutual boyfriend by arriving naked in Randi’s shoes, and somehow Randi is both the person holding her while she cries and the reason she doesn’t understand what she’s crying about. I am unwell.
