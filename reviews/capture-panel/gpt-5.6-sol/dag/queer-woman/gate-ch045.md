REACTION

Oh, good for her. God, that hurt, but good for her.

“Words are cheap, and you still won’t spend them on me” is the line I’d text someone at one in the morning with no context except several exclamation marks. She has finally stopped accepting acts of care as a language she alone must translate. The warm house, the food, the drawer, the shirt, his hands—all of it is real, and none of it answers the question she asked. Pace keeps offering evidence when she is asking him to make himself vulnerable. “Believe what I do” sounds beautiful until you notice that what he does includes maintaining a secret architecture around her life. No, sir. Your actions are not the uncomplicated moral high ground here.

And I’m angry because she makes it so easy for him. She doesn’t demand marriage or forever or even the words “I love you.” She asks, plainly, who she is to him outside the erotic enclosure he has made for her. He calls her the most important person in his life, which ought to be enormous, but he still cannot—or will not—give the relationship a name she can carry through the door. Then he answers her with a comparison to her friends’ worse relationships, as though “better than what other women have” is the same as chosen, declared, known. It’s such an intelligent dodge that I almost hate him for it.

His face shutting at her answer frightened me more than anger would have. That folded-arm silence felt like a boundary around something much larger than reluctance to use conventional labels. Is he protecting an old wound? Is he protecting the plan? Is he refusing to call her his girlfriend because Randi already occupies that word? Right now those possibilities are sitting horribly close together. Pace may genuinely believe that deeds are truer than words, but words create public accountability. Words can be repeated to Cassie, her parents, Randi. Words make him answerable for what he claims. He will give Vee everything that can remain inside the house and nothing she can take outside as proof.

And her leaving before his touch could soothe her out of the question—yes. That matters enormously. His tenderness is real, but it has also become the mechanism by which her unanswered questions keep disappearing back into her body. She knows his hands can settle everything, and this time she refuses to let them. She arrives without the overnight bag. She doesn’t cross the room. She doesn’t offer the automatic kiss. She holds the question steady through every lovely thing he puts in its way, then walks out crying rather than accept her own name as a substitute for an answer. I’m devastated for her and fiercely proud.

I still believe he loves her. That almost makes this worse. Whatever prevents him from saying or naming it is costing her now, visibly, and he chooses the line anyway. Love that refuses to become knowledge for the beloved can do real damage while remaining love. This book understands that, and I am very much still here.
