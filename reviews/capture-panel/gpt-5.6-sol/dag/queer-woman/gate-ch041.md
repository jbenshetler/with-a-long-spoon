REACTION

Oh, this got me in the quiet place. Vee can tell her parents Pace is her boyfriend, can tell Cassie she may love him, can tell Randi exactly how he touches her—but “what is he to you?” leaves her with no usable language. That feels important. Not because the relationship is unreal, but because its reality is larger and stranger than the respectable pieces she can carry home. She keeps cropping herself according to the person looking: the photograph at the waist, Pace’s age and house and cooking out of the story, his sewing out altogether, the sex more speakable than the tenderness.

And I understand her father immediately, which makes this hurt more. Sex at least puts Pace in a familiar category. A man painstakingly making his daughter a dress because she once wanted one is intimate in a way he can’t file—and perhaps reveals a kind of attention that would frighten him more than desire. Vee is right that “weird” can be worse than wrong in a family. Wrong gets argued with. Weird gets treated as evidence that you have become illegible.

But the line that lodged under my ribs was that she was grateful Cassie didn’t push and also wished she had. Yes. Because Cassie’s restraint is loving, but Vee is drowning in people who know different edited versions of her, and some part of her wants the safest person in the book to insist on the whole photograph. She doesn’t merely lack a word for Pace. She lacks “the version of herself that stood up cleanly in the telling.” That is the real danger now. Pace and Randi have a combined story about her; Vee has fragments she keeps cropping for everyone, including herself.

And although Randi is absent, she is all over that title for me. Vee showed her the cropped nude, and Randi maneuvered her into producing the whole thing. Cassie asks one careful question, accepts Vee’s boundary, and receives almost nothing. The trustworthy woman gets the crop because she won’t extract more; the untrustworthy woman gets the full image because she knows exactly how to make disclosure feel like courage. That contrast is brutal.

I’m not frustrated by the lack of overt erotic movement here. This is the emotional underside of all that heat: Vee has become vividly herself inside these relationships and increasingly unable to explain who that self is outside them. I want Cassie to keep asking—not coercively, just enough that Vee has somewhere to practice being uncropped.
