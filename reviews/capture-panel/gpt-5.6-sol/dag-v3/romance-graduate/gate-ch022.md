REACTION — Oh, Peter.

That name felt more intimate than anything they have done naked. Pace is the name of the man who crosses rooms at exactly the right moment, chooses dinner, builds dresses, controls the tempo, and seems to have arrived in adulthood already knowing how to hold every difficult thing. Peter is the fifteen-year-old whose desk was put in the hall, who left home because staying was worse, and who was assigned a nineteen-year-old woman to help “the child” find his feet.

And Vee sees him. Not with Pace’s terrifying confidence, not by announcing the meaning of his own life to him, but with these astonishingly gentle little recognitions: they made the thing you loved the punishment. You were so young. The careful voice is carrying a full glass. She understands what he is protecting before he does, and she does not rip the protection away just because she has found the truth underneath it.

The Daphne revelation made me feel ill.

Nineteen and fifteen. She was assigned to him because he was a child alone among adults, and the person entrusted with helping him became his lover for two years. Pace gives Vee every reason it was acceptable: he did not feel young, it was the best period of his life, nobody did anything wrong, Daphne sensibly moved on when the age gap became socially inconvenient. He has arranged the facts so carefully that he cannot see the clean ugly word Vee has to hold beneath her tongue.

He handed her the thing that hurt him and called it luck. God.

That changes the emotional temperature of everything I knew about Daphne without settling it. Pace still believes he later crossed a door she built, and I still need to know what he did. But now I know his first formative intimacy taught him that a child’s profound attachment, genuine pleasure, and freely felt love can coexist with an adult’s misuse of access—and that the person inside the experience may defend it forever because it was also the first tenderness he had. This is the exact moral problem of the whole book sitting inside Pace’s own history, and he cannot recognize it there.

Every yes may have been freely given. That was the problem.

It also makes his obsession with leaving the final inch, asking precisely, waiting for the active answer, and never opening a closed door feel newly heartbreaking. He has built an entire ethics around making sure he never becomes the person who takes what has not been consciously offered. But he may have learned the wrong boundary of harm. Daphne perhaps did not force him. Pace may never have experienced himself as refusing. The injury was in the structure: she was nineteen, assigned to care for a brilliant isolated fifteen-year-old, and became the soft middle of his life. His current rules are exquisitely attentive to the visible yes while still failing to account for concealed architecture. Of course they are.

And now he is doing the same thing differently with Vee: not age, not authority, not coercion, but a structure she cannot see, wrapped around choices she genuinely wants. He believes the wanting protects the meaning because he needs that to be true about Daphne too. I am suddenly terrified that his entire consent philosophy depends on never combining those numbers.

Vee’s restraint was beautiful. She does not make him comfort her for what she has realized. She does not say the word and force him to either accept it or defend Daphne harder. She puts her body beside his, her palm over his chest, and lets him keep the story at the size he can currently bear. That is not cowardice. That is emotional intelligence. She knows recognition can become another kind of taking if you insist someone receive it on your schedule.

Which made the scar moment almost unbearably tender. “Not tonight” is the cleanest boundary Pace has given her, and she receives it perfectly. He does not remove her hand or turn the scar into forbidden territory. He keeps her palm on it beneath his own: this exists, you may remain here with me, and the story is still mine until I choose the time. Then she turns her hand so they are palm to palm over the thing he will not yet explain. I nearly cried. That is intimacy without extraction.

And I loved him talking about mathematics. Not because I suddenly care about coastlines, but because for once Pace was not using his intelligence to interpret a woman, arrange an experience, or solve a need. He was simply lit from within by the thing he loves. Vee gets to watch him become young in the good way: eager, expansive, almost breathless, offering her the world as he reads it. “Most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it, and I got to read some” went straight through me. This man’s deepest joy is that reality contains a language and somebody let him learn it after a teacher punished him for wanting to know.

And Vee gives something back. She recognizes that his total presence during sex is the lifting face—the state where there can be no future or past, only this body, this moment, this heartbeat. I have been reading Pace’s attention as a gift he gives women. Vee sees that presence is also where he goes to be free. That is the first time someone has explained Pace to me in a way that does not reduce him to control or care. He is not only attending to her. He is escaping time inside the act of attending.

The food conversation hurt softly too. Pace hears affection underneath the beige casseroles instead of congratulating Vee for graduating into cumin. Of course he does. He goes first to the hand under the thing. And she, in turn, hears the injury underneath his absurdly restrained “not a good fit.” They are extraordinarily good at finding each other’s buried tenderness when neither one is trying to perform insight. This is the romance I came for.

Which is why the ending is vicious.

Vee lies wrapped around him thinking Randi and Pace came to her “all of a piece,” pure fortune arriving twice in six weeks. She thinks she stumbled into Randi. She thinks Pace crossed the coffee shop because she happened to say she could not do an integral. She has just spent an evening learning that a loving experience can be real and formative while the structure around it remains something the younger person cannot correctly name. Then she looks directly at her own concealed structure and calls herself lucky.

I wanted to reach into the book and stop her at “for no reason.” There was a reason. She was selected. Randi manufactured the collision. Pace’s coffee-shop entrance was cued. The beautiful one-piece providence she is thanking the universe for is a plan made by two lovers in bed.

And the awful brilliance is that none of her happiness is false. The conversation was real. Peter is real. Her hand over the scar was real. His math, the food, the listening, the way he is all the way present—real. That is what makes Daphne’s story such a devastating mirror. Genuine love does not retroactively make concealed power innocent. Genuine pleasure does not make the structure irrelevant. Pace knows this when a door is explicit. He does not yet know it about the architecture of a whole relationship.

I am completely in love with Vee for the way she held Peter tonight. I am also more frightened for her than I have been in the entire book.
