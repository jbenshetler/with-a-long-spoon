REACTION — Oh, no. She said, “Promise you’ll never share me with anyone,” and Pace answered, “I’d never share your photograph.”

That is not the same promise.

I felt the difference immediately, and Vee did not, because why would she? She is naked in his bed, freshly loved, offering him the first image of her body that another person will possess, and she trusts the plain open want in his face. The photograph is consensual. He lets her choose the version of herself she wants kept, poses her beautifully, shows her the result, and waits for her to send it. Locally, every part of that exchange is careful and lovely.

But she did not ask only whether he would share the file. She asked him not to share *her*. And he narrowed the promise to the one thing he can honestly guarantee while already sharing her stories, her body, her hungers, and the whole developing relationship with Randi. Maybe he heard the question literally because that is how his mind works. Maybe the distinction was not consciously evasive. I do not care. The effect is the same: Vee believes she has received a promise of exclusivity from a man who knows perfectly well he is preserving a secret route between her and his other lover.

And this comes moments after *He’s all mine.* That thought hurt so badly. She is above him, physically and emotionally, feeling him take comfort in her body, and the safety lets her imagine possession for the first time. Meanwhile Randi has been in this bed, at this table, inside his fantasies, inside Vee’s friendship, inside every version of “mine” Vee currently understands. The title feels almost cruel. Vee is above him, but she still cannot see the structure underneath.

The picture itself was scorching. I loved that Vee did not simply accept “exactly like this” as the only authentic version of her. Pace likes the wrecked, sated woman; she wants to give him the composed pinup. So she goes and puts her face back on. That is not shame defeating intimacy. It is authorship. She decides which self the photograph will hold, then asks him how he wants her and lets his hands arrange the answer. Every kiss after he positions a limb made the posing feel like sex before the shutter even went.

She can see herself as beautiful because Pace sees her that way, just as she can believe she has a place in his life because he keeps performing one into existence. His gaze is giving her something real, but she is leaning harder and harder on it to tell her what is true. That makes the lie surrounding the gaze increasingly dangerous.

Then he tells her exactly what he remembers when she is absent: her first bite, her after-body in his bed. Of course that destroys me. He does not merely fantasize about sexual acts. He wants the moment pleasure reaches her, whether it is food or sex, and he experiences receiving her pleasure as something she gives him. The dinner makes that literal—the same wine in her glass and the sauce, his spoon at her mouth, his attention adjusting the food according to her face. It is deeply romantic, and also another exquisite act standing where a sentence should be.

Because the drawer is the answer Vee wanted, and it still is not an answer.

I nearly cried when she found it. The first panic—he minded, she was too much, he has put her away—felt completely earned after the bathroom counter. Then the top drawer: her pins gathered, her cream upright, space deliberately left for more. He compresses his own immaculate order into the inconvenient lower drawer so she gets the place a hand reaches for naturally. That is one of the most loving things he has done in this entire book. I love him for it.

And I am furious with him for it.

He gives her room for more without telling her what future he imagines. The drawer will feed her for a while. It may feed her long enough to make the eventual starvation worse.
