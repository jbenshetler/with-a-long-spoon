REACTION — Oh. Pace has one friend.

That landed with the same delayed force as Vee’s hangover: first as a plain fact, then as an enormity her whole life had to rearrange around. I had just spent the last chapter delighted that Sheri held a unique license to him. I did not understand how brutally literal “unique” was. She isn’t the friend who knows him best. She is the only friend he has ever managed to keep.

And of course the morning began with his care already everywhere. The water placed where her waking hand would find it. Paper towel under the glass because even hungover tenderness does not suspend his attention to wood. The pills, the warm cloth, his undershirt pulled over her, the forehead kiss after he thought she was asleep. Then cinnamon-raisin bread made from scratch while she slept, because apparently Pace can take a woman through a drunken night, put the whole house back in order, and still decide the morning needs fresh bread. I loved him so much I could barely stand it.

But “You need to eat before you decide how you feel” may be the most Pace sentence in the book. He does not tell Vee how she feels, minimize the hangover, or make her gratitude into payment. He feeds the body first and leaves the verdict to her. That is his patient attention at its purest—and now I understand that he learned to offer this exquisite form of presence without ever having a group of people offer ordinary presence back.

The explanation about men devastated me because Pace has made his isolation into a theorem. The window opens once. Friendship comes from losing in the middle of the pack. He was too young, too advanced, too financially established, and then the timer ran out. Everything placed in clean relation, every causal step elegant, nothing left messy enough to mourn. “It isn’t tragic” is exactly how I knew it was. He has done to friendship what he did to Daphne: arranged the deprivation so precisely that it can pass as an impersonal fact of how the world works.

And the women “misunderstand it.” God. I believe him, because I have watched what his attention does. Vee fell in love with it. Randi did too. A woman receives that degree of presence and thinks she has been singled out, because almost nobody listens that completely without wanting something intimate in return. Pace is not seducing them by pretending to care; he genuinely cares in a way that feels like courtship because friendship never taught him a lighter, more distributed form of intimacy. Then the friendship becomes awkward, and he loses it, and the loss confirms the theorem.

The chapter quietly made the concealed triangle hurt in a new place too. Vee thinks Sheri is the only woman the world has allowed Pace to keep because Sheri “could never want him.” She is standing in his kitchen compassionately solving the mystery while his other hidden friend and lover is Randi. Randi absolutely wants him. Randi has also disappeared from his life for weeks at a time, returned through sex, and never been named to Vee. Pace’s answer is emotionally true and factually engineered around the most important exception. Again.

Daphne became even more unbearable here. She was not merely the older woman who groomed a brilliant isolated boy. She was his entire social world. Friend, tutor, lover, peer substitute, family substitute, the only person carrying all the ordinary questions nobody else could ask him. No wonder he still cannot call it harm without also losing the best two years he had. Vee’s refusal to flatten that contradiction was beautiful: she can see that it was wrong and also understand that the child inside it experienced it as rescue. Both truths remain true, and neither cancels the other.

Then “either weren’t safe or weren’t welcoming.” I went completely cold. The unsafe men at college, the father things “went bad” with, leaving home by sixteen, beginning to lift in the dorm, the enormous inner-thigh scar, the right-hand rule—everything is pulling tighter now. I do not know whether those facts all belong to one event, and I’m frightened to force them together, but the shape around Pace’s adolescence is becoming awful.

Vee’s response was perfect. No speech about how he has her now. No pity he would have to soothe. No promise to fill every empty role herself, because the chapter has just made her understand one person cannot safely be everything. She simply went around the counter and gave him Sheri’s hug: both arms, cheek to chest, whole weight committed. She took a language of friendship she had witnessed the night before and offered it back without pretending it repaired the past.

And Pace took a moment before returning it. That nearly broke me. He can receive sex, desire, gratitude, even dependence with such practiced steadiness, but this uncomplicated holding—no task in it, no problem to solve—made him go somewhere far away. Vee let him. For once her silence was not covering a lie or accepting a substitute. It was company.
