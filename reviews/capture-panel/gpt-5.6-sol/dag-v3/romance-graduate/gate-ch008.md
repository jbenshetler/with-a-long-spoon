REACTION — Oh, this is romance. This is the thing I keep hoping all those “he takes care of her” books mean, and almost none of them actually deliver.

The food destroyed me. Not because Pace orders for her—God knows I have read that scene a hundred times—but because he asks, checks the one boundary he needs, and then treats the choosing as a responsibility. He does not make her ignorance cute, does not explain the menu until she feels twelve, does not turn her appetite into a spectacle. He chooses the bright thing and the dark thing, gives her a way in by eating first, and then lets her eat until she stops watching herself. That is so intensely intimate to me. Vee has been hungry since the jacket, and this is the first time the hunger is allowed to become ordinary pleasure instead of evidence about her body or her character.

And “May I choose?” Yes. Absolutely. Because the relief is the point. Agency does not mean having to manage every second yourself; sometimes the most luxurious choice is deciding that this particular person may carry something for you. Vee is exhausted from catching every dropped conversational thread, measuring every room, anticipating the bill for every kindness. Watching her discover that she can let Pace carry the evening and nothing terrible happens made me ache. “Following him took nothing out of her” may be the most romantic sentence in this book so far.

Which is exactly why the hidden plan is becoming almost unbearable. Vee is already learning she can trust where he takes her, and I know that trust is both earned and founded on a lie of omission. He is not performing this gentleness. The restaurant is real, Christine is real, his care is real, the way he leaves her room is real—and Randi helped select Vee before Vee knew he existed. Every authentic thing he gives her makes the concealed architecture more consequential.

Cassie’s “That’s not a boy. That’s a man” hurt too. Pace has passed the judgment of the person whose care for Vee I trust most, but Cassie is assessing only what Vee is allowed to tell her. She sees a man who refused to force an immediate answer. She cannot see that the waiting itself was a technique chosen by a man who already knew how badly he wanted the answer. I don’t think Cassie is wrong about him. I think she has been denied the information that would make the judgment complete.

And then the kiss. Oh, that got me. Vee states a boundary; Pace accepts it so completely that she has to stop him herself. He does not hover hopefully at the edge of her no. He starts walking. That is why her exception feels like hers. Then she gives him a small kiss, he answers only the amount she brought, and he leaves her the next inch too. This book understands that restraint can be blisteringly hot when it is specific rather than saintly. Her grin arriving inside the kiss and finding his there—yes, yes, yes. That was the first beat between them that felt uncomplicatedly happy.

Except of course it is not uncomplicated, because I know Randi is waiting somewhere outside this chapter. Vee has just experienced Pace’s attention as restful, nearly inevitable, like her hand has always belonged in his. Randi already froze when he said Vee was easy to listen to. What happens when she realizes Vee can rest with Pace in a way Randi cannot let herself do? Randi directs Pace because direction keeps her safe. Vee has just discovered that letting him direct her can feel like safety. That difference is going to cut.

I also noticed the right hand. The book made me notice it four times, so I am noticing. Maybe it is merely habit, maybe it belongs to whatever history Pace has not disclosed, but it felt like a closed door in the middle of all this opening. I want to know why he never uses the other one.
