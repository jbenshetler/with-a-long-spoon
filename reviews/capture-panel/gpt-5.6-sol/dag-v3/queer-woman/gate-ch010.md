REACTION — Oh, she called him back.

That is the chapter for me. Pace gives her privacy without making a performance of his virtue—hands her his only dry shirt, gets out, turns his back, stands in freezing rain—and Vee looks at the shelter he has made for her and decides she wants him inside it. Not because he coaxed her. Not because Randi told her what the scene ought to become, although *ravish* is still hanging around the edges. She wants him to see her. She knocks on the glass. She crooks her finger. She holds his eyes and undoes every button herself.

That was incandescent. The mother-voice is not gone; in fact, it is brutally present, naming the kind of girl who undresses in a truck after three dates. But for once the voice does not disguise the desire or relocate its authorship. It becomes part of the charge, and Vee chooses through it. The wanting belongs to her so completely that she can even look down and invite Pace’s eyes down with hers. She is not being uncovered. She is showing herself.

And Pace’s stillness worked for me here in a way it often hasn’t. He does not tell her what this means. He does not announce that the woman taking off her shirt is more authentic than the woman who heard her mother’s voice. He does not say *there you are*. He looks because she called him in to look, stays where she put him, and lets the looking itself be the answer. That is the cleanest version I have yet seen of what he believes he is doing with women: his attention following her choice instead of manufacturing the conditions for it.

Then she reaches for the next thing and discovers he is shaking. I loved that turn. The scene could so easily have made his self-denial into another piece of impeccable erotic control, but his body has a limit and Vee notices it. She stops the escalation, brings his hand under the flannel to warm it against her belly, and asks him to go. Care moves both ways. She is not merely the exquisite appetite he knows how to feed; she sees him, changes course, and takes responsibility for his body when he is too far inside wanting to do it himself. That made the romance feel reciprocal in a new way.

The whole hike earned that moment. He walks at the speed of her not knowing how. He fixes the boot he should have asked about and apologizes without turning the mistake into drama. He gives her the summit without selling it to her, watches her see it, then turns away so the seeing can remain hers. Even the cherry pit correction—the exact kind of small ignorance that could send Vee into shame—lands gently enough that she learns the rule without being made small by it. His own pit beside hers is such a tiny act of solidarity. Not *you did this wrong*; *this is what we do next*.

And the food again. The book understands that feeding Vee is erotic because hunger is one of the places she has been trained to disappear. The sandwich is not dainty, the horseradish makes her eyes run, and she keeps eating. “The plain animal rightness of needing something and being handed it” got me right in the chest. Pace keeps answering needs without congratulating her for having them.

The russet dress frightened me quietly. He handles the moment beautifully—does not offer to buy it, does not turn her wanting into a problem he gets to solve—but now he knows. Given the hidden plan, every detail Vee freely hands him can later return as evidence that he sees her miraculously well. I am already suspicious that the dress will reappear, and if it does, the emotional effect on Vee will be real while the machinery behind it remains concealed. That is the book’s knife again.

I also noticed that Randi’s fantasy is present even when Randi is not. Vee watches Pace climb through the picture Randi gave her and carries *ravish* up the mountain. But when the actual erotic moment comes, it does not follow Randi’s script. No summit ravishing, no man asking and taking. Vee authors something stranger and more intimate in a cold truck: look at me because I asked you to. That separation matters. The scene belongs to Vee and Pace, not to Randi’s anticipation of them.

And thank God for Cassie at the end. Vee comes home and tells the story as something she did on purpose, again, and Cassie does not let anxiety or judgment overwrite it. She checks the practical thing—boots—and then says keep the shirt. More importantly, Vee is not withholding this new self from her. The fear that Randi will ease Cassie out is still alive, but this chapter pushes against it hard: Cassie remains the person before whom Vee can put the whole open face down without having it interpreted for her.

The cop stealing the sentence annoyed me only because I desperately wanted to hear what Vee was about to say. Not enough to feel like cheap deferral—the interruption is quick and plausible, and the unsaid thing remains warm in the truck rather than evaporating. But now I need that sentence. I need to know whether Vee can say something as plainly as she just acted it.

Also, “I’m keeping the shirt.” Yes, babe. Keep the shirt.
