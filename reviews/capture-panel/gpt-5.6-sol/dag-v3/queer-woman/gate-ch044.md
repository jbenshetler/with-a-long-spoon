REACTION — Oh, he knew.

That is the thing that changes everything for me. On the porch, when Vee gave him the meal sentence, Pace did not misunderstand her. He did not innocently translate a whole-person confession into the register he understands best. The true answer rose, complete. He knew the smaller sentence would fail her before he said it, and he chose it because it protected him.

I am furious with him, and also finally inside the locked room.

Daphne does not make the refusal noble. Losing the first person who taught him how to live in the world was catastrophic, and I understand the superstition he built afterward: he said love once, she left, and the word became the dangerous part. If he never said it again, later losses hurt without destroying the structure of his life. That is heartbreakingly legible. It also means “words are cheap” was a lie he told Vee and perhaps himself. The words are so expensive to him that he would rather watch the most important person in his life walk away than spend them. Vee understood the cost more accurately than he did.

Sheri saw it in about thirty seconds. “Then not saying it matters more to you than having her” is pitiless and exactly right. I loved her for refusing all his brick-by-brick evidence. Every weekend, every meal, every room emptied for Vee—yes, all true, and none of it answers why he will not do the one thing she has plainly said she needs. Pace keeps offering the aggregate weight of his devotion as though enough beautiful evidence should exempt him from the requested sentence. Sheri does not let him turn quantity into meaning.

I also loved her giving him hell about “the philosophy of your food.” Thank God somebody can puncture him without either fearing him or needing something from him. Pace’s attention is extraordinary, but he has begun to believe his own exactness is almost a moral language, that if he makes the right dish and notices the right first bite, the woman should be able to derive everything he cannot bear to say. Sheri laughing at the grandeur of that felt medicinal.

But I went cold when he started building the three-person morning again. Even shattered by Vee’s departure, even missing Randi after six weeks, he is still imagining the kitchen after the concealed destination has been reached: Vee in his sheet, Randi with her coffee, everybody laughing, nobody hurrying. It is tender. I can feel why he wants it. It is also a future in which the difficult part has somehow already happened without anyone telling Vee what room she entered. His worry is whether the room will ask more of Vee than she has in her—not whether she would choose the room if she knew how long they had been furnishing it behind her back. He is still treating her desire as known and the disclosure as an emotional load to be managed.

And there it was again: Vee’s pleasure arrives visibly before her words, and Pace trusts the face. The first bite tells him. Her body opens and he knows she understands the philosophy underneath what he made. I believe he reads her beautifully. But this entire disaster is the limit of that method. He can read surprise, appetite, pleasure, arousal. He cannot read what promise she believes she is inside. He cannot cook a declaration into the chicken and wait for her face to confirm she received it.

Randi’s absence hurt in a quieter way. Six weeks, and Pace waits because she has always been the one who comes. He calls that preserving her choice. Maybe it is partly. But once again his version of ethical restraint leaves a woman alone with the cost of initiating every return. He knew her crying was not hormones. He knew enough to know that, and still accepted the safe explanation because pursuing the truth might require him to act without the invitation he depends on. Both women have now disappeared from his house after asking, in different ways, for a place he will not articulate, and he sits still because stillness can be called respect.

The drinking frightened me—not morally, just viscerally. Pace’s whole life is systems, portions, control, the body fed because it needs feeding. Watching the order go out of the glasses and then discovering bleach, vomit, the mop abandoned in the hall was awful. He tried the one culturally available method for not feeling and found, correctly, that it is useless. “Whiskey River was a liar” would have been funny if it had not landed in such wreckage.

And then the chicken and broccoli. God. The man who can make Vee’s first bite feel like revelation gives himself prison food and forces it down because a body is machinery that must continue. That is Pace’s private violence: he reserves appetite, beauty, and meaning for the people he feeds, then treats his own survival as an instruction to obey. Vee believes he can go on untouched because he sleeps, works, eats. Here he is doing exactly that—going on—and it looks nothing like being untouched.

The passing engine broke me. Not because sobbing proves love more truly than words—absolutely not, and the book had better not try to cash it that way—but because Pace finally cannot keep the loss survivable through discipline. The body he trusts so much does not give him a message to interpret or a truth he can substitute for speech. It simply breaks. And afterward there is still no Vee, no absolution, no romantic arrival. The truck passes. The food cools. He eats anyway.

I do not want Vee to discover this grief and decide it answers her question. It does not. Pace loves her; now I know that beyond doubt. He also knowingly withheld the answer, lied about the value of words, and asked her to trust acts whose concealed history she still does not possess. His suffering makes him understandable. It does not make him finished.

Now I need him to do something terrifyingly simple: go after her, say “I love you,” admit he knew it on the porch, and then tell her about Randi before the declaration becomes one more warm truth offered inside a false structure. Anything less is another smaller answer.
