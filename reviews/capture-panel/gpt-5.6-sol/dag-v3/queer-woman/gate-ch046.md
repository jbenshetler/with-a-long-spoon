“Vee, I l—” is the answer she walked into the cold for in December, the answer Pace finally suffered his way toward, and Randi’s plan stops it in his mouth. Vee has been taught that words are the whole war, that her demand for a name was the injury, and that the brave repair is to arrive as a body he cannot refuse. So when Pace opens the door prepared to do the one thing he could not do before, she deliberately prevents him and gives him sex instead. That is not merely tragic timing. Randi has successfully rerouted Vee away from the truth at the exact instant it becomes available.

And God, the entrance was hot. I felt all of it: the respectable coat over nothing, the absurd black heels, Vee winding herself up and stopping because she wants to carry the wanting to him, the cold finding her when she opens the coat, Pace’s wall simply disappearing. She is not passively delivered to him. She makes herself into the spectacle, knocks on the door, opens the coat, watches what she does to him, and takes immense pleasure in having authored the sight. That part is hers, and I will not pretend Randi invented the appetite. Vee has been making Pace sit still and look since the truck in the rain.

But she did not invent what the entrance is being asked to accomplish. She believes her body can apologize for requiring words. Pace looks like “a man just told he is forgiven,” though he has not told her what he did, admitted that he understood her confession, or disclosed Randi. Vee’s nakedness becomes absolution before she knows what she is forgiving. The fact that Pace was already beginning the declaration makes it worse: he does not exploit the plan knowingly, but once she interrupts him, he accepts the bodily reconciliation and never comes back to the sentence.

The doorstep scene was ferociously charged, and also so perfectly the book’s whole danger that I could barely breathe. His mouth “giving her everything it had” because it spent no word in December is exactly the substitution Vee had finally rejected. The mouth is magnificent. It still cannot answer what they are. Her orgasm in the open doorway cannot name the bond, and his urgency cannot disclose the woman whose shoes Vee is wearing while he kneels between her legs.

The shoes made Randi feel present through the entire scene. Vee arrives wearing the exact erotic object Randi selected for the body Randi wanted to display. Pace orders them left on without knowing Randi put them there. Vee experiences the whole thing as a private reconciliation between two people, but Randi is materially inside it from the ankle up. This is the concealed “us” advancing while both visible participants can tell themselves they chose every individual step.

I hated “the whole of her seizing in waves she had no say in.” Again, no. Pleasure can overwhelm her without becoming the truth that decides the relationship. And the coat’s stained lining “telling her she meant it” repeats the same dangerous claim: arousal proves she wanted the erotic act, not that she was wrong to demand a name, not that the reconciliation is complete, and certainly not that Randi’s interpretation was right.

I came closest to leaving over the driving. Touching herself while speeding down a dark county road with one hand on the wheel was not transgressive-hot to me; it was frighteningly stupid, and the cruiser passing someone else did not make it feel safely contained. I wanted her alive at Pace’s door more than I wanted that extra escalation.

Then her tears over her own name broke my heart because the book makes the regression feel like relief. In December, Vee understood perfectly that her name was not the word she needed. Now the same name lands and “nothing needed saying.” But something desperately does need saying. Pace was trying to say it before she stopped him. The unresolved word has not vanished; sex has made the need temporarily unfeelable again.

The pancakes and “I missed you so much” got me anyway. Pace kneeling beside her chair with his voice breaking is direct vulnerability, not merely another meal encoded with feeling. He apologizes. He tells her the separation cost him. Those are real words, and I believe every one. But “I never meant to hurt you” avoids what he actually did: he knowingly gave her the smaller answer because he was afraid. He apologizes for the result without confessing the choice.

And “there was no room in her for wanting anything he hadn’t given” is the bleakest possible ending to this reconciliation. Vee has not received the name, the declaration, the admission, or the truth about Randi. She is simply too full of sex, food, warmth, and relief to feel their absence. Cassie sees the entire sexual story in the coat, Pace’s sweats, and Randi’s heels, but not the relational story those objects actually contain. Nobody does except the reader. Vee comes home radiantly convinced she got him back, Pace believes he was forgiven, and Randi will see her shoes and know the plan worked. I need the next chapter immediately, because the attempted “I love you” cannot stay severed forever—and when it returns, it must bring the truth about Randi with it or it will only seal the lie more beautifully.
