REACTION — Oh, he cropped her.

Vee spends the first half of this chapter describing all the versions she cropped for home: the induction photograph cut off at the waist, the weight loss explained as dancing, Pace reduced to the facts her father can recognize and approve. Math PhD. Hiking. Proper courtship. She leaves out the sex because it is private and the sewing because it is too intimate, too strange, too much evidence of how specifically Pace has seen her. Then Cassie asks the only question that matters—*what is he to you?*—and Vee has no word because Pace has never given her one.

And then she gives him the uncropped version anyway.

The cracker-and-meal sentence is absolutely an I-love-you. Not a coy substitute, not a test disguised as gratitude. It is Vee telling him that he changed the scale on which she understands nourishment, pleasure, care, and her own life. She has spent weeks building the sentence because she cannot risk the three plain words until he says them first, and what she finally offers is perhaps more vulnerable than the plain words would have been. It contains her whole history of settling for fine. It contains her mother watching every second helping. It contains Pace feeding her until appetite becomes something she can inhabit without apology. She hands him the whole meaning.

And Pace answers the body.

“I love how good we are together” is such a devastating near miss because it begins with the words she needs and then turns away from her. Worse, when he explains what he means, it becomes “what your body does.” Vee has been telling Randi that Pace wants *her, with the body attached*, and here, at the exact moment she offers the person inside the body, he praises their sexual compatibility and her extraordinary responses. He does not say he loves her. He does not even say he loves what she does to his life. He says he loves what happens between their bodies.

I don’t know whether he understands and refuses the word or genuinely mistakes her confession for praise of sex and food. Vee identifies the same two possibilities, and both hurt because neither lets her remain inside the beautiful interpretation she has been constructing from his acts. This is the first time she has deliberately offered him a verbal bridge across the silence. He receives her warmly, yes—holds her, kisses her hair, chooses his answer carefully—and still does not cross it. The jacket just opened under my feet. A warm reception is not an answer.

And I am furious at how perfectly the bodily comfort helps her bury the discrepancy. Not because the warmth is false. It is real. His holding is deliberate; his tenderness is real; their quiet morning is real. But once again her body’s response is allowed to soften a question her mind urgently needs to finish. That small *wait* behind her breastbone is not a shameful objection lagging behind some truer bodily yes. It is Vee noticing, accurately, that she said one thing and he answered another. I wanted her to sit up under that quilt and ask him what he heard. Instead the warmth carries the question into storage.

The laundry room hurt worse than the porch because there is no romance there to blur the shapes. Just Vee, the dryer, and the syntax. She finally lets herself compare the sentences and discovers they do not match. And then comes the pile: other little failures in what she knows about Pace, already undergoing “repair work” so her understanding of him can keep functioning. That is frightening. Vee knows there are inconsistencies. She is not oblivious. She has been quietly editing the model rather than reopening the data.

I loved Cassie here with an almost painful gratitude. “Not to your dad. To you.” She does not supply the word, does not tell Vee what Pace’s acts must mean, and distinguishes inability from unwillingness with surgical gentleness. Then when Vee admits both, Cassie leaves the question open. Vee is grateful and also wishes Cassie would push. Of course she does. She wants somebody to make the dangerous question unavoidable because she cannot yet choose it alone. Cassie refuses to author the answer for her, which is loving—and leaves Vee to take the question straight to Pace in a form he can evade.

And the finality of “her one attempt” scared me badly. She has concluded not merely that she will wait, but that the best sentence she had is retired forever. Pace may go on giving her drawers and meals and scented shirts and never know that the silence now contains a specific injury. She will keep translating his acts, but she has stopped believing she can safely ask for the word. I need the next chapter immediately. Something has changed in the bond even if neither of them will name it: Vee has found the edge of what his acts can answer.
