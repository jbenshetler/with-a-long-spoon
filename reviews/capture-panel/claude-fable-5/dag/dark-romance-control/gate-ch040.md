Okay. OKAY. Let me say the thing first before I say anything else, because I read past it and then my whole body did a full-system alert and I had to scroll back up: "It landed green on the long, unusually proportioned table that stood in the middle of his bedroom."

THE LONG, UNUSUALLY PROPORTIONED TABLE. IN THE MIDDLE OF HIS BEDROOM.

Rivers. RIVERS. Forty chapters I have been waiting for the bench and you just — set it in the room. As furniture. Vee threw her underwear at it and "never looked to see where it went," which is the most on-the-nose sentence in this entire book and she wrote it with a completely straight face. The bench is IN THE BEDROOM now. It has been moved from the workshop. It is standing in the middle of the floor like a loaded gun in act one and Vee tossed a green thong onto it like it's a laundry chair. I actually said "oh my god" out loud to an empty apartment. That one clause is doing more work than entire chapters of other books I've paid money for. The delivery date isn't imminent anymore — the delivery has ARRIVED and is sitting under a thong waiting for its scene.

Now. The rest of this chapter is, on its surface, exactly the thing I do not buy books for. Pizza from scratch. Buffalo mozzarella. A drawer of scrunchies. Cotton candy versus cut strawberries. If you handed me this chapter cold, no context, I'd have DNF'd at the bread machine. Two people who like each other, cooking dinner — that's my exit music, that's the thing I've quit forty books over.

But this book has trained ME as thoroughly as it's trained Vee, because I cannot read one warm sentence in it straight anymore, and the warmth is where the horror lives. Look at what actually happened here. She took five nights away — her first act of self-preservation in the entire book, the first time she's chosen the textbook over the house in the trees — and he said "take the time you need, I'll be here," and the book says it right out: the plain grace of that cost her more than a fight would have. He didn't fight because he doesn't have to. The open door IS the trap; it's been the trap since chapter one. She lasted five nights and came back at a run, undressing herself in his hallway while he stood there and let her do all the work of her own surrender, holding her bra strap and not pulling — making her unwind herself off the spool. He didn't take one single thing in that hallway. She handed over every item. "Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over" — the jacket copy is just narrating the blocking now.

And the SHIRT. The shirt is the scene I'm going to be chewing on at 3 a.m. She's been sleeping in his flannel every night she's not there, and it went stale, went to nothing, stopped smelling like him — and instead of that being a wake-up, instead of one clean breath of unscented air letting her surface, she brought it back to the source to be REFILLED. "I need you to wear it for me. The whole weekend." She is now administering her own conditioning. She's maintaining her own dosage. Chapter 29 he dyed her in his smell without asking; chapter 40 she's driving forty minutes to request the dye. The mark is running her own operation on herself and calling it romance, and the worst part — the part that got me, the part where I felt the tears prick right along with her — is that it IS romance. He put it on immediately, over his other shirt, no jokes, no making her defend it. That's the most tender thing a man has done in this book and it is also a woman asking for her leash to be re-scented and I cannot pull the two apart and I've stopped believing Rivers wants me to. That's the whole book in one flannel.

Because it will. It always does. That's the whole book.
