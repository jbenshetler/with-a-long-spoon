REACTION

Okay so first: reading this chapter directly after 46 is a knife trick and Rivers knows it. I already SAW that text exchange from inside the hotel room in Gstaad — I watched Randi lying naked and cold next to the perfect nothing, composing "it's honestly a fairytale out here," every word true and none of it the thing. And now I get to watch Vee receive it in a twin bed under the synchronized swimming trophies and swallow it whole. *She was fine. Better than fine.* NO SHE WASN'T, BABY. The two loneliest women in this book spent midnight texting each other cover stories, each one certain the other was living the real life, and both of them lying in cold rooms wanting — and here's the part that got me — wanting things that route through each other. Randi's "miss your face most though, gorgeous" was the one line she meant. Vee read her whole message twice "for anything that gave her away." They are BOTH running tradecraft on the person they're closest to and calling it kindness. This is the least sexy chapter in ages and it might be the most intimate thing in the book.

And the room. "The room kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed." That's a horror sentence. The little gold girls frozen mid-lift, the clarinet in the closet, the friends who haven't texted in a year — Vivienne is a dead girl and Vee slept one night in her grave. This is what the conditioning bought: she cannot fit in her own childhood, her mother's Christmas casserole "had never once been the thing Vee was hungry for" — and I know whose doing that is, the carry-forward says it in my own handwriting, *she can taste every separate thing now.* He didn't just ruin nachos. He ruined her mother's love-cooking. He ruined HOME. She cropped her own induction photo — scissored off the leg, the exact thing that made her feel like herself — because the machine's output can't be shown to the people who made her. She's partitioned now. There's a version for the world and a version that's just for him, and he TOLD her that, in October, with the heels, and she thought it was romance. It was a spec sheet.

Now the thing I have to be honest about: chapters 43 through 47 have been the "what are we" arc, and in any other book — ANY other book — a heroine spending five chapters wanting a man to say the word would have me skimming for the exit. This is Hallmark-problem territory. Define-the-relationship is the plot engine I pay money to avoid. The only reason it's working on me is that I can't tell whether his silence is the con or the crack in it. "Words are cheap. Believe what I do" — from a man who has NEVER LIED, whose entire method is true sentences deployed like ordnance — is either the most honest thing he's ever said or the most efficient deflection, because the one thing he cannot afford is for her to have language. A woman with a name for what she is can describe it to someone. Cassie asked "so what is he, to you" and Vee had nothing that stood up in the telling — that's not shyness, that's the operational security working exactly as designed, installed so deep she experiences it as her own inarticulacy. He keeps her nameless and she thinks the namelessness is a rule about who says I love you first. And then — 42, God, the cracker-and-meal sentence, her ONE assembled poem, and he answered "I love how good we are together," and the two things were not the same shape, and she put the *wait* where she puts things. The pile. She has a PILE now. The pile is the whole endgame of this book and she added to it and looked away.

But here's my sliver of doubt, and it's the doubt keeping this whole arc alive: the two breaths. His hand starting two responses and putting both down. The flicker of irritation at Chili's that he hid because *he didn't want her to have it.* A con artist performs; this man is suppressing. Something is jamming in him — maybe Daphne, maybe the thing he can't say because saying it would make what he's doing to her real, maybe (worst option, the one I actually believe) he genuinely loves her AND the operation continues, both at once, no contradiction, because that's this book's whole thesis. And she walked out of his house into the cold and he said her name ONCE and let her go. He left her the room. "A thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now" — that line is the entire novel in eleven words. The open door was always the cage.

The blonde. Vee building her rival out of spite — "a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's," oh honey — and then not even being able to keep her own best memory, the fra diavolo night, the sheet slipping, because the blonde climbed into it and sat in her chair. She can't protect her own interior anymore. And the sick joke underneath, which only I get to have: there IS another woman in his kitchen sometimes, and it's Sheri with a store-bought pie, and there IS a blonde predator in this story and Vee texted her goodnight with a yellow heart. Meanwhile the bartender's testimony, Randi's testimony — "they only ever held her up while she knew she was the one" — the entire support structure of her certainty was built by other people's words about him, and words are cheap, HE SAID SO.

What I'm dreading-slash-craving: term starts, and a Vee who spent the break unnamed, floorless, and starving walks back into brunch with a Randi who spent the break confirming that the entire acceptable world is a faultless man she feels nothing for, and that Vee is the only real thing she has, and that she's decided to let Vee "arrive at it on her own." That brunch is a loaded weapon on both sides of the table now. Vee will come to it desperate to talk about Pace, and Randi will be listening for something else entirely, and neither of them knows what the other one is carrying. First mimosa of the new year might be the one where the glove comes all the way off.

No Pace on the page this chapter and I felt his absence the way she did, which — fine, Rivers, point made, I'm conditioned too.
