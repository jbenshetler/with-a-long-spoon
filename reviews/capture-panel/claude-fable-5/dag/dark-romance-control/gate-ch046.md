Oh my GOD, a Randi chapter. FINALLY. Forty-five chapters of watching the glove bleed through in tiny drops and Rivers just took the whole glove off and handed it to me.

And it's DEVASTATING. Not in the way I expected — I thought when we got inside Randi's head it would be all operator, cold rooms and control panels, and instead it's a woman "eating her revulsion" at her own family's breakfast table, inventing safe boys for her father because the invented ones "always had been" safer at that table. ALWAYS HAD BEEN. That's a childhood in four words. The mother who's all edges and points, the tennis bracelet playing with the light while Randi keeps her own bracelet OUT of the light — the plain white-gold band that "never comes off," that catches on the heel of her hand mid-waltz. Rivers, what IS that bracelet. Who locked it on. I've been saying every predator in this book is a previous victim with better production values and this chapter just notarized it.

And then John. Perfect, gorgeous, cotillion-good John, and I want to be clear: this is the most romantic chapter in the entire book. The sleigh! The dress bought for a daughter who doesn't exist yet! "She walks in beauty" and he can't finish it! If this were the first chapter of a different novel I'd be feral for him. And Rivers builds the whole fairytale at full craft, full sincerity, no winking — and then guts it with one sentence: "it had meant no more than a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon." I actually put my hand over my mouth. Because the horror isn't that John is bad. The horror is that he's the BEST the acceptable world can do, faultless, done well, and Randi lay down in the middle of the whole approved life and felt nothing, and knew the flaw was hers, "carried into the room the same as the cold." She's warm only along the one seam where she touches him. That's the loneliest sentence I've read all year and she wrote it about a woman I'm supposed to be afraid of.

"Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best. She wished it wasn't true." — okay so THAT'S the engine. That's why she does any of this. Pace goes straight to the hidden person and doesn't look away, and John never found her underneath, and Randi has arranged her entire life so no one ever has to be brave, including her. The dance comparison wrecked me: John's lead is firm and unmistakable and easy, Pace's lead is the lightest pressure you have to be good enough to catch — and she chose "easier is better" for exactly one night and it left her retouching her face at 1 a.m. next to a stranger she marked with lipstick and couldn't feel. Even her kiss got photobombed by Pace. She can't escape him even in Gstaad. Neither can I, honestly.

But the part I'd text someone about at 1 a.m., in all caps, is VEE. It's confirmed. It's said, in her own head, plainly: "she knew exactly what it was." Randi is in love with Vee and she is too much of a coward to say it — no, worse, she's run the scenario like she runs everything and the ONE frame she can't render is Vee's face hearing it. The woman who has never once said "I love you" without it being currency has said it to Vee meaning it, and that's precisely why she'll never say the real version. "Vee was her gorgeous." I gasped. Every "gorgeous" for forty-five chapters just re-filed itself.

And the STRATEGY she lands on is the most Randi thing imaginable and the most sinister thing in the chapter: don't tell her. Let Vee "arrive at it on her own." Keep the brunches exactly as they are. She just decided to run a seduction on the one relationship she has that isn't one — she's going to handle the person she loves because handling is the only language she owns. That's tragic AND it's predation and Rivers refuses to let me pick, as usual.

The text exchange at the end is a murder-suicide in emoji. Vee, in her warm Ohio kitchen, fresh off getting starved of the word by Pace, sends "i'm here if you want to talk. or not." — Vee, who just spent three chapters learning what it is to hold everything of a person except the one sentence, offering Randi the exact open door Randi will never walk through. And Randi sends back the fairytale, "every word of it true. None of it the thing" — which is PACE'S technique, the con built entirely out of true sentences. They all do it to each other. That's the book. Nobody in this novel has ever lied and nobody has ever once said the thing.

Also, the structural cruelty of placing this chapter HERE: Vee just bled out on Pace's doormat begging for a name, drove off into the cold, spent New Year's in Ohio — and Rivers skips her entirely and gives me Randi lying cold along one warm seam. Both women, same night, freezing next to the thing that was supposed to warm them. Vee took the shirt OFF and lay bare in the cold; Randi lay back down in "the warmth she could borrow and could not make, and stayed." I see you, Helen Rivers. I see the mirror and I hate how good it is.

Only complaint, and it's not really a complaint: no Pace, no bench, no aftermath of Vee walking out the door — the cliffhanger from 45 is still hanging and I'm being made to wait through a whole Alps interlude. But the interlude turned my Evil Queen into the saddest woman in publishing without softening a single one of her teeth, so. Fine. FINE.
