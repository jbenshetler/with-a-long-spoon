REACTION

Okay, so the hangover chapter. And it's the right hangover chapter, which matters more to me than almost anything else this book could have done after Chapter 1 — because Chapter 1 ended with a man sitting there feeling quietly pleased about the "remarkably good evening," and if the book had agreed with him, I'd have been gone. It doesn't agree with him. Chapter 2 is forty-eight hours of evidence that whatever happened on that bench, Randi experienced it as a breach, and the book is watching her seal the hull in real time.

The Brooke scene got under my skin in the best way. It's the bench again, is the thing — I don't know if I'm supposed to see that and I don't care, I see it. A person with more power keeps her pinned in a position, controls the tempo, decides when it's over, releases her when *she's* done ("Go"). Except there's no safeword in the common room and no aftercare and Brooke isn't even cruel, she's just *seated*. Randi runs Standards. She lives inside interlocking management systems and is fluent in all of them and has apparently never once been off the clock. The lie about the paper, the lie about Wilson Street, the laugh that comes out at Kinsey's pitch before she can choose a pitch — I felt that last one in my teeth. She can't even calibrate anymore. The machine's running her.

And then the room. God, the room. Standing with her hand on the knob behind her. The closet she opens and can't remember why. The water she runs and doesn't touch. Shoes on, under the duvet, cardigan buttoned. "It was not — it was a duvet." That sentence broke me a little, because you know what she was about to say: it was not *his* blanket. The cashmere one she brought to his house because she gets cold after — she gave the good blanket to the place she can't go back to being seen in.

Here's what I'd text a friend at 1 a.m., though: *"He's hard to say no to," in a different key.* The book just showed me its own hand and I respect it enormously for that. Because I've read the jacket. I know what Randi does next — she finds Vee, she steers her toward Pace, laughing, as if by accident. And this chapter is quietly building the why: a woman who got seen once, couldn't survive it, and — I'm guessing, I'm dreading, I'm *leaning forward* — is going to feed someone else into that gaze. Watch it happen to another girl from a safe distance. Have it without having it. That's monstrous and it's completely human and the book is not romanticizing one inch of it; it's showing me the wound the monstrousness will grow out of. The not-crying that "did not feel like holding a door shut. It felt like there being no door" — that's the most precise description of dissociation-as-lifestyle I've read in this genre in a long time. And the bottle cap under the nail bed last chapter wasn't a one-off flourish; this whole chapter is that gesture, extended.

What I'm holding warily: Pace. He's absent here, and his absence is doing him favors. Chapter 1 let me see that he thinks he "found her and answered her" while she experienced something closer to being cracked open without a plan for what came out — the book knows he's wrong about what happened, but I don't yet know if the book knows *how* wrong, or whether it thinks his kind of attention is a gift that Randi simply couldn't receive. When he turns that "seeing" on a twenty-one-year-old junior who's been set up by her own friend — with a plan she doesn't know exists — the book is going to have to keep both eyes open. So far both eyes are open. I'm noting the age gap that hasn't been stated, the TA question Brooke asked that got answered a beat too slowly, and the fact that the jacket keeps whispering *every yes was freely given* like it knows exactly which nerve of mine it's standing on.

Two chapters in and nobody's even met Vee yet, and I'm scared for her already. That's the jacket working exactly as promised: I'm ahead of her from the start.
