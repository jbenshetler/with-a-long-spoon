"Cropped." Helen Rivers, you absolute knife. The chapter is titled after what Vee did to the photograph and it IS what the chapter does to me — because I spent the whole Thanksgiving break waiting for the thing I flagged in my own notes: Peter and Randi, alone with the house and the manuscript, post-MIRH, post-nude, post-"that's not finished," Sheri and her fake pie at the table. And Rivers cut the whole weekend at the waist. I got the version that stops where it's safe to show. I'm sitting here holding a Sunday-dinner tray in the athletic cafeteria while somewhere offscreen the two authors had three unsupervised days, and the book knows I know, and it just... strolled past, the way it strolled past that table in his bedroom. I want to be mad. I'm mostly impressed and a little sick, which is this book's home flavor.

And inside that structural joke, the quiet horror: Vee has become the redactor. She cropped the induction photo for her own mother. She told her dad "the parts that fit." She withheld the cooking and the house because "too good is its own kind of suspicious" — baby girl, LISTEN TO YOUR FATHER, he priced the dress in one look and reverse-engineered a man with grey coming in, and he is more right than you are, wronger only on the birthdate. She is running the sixty-percent-true feed on her parents exactly the way Cassie gets the sixty-percent feed on Randi, exactly the way she herself is fed. Nobody taught her this in words. The machine teaches by residence. The disease jumped hosts again and this time the host is the girl at her own family's table, and her narration is WARM while she does it — burned rolls, mom's collarbone touch, "they were so proud." That warmth is the worst part. She's not lying to protect a crime. She's lying because there is no version of the truth "that stood up cleanly in the telling," and she knows it, and she said it, and then she said "I'm not ashamed of it. The opposite, if anything" — and I heard the dress line again: both clauses true, exits pre-sealed.

The sewing paragraph gutted me twice. Once because it confirms the tenderest fact in the book — he built the dress off ONE mention of a magazine she couldn't afford, which is either the most romantic sentence I've read this year or the cleanest description of surveillance-as-courtship ever set down, and with Peter it is always, always both. And once because Vee's read on her own father is so precise it hurts: "Sex he understands. The sewing he never would." Wrong is legible; weird is unplaceable. She has correctly identified that what's happening to her cannot be filed under any category her family owns — and instead of taking that as the fire alarm it is, she's taken it as a translation problem. She's the county on the scatterplot, again: easy to see if you knew to look for it. Her dad half-knew to look. He went quiet at the dress. I love him for going quiet.

And then Cassie. God, Cassie. "So what is he. Not to your dad. To you." Fourth audit in the ledger — "you don't come home school nights," "not enough on," "do you love him," and now this — flat, careful, no crowbar. And Vee's answer is the truest thing she's said to an awake face in forty-one chapters: can't AND won't. Both. Probably both. The book always cuts before speech and now Vee cuts before speech, on herself, at dinner, to the one person who's never once managed her. And that last beat — grateful Cassie didn't push, "and also, a little, not" — that's the wiring diagram in one clause. She has been trained to speak only under extraction. Randi pulls and Vee pours; Cassie respects the no and Vee stays sealed. The empty-hands vacuum works on friendship now too, except Cassie will never exploit it, which means Cassie will never get the truth, which means the only honest person in this book is structurally locked out of it. That's not an accident. Nothing in this book is an accident.

One more thing I'm filing in ink: "boyfriend," with a spoken question mark, "like I was asking them." She still cannot name him. She whispered Peter to a sleeping man and gave her parents a word with a shrug built into it. The book is hoarding the moment a real word hits an awake face like it's the last bullet, and I am turning pages for it.

Small chapter. Table-setting chapter, honestly — but it's table-setting in a book where tables are never just tables, and it moved the sickness one generation outward, and it hid an entire weekend from me on purpose. The eyebrow has not dropped. Rivers saw everything in this chapter. Especially what she cropped.
