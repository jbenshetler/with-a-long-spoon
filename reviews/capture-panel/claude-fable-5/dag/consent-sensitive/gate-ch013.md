A breather chapter, and I still came out of it with my pulse up, which tells you where this book has me. Girls at a dinner table, gold light, fries — the lightest thing Helen Rivers has written yet — and I spent the whole of it doing threat assessment, because she's trained me to.

First, the relief that isn't relief: no Randi. Not one mention. Vee has a whole other social ecosystem — Kayla with the curls and the unbroken stream, Meg following "the Pace situation in installments like her shows" — and it's warm and real and it made me feel briefly like Vee has more net under her than I'd feared. And then I caught myself. Meg was *there at the collision.* Meg is asking for the shirt story like it's television. These girls aren't a net, they're an audience — and an audience doesn't catch you, an audience wants the next episode. Vee even hears herself doing it: "She could hear the story getting better in the telling and did not care." That line is the quiet knife of the chapter. Randi edited Vee's memory three chapters ago and now Vee is editing her own, polishing the myth of Pace for the table, and she *knows* it's getting better in the telling and does not care. The groomed girl becomes her own PR department. That's the book knowing exactly what it's doing, and I logged it with a chill.

Second: the intel. He's a *powerlifter.* First thing every morning, before the math. I want to be very clear about what my body did when I read that — the same thing Vee's did, a little flip, the situation under the jacket — and what my head did one beat later, which is note that we have now been told this man is enormously physically strong, lives past where the road goes to gravel, and we learned it in the cutest possible packaging, over fries, with the reader purring. Rivers keeps doing this — handing me the dread wrapped in the exact pleasure that would make Vee miss it. Lifts, then writes six hours, then stops: a man of total self-regulation. A man with a *system.* Everything about him is a system. Vee thinks it's discipline. I think it's the same architecture as the apple slices.

And then Vee says the truest thing she's said in the whole book, and it goes by as girl-talk: "He asks me first... and he waits for the real answer, and then that's exactly what happens, the way I said it. It sounds like nothing. I can hear it sounding like nothing." SHE CAN HEAR IT SOUNDING LIKE NOTHING. She is standing directly on top of the mechanism, narrating it accurately — the man for whom her stated want is always, perfectly, frictionlessly granted, which is precisely how you teach a starving person that wanting is safe *with you and only with you* — and she experiences it as inexplicable arousal. And Meg, bless her, lands the one needle anyone's landed in weeks: "Everybody's different at the start. Sometimes they even are." Vee lets it go by. "It didn't catch on anything." Of course it didn't. Cassie's needles don't catch either. The book keeps showing me warnings sliding off her like water off the pool girl, and it keeps *showing me that it's showing me,* which is the only reason I'm still here.

The text. "Would you come to my house Saturday. Two o'clock." Kayla calls it a ransom note as a joke and Kayla is right and doesn't know it. The least information a human being has ever sent her, read a thousand times — that's not courtship minimalism, that's the withholding that makes her fill the silence with her own wanting, and she's doing it at the table in real time. He's run this play since the coffee shop: leave a gap exactly her size and let her climb into it and call it her idea. And the house is *the house.* Off 614, past the gravel, where the bench lives, where Daphne's door broke. I've been waiting for this invitation with my stomach clenched since chapter one and the book delivered it via group-chat squeal, in the gold forgiving light, with hair plans. "Bring a change of clothes," Kayla says, delighted. I wrote GET OUT in the margin of my own brain and also I am counting the days to Saturday right along with Vee, twelve and all, and that doubleness is this book working exactly as designed on me.

Last thing, the one I'd text at 1 a.m.: the chapter is called "What to Wear," and there is a russet silk dress sitting in a magazine from two chapters ago that he called beautiful and never picked back up. If I walk into that house Saturday and that dress is laid out on his bed — the want she voluntarily set down on the rock, retrieved and gift-wrapped and handed back so she learns that *he* is where her abandoned wants go to come true — I will scream, and I will keep reading. He didn't say what to wear because he already knows what she'll be wearing. I'd put money on it.

Vee didn't debrief the hike to her, or if she did, the book didn't show me — and after the memory-edit chapter, an off-page Randi is scarier than an on-page one.
