REACTION

Okay. "Never. I'd never share your *photograph*."

Helen Rivers, I see you. She asked him to promise he'd never share *her* — "you'll never share me with anyone" — and he answered a different, narrower question, and he answered it in that exact, careful, each-word-set-down voice the book has taught me to hear as the sound of the machine choosing its liability. Because he *can't* promise never to share her. He shares her every week. Sharing her is the founding act of the relationship. She is, structurally, a shared document with edit access for two, and the one honest thing in that promise is its precision: the photograph, the file itself, he'll keep. Everything the photograph depicts already travels down the wire every Saturday — half of it carried by her own mouth, over mimosas, to the woman she thinks is her best friend. She set the one condition that was already broken before she spoke it. I had to put the book down and walk a lap of the kitchen. That's the jacket knife again — nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over — and the chapter literally stages it: *she* offers the picture. He doesn't ask. He just says "sometimes I take care of it myself, thinking of you," and lays the bait on the pillow, and thirty seconds later the offer comes out of her mouth as her own idea, tapping her tooth, brave and glowing. This is the rehearsal for the big one. This is exactly what my notes said to watch for — the ask engineered to be authentically hers — and the book kept its eyebrow up through the whole thing. The seeding is on the page. You can watch it happen. She can't.

And the posing. God, the posing. She goes and puts her face back on — arranges herself, composes the woman she wants him to keep — and he says *no, not like that, you'll have to wait*, and takes even that. She offered him a gift and he assumed art direction of the gift. Hand here, knee carried a little ahead, a kiss at each point where his hands set her — which is genuinely one of the hotter things in this book, I'm not made of stone — but clock what the prose does at the end of it: she looks at the picture and finds herself beautiful, and "some part of why she could think so was that the picture was his, was her as his eyes had her." Her own reflection now routes through him. Same chapter, downstairs: "She had used to just eat. Now she tasted, and there was no going back to the other, and she knew exactly whose doing that was." He has rewired her palate, her mirror, her skin (the sheet's thread-count from the spanking, last stretch), and she experiences every rewire as gift. It IS gift. That's the horror. The drawer chapter made me cry a little and this chapter shows me the price of the drawer: he makes room for her everywhere, and in exchange she is being remade sense by sense, and there is no going back to the other.

"He's all mine," she thinks, with his head on her breast. I have chapter 24 in my notes in ink. He is not all hers. The lamplight tenderness is real — I believe every degree of it, that's what makes this book unbearable — and it is also inventory. I can't stop being the reader who knows what he does with the details of that face.

"I don't go back." The parents door didn't just stay shut, it got a voice — level, exact, each word set down clean and apart — and she took her hand off it, and I actually respected both of them in that beat. The desk in the hallway is behind that door. The mother who was taken out of the answer is behind that door. Whatever made a boy into a man who converts punishment into reward lives there, and the book is making me wait for it, and fine, I'll wait, I've been trained too.

And then Thanksgiving hands me two gifts and a dread. Gift one: Sheri, at his table, with a pie she'll "pretend she made" — in THIS book, where everyone pretends authorship of everything, where Randi re-authors memories and Pace ghost-writes Vee's own desires, the throwaway joke about faked authorship of a *pie* nearly took my head off, and I don't for one second think Helen Rivers put a pie there by accident. The clean witness is getting closer to the center. Gift two: the jealousy flicker — Vee felt it, small and low and all her own, another woman at his table — and then extinguished it herself, reasoned it away, and I sat there thinking: that's a skill you're being given a lot of practice at, honey, and someone is going to need you to have it. The dread: Vee flies home for Thanksgiving. Which means a long weekend where the two authors have the manuscript to themselves. What gets written while she's gone is what I turn pages for now.

Also, small and cold: the photo went to Delivered. It lives on his phone. Randi debriefs in his bed. The next brunch is the test — if a camera, or a pose, or "did he keep a souvenir" comes up over mimosas, the syllabus is live again and I want to see whether Rivers makes me watch the exact moment "I'd never share your photograph" gets honored in the letter and violated in every other way there is. I think she will. Thirty-seven chapters and she hasn't flinched once.
