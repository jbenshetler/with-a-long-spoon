REACTION

Helen Rivers left Vee crying on a dark porch, engine cold, "Vee" said once behind her — and then opened the next chapter on stone columns and beveled glass in Gstaad. I actually said "no, go BACK" out loud. And then within a page I forgave her completely, because this is the chapter I've been asking for since the glass room: Randi, inside, sustained, no brunch table between us. Forty-five chapters of watching her manage everyone and now I get to watch her manage herself, and it's worse, and it's so much sadder than I braced for.

Start with the bracelet, because I've been waiting on it for a hundred pages: "her own bracelet, plain white gold, locked on her wrist, and kept it out of the light." LOCKED. She wears Pace's lock to her parents' breakfast table and hides it from the window's light while her mother's tennis bracelet plays with it. She wears it into John's bed. It never comes off. That's the answer to "whose band Randi wears" and it was delivered in a subordinate clause at a breakfast about steak, and I had to put the book down. She had it on the whole night. Pace was in the room the entire time and John never knew, and neither, really, did the narration until I made it.

And the breakfast itself — "eating her revulsion," the invented boys who "always had been" safer than the real ones "at this table." *Always had been.* That's her origin story in four words. This is a girl who learned in childhood that the true thing doesn't survive her parents' table, so she got brilliant at serving the fabricated thing, and thirty years later that skill is called Randi. The mother who hugs "all edges and points." Central Florida, "and did not mention it." New money, loud father, keep your eye out for an eligible bachelor — and the chapter never says MIRH once, which is the loudest thing in it. She sat across from the fraud and ate smoked salmon and made up boys for him. The re-authoring I predicted after chapter 39? This is what it looks like. It looks like breakfast.

The John section is diabolical because Rivers writes the wish-fulfillment *straight*. Meet-cute in the lift line, the fake marriage with three invented children (Randi supplying the names without missing a beat — of course she did, inventing a family is her first language), the ball, the sleigh, the midnight kiss with silver coming down. For a stretch I genuinely wondered if the eyebrow had finally dropped — "she thought she would remember this one the rest of her life" reads like a different, dumber book — and then I realized that's the trap and I was in it, same as always. Because every glittering beat has a bruise under it if you're reading with the lights on: warm only along the seam where she touched him. The cold that "did not belong to the room." Her body spending itself "from a long way off." And then the autopsy: "It had meant no more than a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon." A faultless man in a faultless room, "the best the acceptable world could do for her," and — the line that gutted me — "Nothing had happened to her eyes."

But here's where my alarms and my heart went off at the same instant. "Pace made her say it, out loud, every time. She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked." John leads so there's nothing to do but let him; Pace's lead is "the lightest pressure... and it left the rest of the way to her. That was more work." This is the most honest thing anyone in this book has said about the machine: the asking IS work, consent-as-architecture is exhausting from inside, and being assumed can feel like rest. A naive book would use that as the alibi — see, the safe script is hollow, so the manipulation is the real love. I watched for it. I don't think Rivers is doing it, because she immediately shows us what "not being asked" purchases: a night worth exactly a sandwich, a man who "never found her underneath." She's not ranking Pace above safety. She's showing us why Randi can't leave him, which is a different and much more frightening fact.

And then Vee. God, then Vee. "Something came off Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday that this room could not counterfeit one thread of." Randi, who can run any scenario to the end, cannot run the one where she says it to Vee's face — and so she chooses, in cold sentences, the exact method. Read it again: "Let the thing keep the shape it was already taking. Let Vee arrive at it on her own. Keep the brunches exactly as they were... as long as the word kept its cover." That is the machine's patent — the engineered arrival, the choice that will feel like hers — deployed now not as a game but as *cowardice in love*, which is how the disease survives even sincerity. "Vee heard it one way; Randi meant it two ways." And the horrible symmetry Rivers has built: Vee just spent four chapters starving to death for one withheld word from Pace — "words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me" — and the chapter ends with Randi withholding the same word from Vee by the same logic. Three people, all actually in love, and the word is currency none of them will spend first. Pace holds it as policy. Randi holds it as strategy. Vee holds it as fear. The book's engine is a single unsaid sentence now and I can feel it running.

The text exchange nearly broke me. Vee — who walked out of Pace's house into the cold, whose own heart is in pieces, home in Ohio with parents who love her out loud — spends the last minutes of her year on "i'm here if you want to talk. or not." Even shattered, she's the only one at that three-way table who gives without invoicing. And Randi's reply is the whole novel in miniature: "Every word of it true. None of it the thing." That's the jacket. That's "I'd never share your photograph." That's every promise in this book — honored in the letter, hollow at the center. And "Vee was her gorgeous" — the endearment turned into a possession, tender and proprietary in the same breath, and I believed both.

What I don't know: whether "Pace on his worst night" means Randi knows about the fight — whether Vee's walk-out has already traveled down the wire. And I notice the book skipped right over whatever happened after that door closed. Rivers is doing the withholding to me now, again, and I'm holding my plate out, again. One more thing, filed in ink: Randi stood at that mirror on New Year's Eve thinking "this was the night the rest of it started" — about the wrong person, in the wrong room, wearing another man's lock. The jacket promised me the best *year*. The clock just turned over.
