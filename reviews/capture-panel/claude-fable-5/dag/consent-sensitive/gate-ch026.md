The dance lesson is the whole book performed on a concrete pad, and I want it on record that I saw every layer and stayed anyway, grinning like an idiot, because Helen Rivers is not hiding it from me. "Give me something to push back against. Being jelly doesn't work." That's it. That's the machine's engineering spec, said out loud, in the open, by the machine. He doesn't want a limp girl — he *needs* her will, her pressure, her actual pushing-back, because that's the surface the lead transmits through. Consent isn't the loophole in this book, it's the drivetrain. And then the sentence that made me put the book down on my chest for a second: the frame "held her in the one place the next step could come from, so there was nothing to do but take it." He builds rooms. Now he's teaching her to *be* the room — closed all the way in, no space kept, because the space you keep is what gets you stepped on. Twenty-six chapters and the prose has never once blinked about what it's describing.

And then — THE THREAD. I have been waiting since the brunch for her fear to surface again, I wrote it down, I said "whether anyone's there when it does," and it surfaced mid-spin — *wish laid over man so neatly she couldn't find the join* — and nobody was there, because the thing that was there was HIM, and "he turned her, and the question went where the count had gone." The lead ate the doubt. The doubt about the lead was dissolved *by the lead*. I'm genuinely a little breathless at how mean that is, structurally, wrapped in the single most pleasurable dance scene I've read in ages, because that's the book's whole deal: the anesthesia is real. The dancing IS that good. That's why the question goes where the count went.

The blowup, though — the blowup is why my capture number is what it is. That was the first event in this entire novel that *neither author planned.* One jealous blonde with an agenda, thirty seconds of poison, and Vee goes off every rail anyone ever laid for her. And what did I learn? Two things, both terrible. One: the rewiring is site-specific. All that gorgeous shame-into-heat conversion works in rooms he built — the sun porch, his bed, his mirror. Out in a bar full of rivals the old wiring fires raw and unconverted, and she reaches for her mother's knife and puts it in herself — "spread her legs like a slut," three dates — the exact word Randi tossed at her as a toy in stats class, come home with the safety off. His healing doesn't travel. Which means it isn't healing, quite. It's climate control.

Two: when she goes off-script, his system has no move. The man who patented pulling signal from noise met noise he didn't engineer and his whole play was — flat answer, set the beer down, leave. And I cannot collapse it and I've stopped trying: real hurt, or withdrawal as leverage, and the horror is it doesn't matter, because the rupture-repair cycle will bind her to him tighter than any planned date ever could. She spent the night doing his advocacy *for* him, prosecuting herself, and he wasn't even in the room. The machine profits from its own accidents. That's when you know it's a good machine.

It has still never dropped.

The shirt gutted me. Rationing the smell of him, then the machine wash — "fabric softener and no one" — and she puts it on anyway. That's the loneliest sentence in the book so far.
