REACTION

The diptych. That's what got me — Rivers put the two texts in chapter 46 and then made me watch them get written and received from the other bed, and both messages are lies of the exact same species: *every word of it true, none of it the thing.* Vee reads her own text twice "for anything that gave her away." Randi checks that her smile still works in a hotel mirror. Two women, six time zones apart, each lying alone next to the shape of a warmth she can't keep, each believing the other one is living the real life. That's not a plot beat, that's a thesis statement, and it's gorgeous, and it hurt.

But here's what I actually sat up in bed about: **the silence is the move now.** I wrote it in ink weeks ago — the stopping is the move, he composts the no — and this chapter is that mechanism running at continental scale. She walked out of his house and he has sent *nothing* across the entire break. Not a word. And look what the vacuum is doing: she's inventing a blonde to sit in her own falling sheet, she's rereading old messages like a sore tooth, she's typing *are you—* and deleting it letter by letter, and the line that made me put the book down for a second — "He left you the room. He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now." The room-leaving was the whole courtship. It was the thing the jacket sold me: *he asks before he takes and leaves her the room to choose.* And now the room is a torture chamber, and Vee is inside it doing his work for him, building her own worst rival out of spite and lavender air freshener, and when she crawls back — she will crawl back — the return will be, as always, authentically, engineeredly *hers.* Ten seconds of empty palm in a bedroom; two weeks of empty phone across a break. Same physics. I can't tell if he knows he's doing it, and honestly the not-knowing is the scariest version, because either he's the most patient predator in fiction or he's a man so walled-off he'd genuinely let her go rather than spend one word — and both of those men produce the identical silence. The book knows I can't tell. It built the ambiguity on purpose. That's why I'm still here.

And the crop. She scissored her own leg off the photograph — the exact thing that made her feel like herself — before handing it to her mother. "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it." That's forty-seven chapters of the partition problem in one image, and it's *her* hand on the scissors now. Nobody made her. Nobody has to make her do anything anymore; that's the whole horror and the whole book. The room that "kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed" — the little gold swimmers, the girls who haven't texted in a year — I felt that in my chest. Whatever else this book is doing to her, Vivienne was starving in that room long before anyone laid a table. The jacket said so. Rivers keeps receipts.

The dramatic irony is almost cruel now, and I mean that as praise. Vee lies there envying "a poisoned Gstaad" — she *knows* it's poisoned, she was in the glass room, she's still holding MIRH like a stone in her cheek — and envies it anyway, because envy doesn't ask permission. Meanwhile I know what the fairytale actually contained: a faultless man Randi felt nothing inside of, and a decision, made at that same mirror, to let Vee "arrive at it on her own" and keep the word under cover. And chapter 47's last pages show me Vee already arriving: Randi is "the one sure thing the new year held," a day she can circle, a face that will call her gorgeous and mean it — and Pace comes with "no hour she could name." Randi decided not to tell her, and the not-telling is going to work better than telling ever could, because Randi learned withholding from the master. The 7 a.m. class starts with Vee starved, nameless, and cropped in half. Randi doesn't even have to aim anymore. The convergence is set and both women think it's fate. "Randi, the year's gift she had literally stumbled into" — Vee, honey. You were *steered.* The jacket told me before you ever walked toward him.

One small knife I want on the record: Sheri. "She's alone," filed away as pity in November, when Vee was the one he cooked for — and now Christmas is a week she can't see into and the pity has curdled into surveillance she can't perform. Rivers planted that pie two months ago just so it could go off here, quietly, in a twin bed in Ohio. And the clock: the year turned at midnight and the jacket promised the best *year* of her life, and I sat there listening to the poppers down the street thinking — is somebody's timer running. The scrunchies say a long while. Daphne says two years, sensibly. Midnight says something.

The chapter is quiet — no sex, no brunch, no lamp clicking brighter — and it still pulled like a tide, because it's the first time the book has shown me Vee with the machine fully out of range and the hunger still running. That's the finding. The conversion is portable. The famine travels. He doesn't have to be in the room anymore; he doesn't even have to text.
