Okay. Okay okay okay. This is the chapter I said I was waiting for — "Randi wanting Vee for herself is a live wire that has to arc eventually" — and it arced, in a fire lane, and I've been sitting here with my hand over my mouth like I'm Vee on the curb.

Let me do this in order because my heart rate demands it.

The lunch is an interrogation and the book knows it's an interrogation, and what wrecks me is that it's ALSO the best lunch of Vee's life, and both of those are fully true at once, which is this book's whole signature move. Watch the methodology — I couldn't stop watching it. "Nobody measures over a bra" — the easy authority of a person who'd had a great many things made, delivered like the salad-fork rule, dissolving Vee's shame so the confession can pass through the space where the shame was. Then "were there any — challenges?" with its little hook. Then the cold-reading run: I bet he started at your hip. Which hip? The left one. "All the best ones start at the left hip" — WHICH MEANS NOTHING, the book says so, "landed like a benediction," and Vee gets led down "one small yeah at a time, down and down." That's not a friend asking about your date. That's an extraction with the room-temperature raised and Sinatra on. The chapter is called "How It's Done" and I want to shake Helen Rivers' hand and also back away from her slowly, because yes — that's how tailoring is done, and that's how this is done.

But here's the thing that has me pacing: I don't think Randi was collecting for Pace. Not this time. The notes said her attention was "wound a notch tighter than the coffee places had ever drawn out of her" — she wanted this story for herself. She was starving for it. And then the tell, the beautiful terrible tell: Vee glowing about Pace — he looked at me and I was fine — and Randi goes still, smile held by a muscle, thumb working the water glass one slow degree against the cloth. Vee reads it as "maybe she's moved, maybe she's happy for me," reaching past the true thing for the kinder thing, and the book flags the reach in real time — "the way you reach for the kinder thing because you want it to be true." That's my whole condition for staying with this series, executed in a single sentence. Randi is jealous. Of him. Of the fitting. Of the box and the kneeling and the being-seen. She built this machine and now she's standing outside it with her face against the glass.

And then. THEN. "You don't have to tell me."

I've read that line four times and I still can't decide what it is, and I suspect that's the point. Vee is dreamy, open, three words from handing over the deepest thing she has — the thing she never even told Cassie — and Randi, who has spent forty-five minutes engineering exactly this moment, closes the door. Softly. Kindly. Is it mercy — real feeling jamming the mechanism? Is it jealousy — she couldn't bear to actually hear Pace's triumph narrated? Or is it the coldest technique in the book — leave the confession half-birthed, "her secret still half out of her with nowhere to go," so that the pressure of the unsaid thing does the work, so Vee spends the next week aching to finish the sentence? Because a confession refused is a confession owed. I genuinely don't know, and the fact that all three readings hold — that's why I'm still here. If the book had told me which one it was, I'd trust it less.

The scarf story. Oh, I have notes on the scarf story. Randi has a documented history of manufactured anecdotes (Switzerland, lacrosse, the disaster date), and this one arrives carrying a payload: tied wrists, helplessness, "somebody takes the reins and you can't hold them even if you wanted to, and it turns out that's the only time some of us ever put them down." That is curriculum. That is doctrine being installed over tomato soup, ahead of whatever's coming, and "a little helpless can be a very good thing — done right" is going to echo in some future chapter and I already dread the acoustics. But it's worse than curriculum, because I know what I know: the last time Randi was actually helpless was on that bench, and she survived it by deadbolting herself away forever. So either the story is fake — grooming material, pure — or it's real and she's re-authored her own breaking as a party anecdote, "I went off like a rocket," the wound retold as a win. Both readings make me want to lie down. And Vee's little question — "weren't you scared, though?" — coming "from nearer than she liked." Vee's body already knows what class she's enrolled in.

Small things I'm filing: the white gold band Randi never takes off, plain and heavy, no stone, the one fixed thing — the book pointed at it deliberately and I want to know whose it is. Brooke's? His? The money texture killed me — Vee reading the right side of the menu "the way you read a road in fog," the glass of Rombauer costing more than everything she ate, and her putting her card down anyway because "the one thing she would not do at this table was let Randi buy her seat at it." That's Vee's dignity, intact and expensive, and Randi watching her pay and not reaching for the folder again — respecting it, or pricing it. Probably both.

And the kiss. The book did the fire lane twice — Pace pulls into the fire lane and a cop moves him along, obedient, taillights gone; Randi stops in the fire lane "like fire lanes were a thing that happened to other people." She stood on a box bare-chested for a man and felt like she was supposed to be there. One two-second kiss from a woman and she's counting dark windows for witnesses. The shame lands exactly where her mother installed it and nowhere else, and the book knows precisely what it just showed me. "And Vee never lets herself notice how much she grows to want Randi, too" — the jacket said it, and this chapter is that sentence made flesh: the heat with "no shelf for it," the looking too long at the jaw and the collarbone, the two fingers to her mouth "without deciding to."

Where I am: scared for Vee in a new direction now, because Pace's machine at least has rules — asks first, waits, leaves the room to choose.
