REACTION

The "Peter" gutted me. Barely the breath of it, to the sleeping shape of him. She's carrying the true name around all week like a stone in her mouth and the only place she can set it down is next to an unconscious man's ear. She loves Peter — the boy with the desk in the hallway, the one who got handed to a nineteen-year-old as a reward — and she can only love him out loud when he can't hear it, and he can only be Peter when nobody's checking. These two are conducting an entire second relationship in a register neither of them will run while the other is awake, and Helen Rivers just lets it lie there in the dark, ticking.

And then the passage I'll be chewing on all week: "he felt it in everything he did and said it in nothing, and she found, tonight, that she could live clear inside the everything-he-did and not go hungry for the saying." The book reached back into its own opening vocabulary — the quietly starving girl — and had her teach herself that not being fed is a kind of fullness. That's the eyebrow, still up, doing its job: she uses the hunger words to describe learning to stop wanting the meal. Because here's what I know that she doesn't, what the jacket promised I'd know: a man absolutely does do all of that for a girl he's only passing a season with. He did two years of it for himself, on the receiving end, and it ended "sensibly," and he filed it as the best thing that ever happened to him. Her entire proof of love — *nobody builds like this for something temporary* — is disproven by the one story he told her with the serial numbers filed off. He says it in nothing because nothing can't be quoted later. Every act is deniable; a sentence isn't. I don't even think he knows that's why. The machine builds in deniability the way his hands square a seam — habit so deep it looks like grain.

The weight-loss paragraph, quietly, might be the most frightening thing in the chapter. Her body "quietly burning down whole weekends of him, and she'd never asked it to." Add it to the ledger: she smells of his soap, wears his deodorant, sleeps in his robe, and now her actual mass is being converted, metabolized by the relationship itself, without her asking. The book logs every one of these erasures in the same flat voice and never once cues me to swoon at them, and that restraint is why I'm still here at thirty-five chapters.

Now the drawer. Look — I know what the drawer scene is. I have read four hundred romances; the drawer is the genre's sacrament, and I am not immune, and when she pulled the second one open and found his whole careful life compressed to make her the easy reach, the top one, the one your hand goes to first — I felt it. Actually felt it, warm, in the sternum. A man folding himself smaller to make room is the real thing, and the title tells me the book means it: *Above Him.* He put her above him. In his one bathroom, in the load-bearing geography of a fastidious man's morning, that is not nothing.

But watch what actually happened, because I did. In the mirror he *read* her — watched her worry flicker over the counter mess, held on it a second, said nothing. She spent a week carrying "had he minded, she couldn't tell, he never said." And his answer to her anxiety wasn't a sentence — "you're not a guest," six words, free — it was infrastructure. She had to open drawers in his house to find out her own standing. She learns her welcome the way she's learned everything about him: by archaeology. The dress, the mirror with the bright screw heads, the pedestal, now the drawer — he doesn't tell her things, he builds rooms where her feelings resolve on their own, and she experiences the resolution as her discovery. "There was no note. There was never a note." The book italicized his whole operating system in five words and I want to believe the never-a-note is love's shorthand and I know, I *know*, it's also the signature of a man who leaves no text in anything.

And the grimmest thought is mine alone, so I'll put it here: a drawer is also the unit of eviction. You can only be moved out if you were first moved in. Daphne presumably had a drawer. Somewhere in the architecture of this tenderness there may be a date, and the scrunchies bought in bulk — "he meant to be looking at her hair a long while yet" — a long while is an interval too. I keep hearing the jacket: the best *year* of her life.

No Randi this chapter, and I noticed how much I needed the quiet after that pastry glaze on Vee's lip — and I notice Rivers giving me exactly the recovery chapter I needed, right on schedule, which is the author running Pace's method on me again, feed and rest, feed and rest. It works. I resent it and it works. And the chapter ends with Vee putting her hair up and going to find him — going, maybe, to say a word out loud in daylight — and it cuts before speech, the way this book always cuts before speech. If she says "Peter" to his open eyes, or worse, the other word, I need to see his face do something it hasn't rehearsed. That's what I'm turning the page for.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *she can only say his real name when he's asleep and he can only say I love you in furniture and I'm supposed to survive whichever one of them slips first.*
