REACTION

Forty-nine chapters and Helen Rivers finally opens the last locked room in the house: Pace's own head. I've been waiting for this and dreading it in equal parts, because a POV chapter for the charming predator is where naive books die — you go inside and find out the author thinks he's just a misunderstood sweetheart. So I went in with the instruments out. And here's my honest reading: the chapter is warm, genuinely warm, the warmest he's ever been — and the eyebrow still didn't drop, because Rivers put Sheri in the booth across from him and let her do the indicting to his face. "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." Fifty chapters of everyone managing everyone, and the five-foot cyclist with no stake is the first person in the entire book to hand Pace an unvarnished bill. That's not naivety. That's the book using its one clean adult exactly the way I always trusted her to be used.

But let's talk about what this cozy little diner chapter actually detonated, almost in passing, the way this book always does its worst work. "That brunette you brought dancing before Thanksgiving." I'm sorry — WHAT. Vee spent chapter 44 crying in the dark asking if they'd ever be seen in public again, got a Chili's and a sensory-overload alibi that I half-bought — "in a room like that I can't hear you," and it was tender, and it was TRUE, and it was also, it now turns out, not the whole ledger, because he takes Randi out dancing. In public. At the hall. Where Sheri hugs him hello. The loud-room explanation is real AND it's cover: he can do public just fine, in the rooms he's chosen, with the woman who's already inside the machine. Vee is begging for daylight while the co-author gets the dance floor. And the phrasing of Sheri's question — "She find out about the brunette, then?" "No. Not like that." — so Sheri knows there IS a brunette to find out about. The whole county road knows more about Vee's relationship than Vee does. "Not like that" is a Pace sentence through and through, a trapdoor with a smile on it: she didn't find out, and also what happened wasn't that, and both halves are load-bearing and neither is the truth.

And here's the thing that actually made me put the book down on my chest for a minute: he tells Sheri the truth. "I can't." Vee stood in his front room, crying, asking the direct question with her whole body, and got "words are cheap." Sheri gets fries stolen off his plate and the naked confession — I can't, not I won't, I CAN'T — the same machined-shut voice as "I don't go back." Everybody in this book gets a truer Pace than Vee does. Randi gets the debrief. Sheri gets the confession. Vee gets the infrastructure — the thermostat, the drawer, the two shirts in a warm kitchen — and never one sentence that could be quoted back. He'd rather build her a cathedral than sign his name. And when he lists what he loves about her? "She gets what it's for. The philosophy of it." Sheri hoots at him and she's right to, but I heard the colder thing under it: he loves her for appreciating his outputs. The meal, the dress, the method. Even his love runs through the things he makes. The air "crackles" — that's the truest and most helpless thing he's ever said, and it still isn't her name for what they are, and it never will be until something breaks him open.

What softened me, and I'm going to admit it because honest inventory is the whole job: "he would not bring her down today" — walking in already deciding to carry his weather alone so the lonely girl with the dead family gets a good burger. The paid-for single-and-shake choreography. "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice" — which, by the way, quietly reframes his own sealed parents next to Sheri's graves, and I noticed the book noticing the difference: Paige told Sheri to call her mama and there's no mama to call; Pace has doors he could open and welded them shut, and he called his own version "grief" by proximity and Sheri let him and I didn't. And "Her name is Vee" — he corrects the name and lets "big tits" ride, and I laughed and winced in the same beat, which is this book in miniature.

Sheri, meanwhile — Paige, the birds, seven at the table, "what always happens" — I would die for her, and Rivers just made her queer and grieving and part-time-at-school with nobody writing checks, which means she now has her own hunger in a book about hunger, and I am BEGGING the machine to never notice her. She's the one room in Pace's life that isn't engineered, the way the pool is Vee's. First Saturday in February, Brooks and Dunn cover band, "I don't care what the redhead's got to say about it" — that's a date on the calendar, and this book keeps its dates. Vee, Pace, Sheri, and a dance floor. Possibly the brunette. I'm already braced.

One more thing I'd text at 1 a.m.: he misses her. Plain, stated, "I want Vee. I miss her." Three chapters of her freezing in twin beds convinced she'd invented the whole thing, building blonde rivals out of spite at midnight — and he's in a double-wide getting told off by a lesbian cyclist because he's gone on her and can't say a word. The dramatic irony runs both directions now and it HURTS, and that's the book working, not the book failing. What I'm watching for: whether "I can't" gets a floor under it — Daphne, the parents, whatever got machined shut — or whether the book lets it stand as romantic obstinacy. If it's the latter, that's when the warmth goes naive. It hasn't yet.
