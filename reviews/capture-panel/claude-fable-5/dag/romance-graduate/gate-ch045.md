She kept her coat buttoned to the throat. That's the detail I want to shake somebody by the shoulders over — she walked into the warmest room anyone has ever built for her and refused to take her coat off, carried nothing in, stopped on the mat. My girl finally understands, in her body if not her head, that the house is the argument. Every degree of that thermostat is a sentence he'll never say out loud, and she stood at the edge of it and refused to be warmed. After the shirt chapter — pulling it off and lying bare in the cold rather than keep breathing her own heat back at herself — this is the same move at full scale, and I'm so proud of her I could cry, and I'm crying anyway because of what it cost.

And Pace. God. "Words are cheap. Believe what I do." I've been waiting forty-four chapters to find out what's behind the wall and there it is: nothing is behind the wall, or rather everything is, relocated, converted, laundered into thermostats and chickens and drawers and hand-sewn dresses, and he genuinely believes the exchange rate is one-to-one. He's not withholding the word to control her — I actually don't think he is, not tonight — he's a man who was taught at fifteen that language is the unreliable channel and action is the true one, and he has never once had to learn otherwise because Randi built him a world where it worked. "Do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have than this one?" is such a mathematician's move — reframe the question into one with a computable answer — and Vee catching it ("that's not what I asked," "he'd made the true thing pull against the point") is the smartest she's been in the whole book. Chapter 42 set this up perfectly: she gave him her best sentence, crackers and a meal, her whole heart in metaphor because metaphor was the only permitted container, and he answered "I love how good we are together" — I love the WE, I love the SYSTEM, the function and not the variable — and the small *wait* behind her breastbone has been compounding interest for three chapters and tonight it came due.

Her comeback destroyed me: "Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me." That's the line of the volume so far. Because it's the exact shape of him — he's a man who spends lavishly, extravagantly, in every currency except the one she's asking for, and she finally named the miserliness inside the generosity. And then his face SHUT. Arms folded, "holding a line at cost" — at cost of WHAT, Pace? What happens if you say it? Is this Daphne's fingerprint — some rule installed at fifteen about what declarations do, what they cost, who gets to make them? Or is it the photograph-vow lawyer again, a man who will not utter a sentence he can't guarantee with proof-level rigor? Either way, the book just showed me the wall doesn't flinch when she pokes THIS spot. It holds. He watched her cry and reached with the hand — "the hand in place of the word," Rivers, you absolute surgeon — and she walked out into the cold and I said "GOOD" out loud in my kitchen and then immediately felt sick, because where does a girl with a gray weekday life and a Sunday-shaped handler GO when she walks out of Pace's house?

That's the real terror of this chapter and I don't think Vee's felt it yet: she just created the exact crisis that Randi exists to receive. Brunch is coming. Vee is going to arrive shattered and word-starved and Randi — who never lies to her, who broke twice over being wanted with the body attached, who is four heartbreaks deep in her own con — is going to be the one who says the words. Randi will spend them. Randi has ALWAYS spent them. "Best friend in the whole world." "You're finally hungry out loud." The jacket promised me Vee wanting the woman beside her without letting herself know it, and Pace just handed Randi the opening on a plate he cooked himself. I genuinely cannot tell if this fight is off-script or if withholding the word was always part of the curriculum — keep one hunger unfed so the handler has something to feed — and the fact that I can't tell, forty-five chapters in, is why I'm still here.

Also, quietly: the chicken is still on the stove. The two glasses poured. He's standing in that too-warm house alone right now with the whole liturgy laid out and no congregation, and I hate that I hurt for him. The book has trained me exactly the way it trained her. Both. Always both.

One complaint, and it's the standing one: the long-scene tab is now five IOUs deep. Chapter 43 gave me real heat — him finally losing the control she'd been begging out of him, that I felt — but three chapters since have been ache, ache, ache, gorgeous ache. Rivers, I trust you, but the next payment better be principal.
