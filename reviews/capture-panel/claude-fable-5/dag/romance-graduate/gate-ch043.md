REACTION

The word count of this chapter that goes to sex versus talk is exactly right and I want to talk about the talk first, because Rivers just did the thing I've been begging every romance writer to do for a decade: she wrote a fight where BOTH people are legible and neither one is a cartoon. "Is this what we are now?" at the wrong volume — a daytime voice in a room built for murmuring — I have SAID that sentence at that volume, in that bed, and felt it land wrong the second it left me, and no book has ever caught the specific shame of listening to yourself not having whispered. And then her escalation in the front room, "you're doing it again, punishing me," when the narration tells us flat out she KNOWS it isn't true, knows who carried the pie up whose porch steps — she says it anyway because she needs the line of it to hold. That's a heroine with actual interiority being ugly on purpose because being ugly is easier than being scared. Thank you. Finally. This is what I left the hockey players for.

But. BUT. Here is what my carry-forward self would grab my wrist about, and she'd be right: I just watched the machine handle a malfunction, and it handled it beautifully, and Vee filed the whole thing under "forgiven" — a word she chose because it "sat well in her," which is the most damning phrase in the chapter. He didn't apologize. He explained. "Places like it are too loud... I'm listening now" — and it's TRUE, that's the horror, it's clearly true, the fork-squaring, the flicker of irritation he hid from her, the shoulders coming down in the truck. Real sensory overload, real Peter underneath, the most human he's looked since the two shirts in his own kitchen. AND ALSO: the net effect of tonight is that she took her one public-facing demand out for a test drive, the outing was ruined — partly by the room, partly by her own ruined palate, he has literally rewired her tongue so that a skillet of nachos tastes like a billboard — and she came home having learned that outside-the-house is where they break and his bed is where they work. The enclosure just got a door that she closed herself, from the inside, and called it forgiveness. "He had ruined it for her, was the thing. Not just this." She SAW it. She saw it for one sentence and then went to him anyway. Rivers keeps doing this — giving Vee the full perception and then letting her body overrule it — and it's psychologically merciless exactly like the jacket promised and I keep saying thank you like an idiot.

The sex. Okay. This is the closest we've gotten to the principal on the long-scene tab and I'll take it as interest paid: he finally LOSES it. She's been begging — the reader's been begging — for the control to crack, and it cracks, over her and into her almost in one motion, shaking, undone, hers. That word, "hers," at the end of the sentence where he spends himself — Rivers put it there so Vee could hold it up two paragraphs later and discover it doesn't prove anything. She took his control apart and she'll have that for keeps, and it still isn't the word. The post-coital ledger — she's his the whole way through, marked all over with where he's been, his spend still warm in her, and what she has of him "lived only here, in the dark, in this bed, and stopped at its edges" — that's the whole book in one clause. It's also, and I want this on the record, genuinely hot AND genuinely sad at the same time, which almost nothing manages. The wanting-him-to-be-rough pooling in her "as her own" — the curriculum teaching her to want the withholding itself — I see you, Rivers.

Two threads from my notes pulled taut here. One: "I love how good we are together" from last chapter now has a sibling — he gave her everything but the name AGAIN, this time with his whole body as the down payment, and the pile she doesn't look at got heavier. Two: RANDI IS STILL OFFSTAGE. Three chapters now. The fight, the sensory-overload confession, the control breaking — none of it debriefed, none of it installed, all of it apparently REAL, and I don't know if that makes me relieved or more frightened, because the book has trained me the same way Pace trained Vee: gentleness with no handler visible reads as either Peter surfacing or a longer game, and I've lost the ability to tell, and Sunday is coming, and when Vee narrates "he lost control inside me" across a brunch table Randi is going to have to hold THAT the way she held the toe, and I don't think she can. The chapter where the machine's two operators find out the machine is running itself is coming and I can feel it.

The one thing I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: "she couldn't hear herself say 'boyfriend' to her mother — she has no name for the best thing in her life, not the big one he won't give and not the small one she can't find." That's not a romance problem, that's the whole architecture: they built her a relationship with no public-facing surface, no word that survives daylight, nothing she could prove or defend or even describe to her own father — and every bit of it she handed over. Every yes freely given. That was the problem. The jacket told me on day one and I'm still falling for it exactly as she does.
