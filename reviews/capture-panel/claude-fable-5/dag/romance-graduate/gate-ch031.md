REACTION

So the induction finally came due, and Rivers paid it in a currency I didn't see coming: solitude. Two full loads of chapters promising dress-heels-mother-detonation, and the actual chapter is Vee, alone, in a warm dull kind room, with neither of her handlers anywhere in the building. Pace in Berkeley. Randi present only as four inches of heel and a frosted-plum thumbprint. And that's when I understood what the chapter was actually testing — not whether the dress works, but whether the curriculum runs when nobody's there to grade it. It runs. It runs beautifully. I don't know whether to cheer or call someone.

Because here's the thing that got me cold before it got me hot: the dress can only be worn naked. He built it that way. "No closure she could even find" — I flagged that sentence chapters ago as the thesis in charmeuse and I thought I was being clever, and now the bill arrives: to wear the most loving thing anyone ever made her, she has to be bare in a room full of somebody's parents. She frames it as her choice — "she had wanted to wear the dress" — and it IS her choice, every yes freely given, that was the problem, the jacket told me on day one. He didn't make it for her. He made it to her. And he's not even there and it's still working on her. That's the scariest version of Pace yet: the one who doesn't need to be in the room.

And then the long step. God. She could have minced the whole walk — the book explicitly gives her the safe path, names it, lets her feel it — and she takes a breath and makes the second step long, and I was up out of my chair with her. The heat coming up "a half-second before her head was sure of it" — that's the exact mechanism from the mirror, from the spank, from the whole curriculum: body votes first, head ratifies. Installed software firing as discovery, which is precisely what I said I'd be watching for, and Rivers had the nerve to make it feel like triumph anyway. Because it also IS triumph. Both. Always both. I'm so tired of this book being right about that and I never want it to stop.

Cassie. CASSIE. "Go make your mother cry again." "Honor student," dry, eyes bright at the corners. Taking the phone, taking the picture, buying the terrible sandwich — Cassie got a whole chapter's worth of love into six lines and my heart broke a little, because she called her "streetlight." Pace called Vee the lamp, the most alive person in any room, and that was the tell that proved he was gone for her — and Cassie, with no protocol, no debrief, no bed to report to, sees the same light and names it in her own dialect. Two people in this book independently describe Vee as a source of illumination and only one of them is being paid, so to speak, and Vee still gives that one whole confessions and this one gets a sandwich. I'm bracing for the Cassie blow harder than ever.

But the moment I'd text at 1 a.m. is the photo. She sends it uncropped to Pace and keeps the secret in the frame: "He would have no idea what else was in the frame. That part was hers." Do you see what she just did? That's a LADLE. That's Randi's signature move — feed him the surface, withhold the marrow — and Vee just executed it flawlessly, instinctively, on the man who taught her appetite. She's not the mark anymore, or not only. My girl is running her own little machine now, one withheld heartbeat at a time, and nobody installed that. Or everybody did. The last line — pride or heat, "or whether, by now, the two came to the same thing" — is the whole novel in one clause and I felt it in my sternum.

Where's the mother, though? Cassie's line implies she exists in this day somewhere and the book never puts her in a chair. Either Rivers is saving her or the absence IS the point — Vee finally in a room her mother's voice doesn't reach, which would explain why the long step was possible at all. And yes, my ledger notes: no sex again, fourth IOU, the tab is getting genuinely insulting — except this chapter was so charged bare-legged on a folding chair that I can't even pretend to be mad. The murmur going down the rows did more for me than most books' full scenes. But Rivers: PRINCIPAL. SOON.
