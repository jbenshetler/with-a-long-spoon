REACTION

Okay, so this is a breather chapter, and normally a chapter where two girls sit in a dorm room and recap a date I already watched would make me itch — I was THERE, Vee, I saw the tree. But it didn't itch, and I've been sitting here figuring out why, and it's because the recap isn't the point. The point is "Nobody did anything to me. I wasn't talked into it. I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose." She's not telling Cassie about the kiss. She's telling Cassie — and herself — that she has agency. On purpose. Me. Look at me. That clumsy spin that nearly takes out the closet door got me more than half the sex scenes in my Kindle library, honestly, because I know something she doesn't: this book's entire thesis, printed right on the jacket, is that her agency is the mechanism of the trap. Every yes was freely given. That was the problem. So watching her hold up "I did it on purpose" like a trophy is genuinely a little sickening, in the way I signed up for. The book keeps handing her true things that are also bait, and she keeps being RIGHT to be happy, which is so much crueler than if she were being fooled by something fake.

And Cassie. God, Cassie is doing the work of a whole Greek chorus with a highlighter and sock feet. "What's his name, so I know what to tell the state troopers when you go missing on a mountain with a man you met at a coffee shop" — it's a joke, and it's also the only person in this entire novel asking the correct questions. "That's the whole address. Off six-fourteen." Cassie, keep pulling that thread! She won't, or she can't, because Vee's happiness is real and you don't put a pin in your best friend's first real joy. But I have not forgotten "Goodbye, Vee" from the lecture hall — the whole word, flat and finished, the word for a longer parting than a Wednesday — and every scene with Cassie now reads like watching someone smell smoke and decide it's probably nothing. Cassie is the person Vee is going to lose in this. I can feel it. The book is quietly building the cost.

Here's the thing that made my stomach actually drop, though, and it's four words: off six-fourteen, past where it goes to gravel. I know that house. I've been IN that house. That's the house with the warm even heat and the honeycrisp sliced thin and the bench with the top that goes back on so it's just a long table again. Randi has been laying her underwear on it. And Vee just recited it to Cassie like a dossier, like plenty — "I know where he lives and everything" — and she knows nothing, she doesn't even know if he's rich, she hasn't clocked that a PhD student shouldn't have a house off a gravel road with handmade quartersawn oak cabinets. Cassie asked the exact right question — is he rich? — and it went into Vee's "best pile there is," the pile of things she'll happily find out. The dramatic irony in this book isn't a device anymore, it's a physical sensation. I'm being made complicit chapter by chapter and I resent it and I would not give it back.

What I'll ding it for: this is the second consecutive chapter with zero heat, and the third where Randi barely exists. I trust this author's pacing more than I trust almost anyone's right now — chapter 1 bought a LOT of goodwill — but I'm starting to feel the withdrawal. I want Randi's POV back. I want to know what she's doing while Pace is holding doors and tearing bread. The jacket promised me lunches too intimate to name and Vee wanting Randi without letting herself know it, and I've had one engineered shoulder-bump and a lavender latte. The Vee-and-Pace courtship is lovely — genuinely lovely, the kind of courtship I'd have killed for at twenty-one — but lovely alone is not why I'm here, and the book knows it, and I can feel it holding the next escalation just out of reach. The hike is at his house. Off six-fourteen. She's going to walk past that bedroom door, or the workshop where he built the bench, and I'm going to lose my mind.
