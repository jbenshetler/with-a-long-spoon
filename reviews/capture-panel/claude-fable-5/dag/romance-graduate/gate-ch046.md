Rivers, you absolute sadist. Vee walks out into the cold with her name — just her name, not the word — hanging in the air behind her, and the very next page is *Gstaad*. I actually flipped back to check I hadn't skipped a chapter. Five chapters of Vee's slow suffocation, the walkout, the door — and the book won't tell me what happened next. It hands me snow and champagne instead. I was furious for about a page and a half. And then I understood what the chapter was doing and I forgave everything, which is, I'm noticing, the exact emotional maneuver this book keeps running on me, and I keep going to it, the way Vee crosses the room.

Because here's the thing about the first half: it's a Kindle Unlimited book. It's MY Kindle Unlimited books. Meet-cute in a lift line, the "play along, I'm with her" bit, the fake marriage banter with three invented children, the white-tie ball, the dress bought that day for that man, the sleigh — an actual SLEIGH with actual BELLS. I have read this book four hundred times. For a minute I thought Rivers had lost her nerve and given me a bonus-novella palate cleanser, and I felt my thumb getting itchy. And then — the lipstick ring on a sleeping stranger, her own mouth doing it "from a long way off," the cold that "did not belong to the room. It was hers." Rivers built the entire fantasy genre chapter, full production values, flawless execution, so that Randi could lie down in the middle of it and feel *nothing* — so that I could feel my own comfort reading turn to ash in my mouth right alongside her. The nachos chapter for Randi. The billboard instead of the painting. She made me eat the trope and taste the packet cheese. I'm a little in awe and a little insulted and I think both are the point.

And the DATA in this chapter, my god. "Pace made her say it, out loud, every time." Stop. STOP. The consent ritual — the asking, the room to choose, the thing that is literally healing Vee, the thing the jacket sold me — Randi has been on the receiving end of it and she experienced it as *work*. She liked not being asked. The exact mechanism that turns Vee's shame into heat is friction for Randi, and tonight she chose the man who assumes, and it gave her a sandwich. So which of them is the curriculum actually built for? And "he was a better dancer than Pace" — she's danced with him, slept with him, weekly, I knew that, but hearing her rank him, hearing that Pace's lead is "the lightest pressure, a thing you had to be good enough to catch" — that's the whole book in a dance step. Pace leaves you the way. Randi doesn't want the way. Randi wants to be taken and it doesn't work, and Pace on his worst night reaches further than the faultless man at his best, and she *wishes it wasn't true.* I have never pitied a villain like this. If she's even a villain. I've stopped being able to hold the shape.

The bracelet. Plain white gold, LOCKED on her wrist, never comes off, kept out of the light at her mother's table, catching on the heel of her hand mid-waltz. Rivers put it in the chapter four times and explained it zero times. That is a collar, or a cuff, or a grave marker, and somebody put it on her — Pace? The husband we've never met? Whoever taught the teachers? I wrote MIRH in my notes with a held breath and now I'm writing BRACELET the same way. Also: "Randi?" "Really?" "Don't you start" — Miranda, confirmed to a stranger before she's ever said it to Vee. And central Florida, "did not mention it." She eats her revulsion at her father's table, invents safer boys for him the way Vee put a question mark on "boyfriend" for hers — both of my girls fictionalizing men for their fathers in the same fortnight — and orders one slice of dark rye, no butter, across from a mother eating egg whites and edges. Starving at her own banquet was my line and this chapter made it literal. The hunger was installed at that table. Someone ran a curriculum on Randi too, and it was her mother, and it taught her to eat nothing beautifully.

And then the text. I could cry about the text. Vee — from the warm kitchen, from the parents who love her out loud, from the wreckage of her own walkout that I still know nothing about — sends "i'm here if you want to talk. or not." The most Vee sentence ever typed, the door left open with no pressure on the hinge, the thing Pace taught her, or the thing she always was. These two people love with the exact same parsed sentence, the truth deployed as cover, and Vee is the only person in this book who says plain things plainly, and she's surrounded.

"Let Vee arrive at it on her own. Keep the brunches exactly as they were." I underlined it and then I sat back from the couch a little, like it might go off. Because that's the whole book, isn't it. That's the invitation. Randi has just decided to run on her own love confession the exact protocol she ran on Vee's seduction — don't ask, don't risk the no, arrange the world so the yes grows on its own and arrives thinking it's hers. She's doing it because she's a coward, and she knows she's a coward, and she names it — "she had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be" — and the self-knowledge doesn't stop her, and THAT is the most honest thing in this novel. Grooming as cowardice as love. Every yes was freely given. That was the problem. The jacket told me on day one and I'm still falling for it exactly as she does.

One more thing and then I'll let myself sleep: the cold. Vee, two chapters ago, pulling his shirt off and lying bare in the cold on purpose because the borrowed warmth was worse than none. One woman took the shirt off and one woman got back in the bed, and I think that's the difference between them, and I think the book thinks so too, and I don't know yet which one it's going to punish for it.

No, the sex with John being summarized doesn't count as fade-to-black, before anyone asks. The closed door WAS the scene. The nothing was the content. Rivers has earned that one.
