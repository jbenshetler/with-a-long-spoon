REACTION

Twenty-two. I had to stop and reread it. Twenty-two. I've had "late twenties" in my head for twenty-four chapters — the house, the patent, the woodshop, the *competence*, everything about him reads like a man with a decade of adulthood behind him — and the book just quietly told me he's a year older than Vee and I built the rest myself. Which is the point, isn't it. Everyone builds the rest themselves. The adults around him at fourteen did. Daphne did. I just did. He's been carrying a grown man's life since he was a kid because nobody ever let him be one, and I fell for it too, from outside the book.

And then Daphne. God. I've been waiting for Daphne since the scar in chapter 18 and I thought she'd be a bad breakup, maybe the person who hurt him. I did not expect the book to hand me *this*: they assigned her to him. A tutor. Nineteen, and he was fifteen, and it went on two years, and he tells it in that terrible careful voice — every reason set in its slot, "nothing happened, nobody did anything wrong" — and Vee does the arithmetic and holds the word down under her tongue, and I was holding it down with her. Because it's the right word and it's also not hers to say, and the book knew both things at once. He handed her the thing that hurt him and named it luck. That sentence is going to live in me for a while.

Here's what it does to the whole architecture, and I can't stop turning it: Pace was the mark once. Someone older, someone he trusted, someone who was *the soft middle* of the loneliest years of his life — and every yes he gave her was freely given, and that was the problem. The tagline isn't about Vee. It's about all three of them. And now his consent practice — the asking, the waiting, the chin cranked up, the door left shut until it's hers to open — it isn't courtliness anymore. It's a man who knows in his body what it is to give a yes inside a room somebody else built, and who has sworn nobody will ever wonder that about a yes they gave him. Which makes what he's doing to Vee — the plan, the game with Randi — almost unbearable. He of all people. He *knows* what an engineered room does to the yeses inside it, he's the proof it can feel like the best two years of your life and still be the wound you can't name, and he built one anyway. Or Randi built it and he walked in. I don't know yet. But the bill just got so much bigger, because when Vee finds out, he won't be able to say he didn't understand what he was doing. He's the one person in the book who understands it perfectly.

And the mirror to chapter 24 is vicious. Randi in his bed: "She's not ready. She'll get there. You'll want to take your time." And him: "I want her to get there on her own." I read that last chapter as the closest thing to decency available inside the con. Now I read it as Peter, age fifteen, insisting his own two years were freely chosen — *she got there on her own* is exactly what Daphne could say about him. The book is not winking. The book is doing the opposite of winking. It's laying his defense of Daphne right next to his plan for Vee and letting me see they're the same sentence, and he can't.

The chapter itself — before the reveals — was just lovely, and I want to say so before I forget the texture. The badly tied robe she didn't fix. His palm over her breast "without pressing, without asking, as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm" — and no, I don't ding him for the no-ask there; she's naked under his robe with her feet tucked under his thigh, this is a body that's been invited and re-invited, and the whole gesture is warmth, not taking. The saffron old enough to vote. "The name that means love in one mouth means trouble in the other" — he does that thing where he hands her sentence back to her more exact than she knew it was, and I understand completely why she's gone. The math aria — the cup of paint, the coastline, Newton's three sentences, "most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it" — I got a little wet-eyed at that, honestly, which is absurd, it's *math*, but it's him showing her the inside of himself and she knows it. And the hallway. Seventh grade, the desk in the hall, long-division worksheets for a year. "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment." She found the right place without being shown it and he *let her keep her hand there.* That's intimacy. That's the thing I read this genre hunting for and almost never get.

The scar: "Not tonight." And he pressed her hand down onto it and held it there. A no that's also a promise, a door with a time on it — and she turned her palm over so they were holding hands on top of his oldest wound and asked for nothing. These two, when the plan isn't in the room, are the best-negotiated couple I've read in years, and the plan is always in the room, and that's why this book is going to break my heart. I keep thinking: the scar is longer than her hand. More of it than she knew. That's the whole chapter in one image.

One more thing I'd text Cassie if I could reach into the book: Vee, baby, you just learned that the last time someone older and dazzling folded him into an arrangement he didn't fully see, it took him years to even — no. He *still* doesn't see it. And you're in one, and your dazzling older friend just put your shoes on your feet with her own hands and ran her thumb across your toenail and you grabbed her shoulder because the floor tipped. The rhymes in this book are getting loud. Somebody's going to have to pay soon — the meter I set three chapters ago is still running, and this chapter didn't pay the debt but it showed me the currency it'll be paid in. "Peter," said once, softly, to keep it. She has his real name now. Randi's sisters don't even have his last one.
