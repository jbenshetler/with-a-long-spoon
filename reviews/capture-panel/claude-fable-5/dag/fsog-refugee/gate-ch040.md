REACTION

The shirt. The shirt, the shirt, the shirt. I had to put the book down for a second because I was making a noise. She washed it because it stopped smelling like him and then it smelled like *nothing*, and she drove out there with it folded in the bottom of the bag having "nearly lost the nerve" — and then she ASKED. Out loud. "I need you to wear it for me. The whole weekend." Do you understand what that is, forty chapters in? This girl started the book unable to order anything but the cheapest thing on the menu so she could afford the chair, and now she stands in his kitchen and asks for exactly the intimate, faintly ridiculous, wholly specific thing she wants — and he pulls it on right then, over the shirt he's already wearing, and doesn't make her defend one inch of it. Two shirts in his own warm kitchen because she told him to. That's his curriculum working. That's the whole thesis of why I read this genre when it's done right: her ask lands, his answer is his body, nobody performs, and the heat and the tenderness are the same substance. I cried a little at "Smelling like me." I'm not proud. I'm not embarrassed either.

And she reached first again — five nights was HER call, her boundary, for school, and he said "take the time you need, I'll be here" and didn't fight, and the book let her be honest that some unreasonable corner of her *wanted* him to fight. That's such a true, ugly, human thing to admit, and it's also — okay, this is where my other eye stays open. "He only opened the door and waited on the far side of it for her to come back through." That sentence is doing double duty and Helen Rivers knows it. Read it warm, it's the most respectful patience in the world. Read it cold, it's the terrarium: he never has to pull because the room is built so she always walks back in. "I want her to get there on her own." The graceful no-fight cost her more than a fight would have — the book SAYS so — and I can't tell anymore whether his restraint is love or the most refined pressure ever engineered, and I think the point is that it's both, and Vee doesn't know there's a question.

Also. ALSO. "It landed green on the long, unusually proportioned table that stood in the middle of his bedroom." EXCUSE ME. She threw her underwear across the room and it landed on *the bench*. That's the bench from chapter one, I'd bet the emergency credit card on it — the one I've been waiting thirty-nine chapters for the book to hand back, the begging, the mirror, "he gets the face, not me" — and it's just STANDING there in the middle of his bedroom being described in the most studiedly casual clause in the whole chapter, and she "never looked to see where it went." *I* looked. I've been looking since page one. It's furniture to her and a loaded gun to me, and the fact that the book put a green thong on it and moved on without blinking is the most confident thing it's done in ages. The reader ahead of her from the start — that's what the jacket promised, and this is what it feels like: I know what that table is for and she doesn't, and it's ten feet from where he just made her come twice.

The hallway striptease disaster was perfect, by the way — the jeans that were sensible in the car and the enemy now, the one-legged hopping, him holding the bra strap and making her unwind out of it herself, her laughing so hard she had to stop laughing to finish. That's what heat with feeling in it looks like: two particular people, one of them cracking up. "She'd have burned the house down for him" — same, honestly.

And the pizza. "He'd have made the pizza exactly like this for an empty house" — that's the best sentence about him in the chapter, maybe the truest. The care isn't a performance aimed at her; it's just the shape his attention takes, and some of it lands on her and some on a ball of dough, and she doesn't mind which gets more. Cotton candy versus a bowl of cut strawberries — that's her whole arc in one line. He gave her her senses back. And the drawer got a callback — "her space," half her life living in his bathroom now — and I noticed, even while I was melting, that everything of hers keeps flowing one direction, out of the dorm, away from Cassie's cracked-two-inches window, into the house in the trees where the music is always already playing when she walks in. She's never once heard it start. Neither have I.

No Randi this chapter, and after MIRH and "cheating bastards" I felt her absence like a held breath. This is a lull, and this book's lulls are load-bearing. The chapter's called "The New Ordinary" and I don't trust a single ordinary this author builds — she made me a nest chapter on purpose, right before something, and I climbed in anyway. That's the deal by now. I know what the spoon is for and I keep eating.
