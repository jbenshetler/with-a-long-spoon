REACTION

No Pace in this chapter, no skin, and I didn't miss either for one second — that's how I know the book's still got me by the collar. And I need to say something before anything else: the professor told them, weeks ago, that there are two kinds of outliers, and Vee wrote it down without reading it, and I wrote it down FOR her, and now the book has walked her into a glass room and put an outlier on a screen with Randi's name under it. Up and to the right, alone in the white. The kind that's in everything that comes after. I actually put my hand over my mouth at MIRA. Four letters, Randi's own shorthand, her own graph tattling on her own house — she built the visualization that exposed her family. She made it look true because it was.

And here's what the chapter did that I keep turning over: it made Randi human at the exact moment it made her money dirty. Because she didn't know. She walked into that project certain — "he kept people on, that was the whole point, I'll show it" — she was aiming the whole picture at home, proud, and home shot back. "Cheating bastards," ragged, first time said, aimed too near. That's not a woman covering. That's a woman finding out. The Mercedes, the boutiques, the wine ordered without a list, the whole glamour Vee's been starving toward — it's pandemic money that was supposed to keep people like Cassie's dad on a payroll. Cassie's dad got laid off in 2020 and the flat way she set that fact down with no handle on it — Cassie doesn't decorate her wounds either. Both fathers are in that graph and only one daughter knows it.

But the thing that actually scared me — and I mean scared, quiet cold scared, not thriller scared — is what Vee did. She saw it, she read the name, she understood everything, and her instinct, instant, before choosing, was to manage the room. Slide the laptop over. Give Randi somewhere to put her hands. Keep Cassie blind. Don't say the name, don't tell her, then or after. She protected the con artist from the honest friend, and she did it with real love and real skill, and the skill is the problem — because where did she learn that? That's Randi's craft. Steering a room lightly, laughing, as if by accident, so the person in it never notices what's being kept from them. Vee just ran her first Randi on Cassie. In defense of Randi. And she walked out thinking the kindest thing a friend can do is not make you carry it in front of anyone — which is exactly, EXACTLY, the logic Pace and Randi have been using on her for forty chapters. The book didn't wink. It just let her think it and let me hear the rhyme. That's why I trust this author.

And the other thing, the one I'd text at 1 a.m.: for the first time in the whole book, Vee knows something about Randi that Randi doesn't know she knows. Forty chapters of information flowing one direction, every secret pooling at the feet of the two people running her, and tonight the water moved the other way. It's small. It's one point on a graph. But the girl who's been the mark since the jacket copy now holds a card, and she doesn't even know she's holding it — she filed it under kindness. I don't know what the book's going to do with that and I love not knowing.

Also — Randi lied at that first brunch table. "The story was more complicated," her father "kept people on with them, that's what the money was for." She believed it when she said it, which is worse and better at once. Her whole self is built on that man's money, the shoes she knelt to buckle on Vee's feet, the whole aesthetic curriculum — stolen. And Randi called herself the Evil Queen once, as a joke, and it turns out she's the daughter in the tower who never checked where the gold came from. I've spent thirty chapters asking villain or most damaged person. This chapter voted damaged, hard, and I resent how well it worked on me.

Cassie was RIGHT, by the way. Right about the data, right in front of the person it indicts, and she'll never know she won. That's my hill and the book keeps salting it.
