REACTION

Okay, so the trap just closed, and I watched it close, and I'm a little sick about how good it felt.

Because here's the thing — taken alone, on its own terms, this chapter is the exact fantasy I have been hunting through this genre for years to find. A man who reads the problem twice before he touches it. Who asks what HER professor said instead of showing her what he knows. Who writes in the margin instead of over her work. Who gives her back the pencil at the precise line where she can finish it herself. I sat there thinking: that's it, that's the whole ethic, that's dominance as making room — he could solve her in two lines and he doesn't, because the solving has to stay hers. And then "May I call you?" — the *may*, the word I would underline and text to a friend at 1 a.m. with just the caption THIS. This is the man I've been asking the genre for.

Except I know what Vee doesn't. I watched him build a bench with his hands. I watched Randi text under the table — texting *him*, obviously, "she's stuck, come now" — and I watched him walk in on cue and perform a meet-cute that was choreographed as carefully as Randi's shoulder-check on the lawn. The pencil-return, the listening, the not-showing-off — every single thing that made my chest go warm was *staged*. And that's the thing this book keeps doing to me that no other book has: it's not asking me to find the deception romantic. It's showing me that the deception is indistinguishable from the real thing from inside, and making ME fall for it in real time, and then reminding me I fell. "He looked at you like he'd forgotten there was anyone else in the room" — Cassie says it as evidence of sincerity, and it's evidence of rehearsal, and Cassie is the only honest person at that table and she's the one unknowingly selling the con. That gutted me a little.

And Randi. God, Randi. The cup coming up to her mouth twice with no sip. The hand in her lap. She is sitting three feet from the girl she chose, watching her own boyfriend run the play she called, and she is *lit up* with it, and Vee's brain files it as boredom. The title is doing double duty and I see it — Euler's substitution, sure, but also: Randi handed Pace her own bit, watched Vee borrow it ("Yes, like the university"), watched Vee step into a shape Randi built. Vee is being substituted into something. Into Randi's transgression, wearing Randi's lines. And after Chapter 2 — Randi flat on her bed in her shoes, deciding Pace gets the face forever and never her again — I can't tell if Vee is a gift Randi is giving Pace, or a body Randi is putting on the bench so she never has to get back on it herself. Both readings scare me. Both readings are why I'm still here.

What keeps this on the right side of the line for me — barely, and I'm watching it like a hawk — is that the book is not winking. The jacket told me the truth before Vee got a word in. The narration keeps count of every unfinished thought Vee doesn't let herself finish. Nobody has laid a coercive hand on her; the horror is entirely upstream of the touching, in the information she doesn't have. "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." I thought that tagline was clever when I bought it. It's not clever. It's a threat, and Chapter 5 is the first installment on it. The "may I call you" that made me melt is also the bait, and the book knows I melted, and I think that's the point — I'm supposed to be exactly as compromised as Vee is. I am. I hate it. Keep going.

One more thing I can't shake: Cassie's "Goodbye, Vee" last chapter, the whole word, and now Cassie flagging Pace across the room like a good friend, feeding Vee into it without knowing. Somebody in this book can see, and it's the girl in yesterday's shirt, and nobody's going to listen to her. If this book hurts Cassie I'm going to be furious in a way I can't afford at midnight.
