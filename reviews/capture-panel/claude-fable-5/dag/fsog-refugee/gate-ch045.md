REACTION

Oh, this hurt. This one hurt in the good way, the way I keep buying these books hoping for, and I need to talk about "Believe what I do" because I've been living inside that sentence for three chapters now.

Here's the thing: chapters 42 through 44 were building this, brick by brick — the cracker-and-meal sentence, the most beautiful thing she's ever said to anyone, and he answered it with "I love how good we are together." I've been carrying her *wait* behind the breastbone with her ever since the laundry room. And then Chili's, and the shirt worn with nothing under it and torn off again in the cold because the heat in it was her own, given back. That image gutted me — the shirt only warm because SHE was warm. That's the whole question of the book in one piece of flannel. Is any of this him, or is it all her heat, given back to her by well-built rooms?

So she drives over with her coat buttoned to the throat and the coin taste under her tongue, and she does the bravest thing she's done in forty-five chapters. Braver than the long step at the induction, braver than the five nights. She stands on the mat — she doesn't cross the floor, she carries nothing in, she keeps her mouth turned down when he arrives for the kiss — and she asks the question straight, out loud, with her whole chest: *what am I to you?* No performance, no costume, no staging. And I want to be so clear about what I felt: I was PROUD of her. The girl from chapter one couldn't have stood on that mat.

And Pace. God, Pace. "You're the most important person in my life" — which is probably true and is also, I notice, another photograph answer. Another sentence that answers the narrow question because the wide one would perjure him. "Do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have than this one?" — that's the terrarium defending itself, that's a man pointing at the enclosure and saying look how green it is. And she SAW it — "he'd made the true thing pull against the point" — that's the smartest sentence she's ever thought and it's exactly right, it's what he always does, it's what the juice was, it's what the Chili's answer was. He solves the problem she can name so she can't reach the one she can't.

But here's what's tearing me in half, and I have to be honest about it. "Words are cheap. Believe what I do." From any other hero in any other book in this genre I'd roll my eyes — it's the classic emotionally-unavailable-alpha dodge. From Pace it might be the truest thing he's ever said, because his words ARE cheap — his words are lawyered, his words are "I'd never share your photograph" — and his deeds are the heat brought up hours early and the drawer and the dress sewn on a sun porch. His deeds are also the conspiracy. That's the trap the book has built and I'm caught in it right along with her: I genuinely cannot tell whether he won't say the word because of the sealed room where his parents live, because of Daphne, because a man whose every yes was once engineered doesn't trust a word he could be maneuvered into — or because saying "I love you" to a woman he is actively sharing with Randi, a woman whose whole relationship began as a bet, would be the one lie even his lawyer's soul can't structure around. Maybe the word is the single piece of perjury he refuses to commit. And if THAT'S what "words are cheap" means — if he's protecting her from a false word while feeding her true deeds — that is the most heartbreaking reading and I think it might be the right one.

And her comeback. "Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me." VEE. That's the best line in the book. Forty-five chapters and she finally lands one he has no answer for — and he doesn't answer it. He folds his arms and holds the line *at cost*, and the book let me see the cost, and I still wanted to shake him until the word fell out.

The ending did the thing I most needed it to do: she left. She saw the hand lifting — "the hand in place of the word," which is his entire language, the only tongue he speaks — and she refused the translation. Every fight before this ended with her body being settled by those hands, the loud thing set down, a plain question handed back, her walking to him across the room and calling it forgiveness. Chapter 43 was practically a diagram of it — "she told herself the thing that let her go." Not this time. This time she went out into the cold and it was worse and she stayed in it, exactly like she stayed bare in the cold bunk rather than lie in a shirt that promised what it couldn't deliver. She's learning the difference between warmth and the word, and she's choosing the cold over the counterfeit. Given that this whole book is about counterfeit money — MIRH, the bought night, the borrowed shoes — a girl refusing to accept warm deeds as legal tender for the one word is the most important thing that's happened since the bet.

What scares me at 1 a.m. is what's on the other side of that door. She's driving away from his house with two sleepless nights in her, cracked wide open, needing exactly one thing: someone to say the word, any version of the word. And there is a woman in this town who has never once asked but who always, always knows when the fruit is ripe. Randi hasn't been on the page in five chapters and I can feel her the way you feel weather. Vee out in the cold is Vee walking straight into brunch, and Randi will do what Pace wouldn't — she'll spend the words. She'll spend them lavishly. They'll be cheap in a way Vee can't detect and they'll land on the exact bruise Pace just left. The book has spent four chapters carving a word-shaped hole in this girl and I know who fills holes in this book.

Also, quietly: Pace saying her name, once, at the door — "Vee" — and it not being the word. He had one syllable's worth of nerve and spent it on her name. I'm going to be thinking about that all week.
