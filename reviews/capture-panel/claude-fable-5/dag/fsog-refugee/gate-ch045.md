REACTION

Oh, this hurt. Two pages and it hurt more than the whole Chili's fight did.

The shirt. THE shirt — chapter 41's shirt, the one he put on over his own shirt in his own kitchen because she told him to, the scene that pricked tears out of her and honestly out of me too. And now the book walks it back through her hands one layer at a time: over her sleep shirt for the hallway, where it's just a thing she wears. Then bare against her skin, the way it's meant to be, and it works, it always works — until she feels the one thing you can't unfeel: *the heat in the shirt was her own, given back.* He isn't in it. She's warming a smell in the dark and calling it him. I had to put the book down on my chest for a second. That's the exact anatomy of loving someone who hasn't said it back — every object he gives you is a mirror and you keep mistaking your own warmth for his — and the book knew it and set it down without one wasted word.

And the heap. She leaves his shirt where it fell, not folded, not set back on the pillow — in a book where he folds his entire self into a lower drawer and there's never a note, an unfolded shirt on the floor is a scream. She'd rather be bare in the cold than lie inside something that promised him close and didn't deliver him. "It was so much worse, and she did not put it back on." That's the truest sentence about being twenty-one and in love that this book has produced, and it's produced some.

Here's what I keep circling at 1 a.m., though, and what I'd text a friend: he's a mathematician. He doesn't misplace a word — the photograph promise taught me that. So "I love how good we are together" wasn't a miss. He *built* that sentence the way he builds everything, two breaths, hand starting and stopping twice, and he chose the version with "we" and "good" and no "you." And I don't think it's because he doesn't feel it. I think it's because he can't say it while the plan is still standing under the floorboards. Saying the word would be the one object he can't hand her clean. Which means his silence might be the closest thing to a conscience he's shown in forty-five chapters — and it's *costing her sleep*, it's costing her the shirt, it's grinding her down in a dorm bed while Cassie breathes easy below. His restraint used to be the sexiest thing in this book. Right now it's just a bill she's paying and doesn't know it.

Three chapters now inside this one ache — the crackers-and-a-meal sentence, the Chili's blowup, and now this — and I want to be honest: the ache is earned and I felt every inch of it, but the pattern is starting to tighten. She reaches, he gives her everything but the word, she files it in the pile she doesn't look at. The pile is getting heavy. And Randi has been *gone* — no brunch, no extraction, no "just us" — and I feel that absence the way I always feel it, like weather building. Vee is alone in the cold at her lowest point and I know, I *know*, who shows up when Vee is soft and unslept and starving for someone to say what she is out loud. Randi will name her. Randi will hand her the word Pace won't, or something shaped enough like it, and Vee will take it because she's freezing. If the next chapter is a brunch I'm going to read it through my fingers.

Also, quietly: the phone. Her thumb over the empty field, knowing no text could carry the question. That's the loneliest image in the book so far and there wasn't a single person in the room doing anything to her. That's craft I felt in my chest, not admired from a distance.
