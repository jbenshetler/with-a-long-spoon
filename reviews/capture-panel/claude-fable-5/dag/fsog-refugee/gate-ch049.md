REACTION

Oh, so THIS is the conversation I've been waiting on like weather — not the Thanksgiving one, but Sheri finally gets a whole chapter at his table and it fed me exactly the way I knew it would. And more than that: this is the first time in forty-nine chapters the book has let me all the way inside Pace's head, and I want to point out what it chose for the very first sentence of his interior life — a decision not to burden someone. "He would not bring her down today." Of course. Of course that's the first thing we hear him think. Whatever he carried in from the truck "could keep" — and I'm holding THAT now too, a third ticket, because what is it, Peter. What's in the truck.

The Chili's vindication nearly made me put the book down just to walk around the room. I said it in chapter 44 — I felt the wrong note the same second Vee did and she didn't stop to turn it over and I did — that wasn't irritation with her, and now I have it from inside: something in him "came apart very quietly over the hour," her voice down under the noise where he couldn't pull it clear. He told her the truth in his front room that night. "I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you" was literal. And here's the part that makes me ache: he sat in this diner thinking *Vee would either love this place or never see it at all, and he wanted very badly to know which* — he has a place, a quiet amber-glass Elvis-under-the-table place, a place where he could hear her whisper, and he hasn't taken her. He took her question "will we ever be seen in public" and solved it with the Chili's she named instead of answering it with the diner he loves. Take her HERE, you enormous fool. Sheri's already saving you a dance in February.

And then the sentence that stopped my heart in both directions at once: "I want Vee. I miss her." He can say it. To Sheri, in a booth, plainly, the wanting and the missing — everything Vee lay in her childhood bed inventing a blonde over, he said out loud to a five-foot woman with two-tone hair, and Vee will never hear it. That's the specific cruelty this book deals in now: the words exist, they're just never aimed at the person starving for them. "I can't," he said. Not "I won't" — *can't*, and Sheri didn't let him off, and I could have kissed her for it: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." FIRST person in this entire novel to hand Pace a bill to his face. Cassie asks Vee the real questions; Sheri asks Pace. The two poor women in this book do all the honest labor for free while the rich ones extract and the careful ones withhold. And I'd put money on "I can't" living in the same locked room as "I don't go back" — whatever happened with those parents, the word got broken there, and the scar's "not tonight" is probably a third door into the same house. Tickets, plural, all in my fist, all for the same show.

Sheri herself — oh, Sheri. Paige with the too-big laugh, the seven-at-the-table house she liked being in, "call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas" done in sing-song and then killed flat. "Ironic. Isn't it." She's grieving people who are gone-gone — "nobody behind her writing checks," "people who've never lost anything think grief is a choice" — and she's the one who told him at the start "two of them at once, if that's not already complicated, it will be." She sees everything and wants nothing off him, which makes her the only person in his life whose company costs him zero, which is exactly why he can be honest there. Also — hold on — the bleached-blonde hair, "four seconds of her at the bar before she started throwing things." Was the bar-wreck blonde SHERI the whole time? I'd let that scene fade to almost nothing and the book just handed it back with a grin on it. Vee burned a night down over his platonic little sister. That's so human it hurts.

But the chapter didn't let me stay warm, because: "She find out about the brunette, then?" "No. Not like that." *Not like that.* Not "there's nothing to find out." Not-like-that means there is a *that*, an anticipated finding-out, a shape it's supposed to happen in, and he and Randi both know it and I've known it since the jacket and Vee is the only person in her own life who doesn't. And Sheri SAW them — "y'all came in dancing," before Thanksgiving, Randi on his arm at the hall, looking at Sheri like a harem applicant — which means my verdict on the Radford story just got its confirmation stamp: it was Pace, laundered, exactly like I said, and Randi told it to Vee's face as a stranger's story while the dance was still warm. The con has a *social life*. It goes out. It gets seen. Meanwhile the real girl can't get a restaurant.

"Her name is Vee," though. He corrected "the one with the big tits" with her *name*, quiet, immovable, and then blushed over the philosophy of his food and let Sheri cackle at him, and I forgave him about four things I shouldn't have. That's the whole problem with this man and this book: the tenderness is real, I can see it from inside now, brick on brick, every brick true — and the true things are supposed to come to something, and Sheri's right that they haven't, and it's been three chapters since he and Vee were in a room together and the last room they shared she left crying into the cold. I feel that gap in my teeth. Somebody drive somewhere. It's a new year.
