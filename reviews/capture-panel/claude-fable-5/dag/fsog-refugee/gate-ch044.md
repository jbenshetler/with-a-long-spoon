REACTION

Oh, this one's short and it left a bruise anyway.

The shirt. The shirt was supposed to be the safe object — I had it in my notes as one of his love-words, the flannel handed over to be worn all weekend, come back Sunday smelling like him, one of the ways he says the thing without saying it. And the book just took its own tender prop and turned it over in the cold and showed me the underside: *the heat in the shirt was her own, given back. He wasn't in it.* That's the cruelest true sentence in three chapters and it isn't even cruel about him — it's just physics. She's been warming herself and calling it him. I put the phone down after that line and stared at my own ceiling for a minute, because I have owned that shirt. Different man, same shirt. Everybody who's ever slept in one knows the exact moment it stops working, and the book found it.

And the phone. Thumb over the empty field, and there's no text that exists — she's right, there isn't, I've composed that non-text at 2 a.m. myself, every version comes back "small and warm and leaves you exactly here." That's the trap of a man who answers everything kindly except the one question. He'd reply in ninety seconds. It would be lovely. It would say nothing. She knows it before she types a letter, and putting the phone facedown was the most honest thing she's done in three chapters.

Here's what's grinding in *me*, though, three chapters into this run: I still can't tell which Pace this is, and the not-telling is starting to hurt in a way I'm not sure the book has fully earned yet or has earned completely — I flip on it hourly. Reading One: he's Daphne's boy, a man who learned at fifteen that words said in engineered rooms can't be trusted, so he will not put "I love you" into a room he built — he says it in fabric and coffee placed at drinking temperature and the top drawer, because objects can't perjure him. That Pace is breaking my heart on her behalf and his own. Reading Two: he can't say it because saying it to a girl he's currently sharing with Randi would be the one lie he can't lawyer — "I love how good we are together" is TRUE the way "I'd never share your photograph" was true. Narrow answer, wide question. He did the noun swap AGAIN, in chapter 42, and she felt the shapes not match and put it on the pile, and now the pile has a chapter of its own. The book knows exactly what it's doing keeping both readings alive. I hate it. I'm here for it.

What I'll say for this chapter against the last two: forty-three ended with her manufacturing "I forgave him" so she could cross the room, and I was getting nervous — that's the genre move I've walked out on fifty times, the woman narrating her own management. But forty-four doesn't let her keep the blanket. She takes the shirt OFF. Bare, in the cold, on purpose, and stays in it, and leaves his shirt in a heap where a dropped thing lands — not folded, and after forty chapters of this book teaching me that how objects are handled is the whole language, an unfolded shirt from HER is practically a scream. That's not a girl being acted upon. That's a girl choosing the true cold over the borrowed warm, and it's the first thing in three chapters that felt like the Vee who made the second step long. It's tiny and it's the most defiant thing she's done since Halloween.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: three chapters, no Randi, and I MISS HER, which — do you understand what this book has done to me? The farmer's been offstage since the photo left the booth and every one of these quiet aching Pace chapters is time that photo has been out in the world doing whatever it's doing. Cassie is RIGHT THERE, breathing slow in the bottom bunk, two feet under a girl coming apart, window cracked her two inches — and Vee wears the shirt over another shirt specifically so Cassie won't see. The one person who asks real questions is being managed with layers of cloth. Also: he's across town gone down easy, "easy as a man with nothing grinding in him," and she believes that, and I don't. I don't think that man sleeps easy. I think we just haven't been in his head since — ever. We've never once been in his head. Forty-four chapters.

It's a thin chapter, honestly — one room, one girl, one shirt, no scene — but it's thin like a blade.
