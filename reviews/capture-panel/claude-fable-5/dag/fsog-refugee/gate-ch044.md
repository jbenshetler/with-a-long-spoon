REACTION

"Under the Rug." The book named the chapter after what she's doing and then made me watch her do it, and I sat there with my chest tight the whole time because I've BEEN her — lying awake next to a man doing arithmetic on what you have versus what you can prove, and coming up short in the column that matters.

First: she picked a fight she knew was false, and the book said so on the page — "some back room of her knew which of them had gone cold that night" — and I want to kiss Helen Rivers on the mouth for that, because most books in my genre would have let Vee's accusation stand as righteous and made him grovel. Instead he did the thing I've waited my whole reading life for a hero to do when he's wrongly accused: didn't argue, didn't plead, didn't punish. "You know what happened after the dance." Set the true thing down and let it be true. And she was ashamed, and the shame was *correct*, and the book let her have it without humiliating her. That's the psychological exactness the jacket promised and it's still delivering at chapter forty-four.

And Chili's. God, Chili's. I went in braced for him to be embarrassed by her, cold to her, and instead I got — I think I got a diagnosis, almost. The fork squared to the table edge. The eyes that keep slipping and the cost of the bringing-back. "Not the sound of it. The whole of it, all at once. In a room like that I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you." That's not a man ashamed of his girlfriend, that's a man whose nervous system can't run in that room, and suddenly the no-tag flannel is not a quirk, and the house full of made things, and "I don't go back," and the sewing, and the way he speaks in objects because words are expensive — the whole man just clicked half a turn and locked. I've been holding two tickets; I think I'm holding a third now, and this one might explain the other two. "I'm listening now" is going straight into the part of my brain where "we find out" lives.

But. BUT. The nachos broke my heart in a way I don't think the book gets full credit for as romance, because it's the con talking. "He had ruined it for her, was the thing. Not just this." Cotton candy and cut strawberries, a billboard and a painting — she can never go back to fine, and fine used to be survivable, and two people DECIDED to do this to her. Chapter one of the jacket told me: an appetite that can only give her the best of herself by taking it. She just lost Chili's. Small, stupid, real. That's the taking, happening in a skillet of orange cheese, and I felt it more than I've felt some of the reveals-in-waiting.

Now the thing I'm actually chewing on at 1 a.m.: the word. Two chapters running he's dodged it — the cracker-and-meal sentence got "I love how good we are together," which, Peter, a mathematician doesn't misplace a word, you swapped the object of that verb ON PURPOSE — and now she hands him her whole body, breaks his control clean off (and that sequence was gorgeous, the loss of it earned and mutual, her want striking his, no cruelty anywhere, the exact rough she asked for arriving only after everything careful), and still: every last thing but the one. And here's where the book has me split in half. The romance reader in me says: he can't say it, because saying "I love you" across the secret would be the biggest lie in the house, and his silence is the last honest thing he has — it's almost integrity, this withholding, the same integrity as the empty upturned palm. The ledger-keeper in me says: don't you dare romanticize it, he could end her not-knowing any night he chose, and instead he lets her lie awake "marked all over with where he'd been" and unable to name the best thing in her life to her own mother. She can't say what he is to her because he has *made himself unsayable*, and that's not an accident of temperament, that's the plan's climate doing exactly what climates do.

And where's Randi? Two chapters without her and I can feel the vacancy — Vee has no one to take this to. Cassie asked "so what is he, to you" and got "both, probably both." The one person Vee tells everything is the one person whose listening is part of the problem. She is completely alone inside the best relationship of her life and the book keeps calling my attention to it with these dorm-laundry, filed-under, put-it-where-she-puts-things sentences. The pile has a name now. She's building the case file herself and calling it a rug.

She got everything tonight — listened to, answered, taken apart, put back warm — "and could not take a bit of it in." That sentence is going to stay with me. That's what the con costs even before it's found out: it's already taxing every good thing at the point of receipt.
