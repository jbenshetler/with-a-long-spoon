REACTION

Okay, first: the cold open of this chapter is exactly the thing I hunt for and almost never get. He asks her a question *inside* sex — "would you like to taste another woman?" — and her body detonates around the answer before her mind can lawyer it, and then, and this is the part, he *waits*. He deliberately waits until the heat has left the room, until she's dressed and back inside herself, and asks it again in daylight, because — the book says it straight out — her being free to weigh it mattered more to him than the answer did. I actually put the book down for a second there. That's it. That's the sentence I've been trying to describe to people for ten years. The man who wants the yes given by the whole person, not just the nervous system.

Except. Except. One line before that: "He took the body's answer for the true one. He usually did." And my skin prickled, because that is the exact logic every bad book in this genre runs on — *her body said yes, so she meant yes* — and I've left a dozen novels over it. I sat there genuinely unsure whether Helen Rivers knows how loaded that sentence is. And then the daylight question happened and I decided: she knows. She's showing me a man whose instinct is to trust arousal as truth, and then showing him correcting for it, asking anyway. That's the most honest portrait of this kind of man I've seen — the appetite to *know* her running slightly ahead of the discipline to ask, and the discipline winning. I'm watching him very closely now. The book wants me watching him closely. I can feel it.

And Randi. God, Randi is breaking my heart in a way I didn't order and am apparently going to eat anyway. "Not a woman who hungered for another woman, which was unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act. A different owner." That's one of the truest things I've ever read about being closeted — she can't own the want, so she launders it through him, and he *lets* her, hands her the wrapper because the wanting can't come out naked. After the duvet chapter, after "there being no door," watching her do her happy little footwork on the brick because a redheaded girl told her her name — I was grinning and then I was almost crying. Her *yes* squeaking out of her. That girl has never wanted anything out loud in her life and she just did, twice, and she doesn't even know it counted.

But here's where the floor tilts, and I know it tilts because the jacket told me it would: Vee. That collision was gorgeous and it was a *con*. Ten years of dance folded into three seconds so clean the mark apologizes to you. I watched Randi engineer an accident onto a laughing stranger — a girl who laughs with her whole throat bared to the sky, who forgot she could be seen, which is precisely why they picked her — and I felt the exact thing the jacket promised: I'm ahead of her, and I'm falling for it anyway, and I feel dirty and I don't want to stop. This is the tightrope. In any other book the engineered meeting would be sold to me as fate and I'd walk. Here the book is showing me its hands the whole time — Pace at his light pole, "far enough back to be nobody" — and asking me to hold the warmth and the predation in the same frame. Vee hasn't handed over anything yet. Every yes was freely given; that was the problem. Fine. I'll be the one keeping score on whether that sentence stays true.

Small thing that got me anyway: "I'm hungry." "Do you want a cheeseburger?" "No. I can't eat that." And then the last line of the chapter — "Let's go feed you." He clocks the starving. He's been slicing the apples thin since chapter one. Whatever else this man is building, he keeps trying to feed her, and she keeps letting him a little more each time, and that's the tenderness I stay for. Also, texting my friend at 1 a.m.: he pointed at the girl who looked exactly like Randi first, ON PURPOSE, to teach her she was allowed to want something that wasn't a mirror. This man runs experiments on his girlfriend's soul and I cannot decide if he's the best or most dangerous romantic lead I've read this year. Possibly the answer is yes.
