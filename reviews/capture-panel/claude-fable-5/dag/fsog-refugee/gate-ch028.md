REACTION

Oh, this is a sly little chapter and I see what it's doing. Three pages of bicycle and it hands me more intel than some of the lunches did.

First: Sheri. I like her immediately, which I've learned to distrust in this book, but I like her anyway — a small fast woman who's been out-climbing him for two years and "never once let it go unmentioned," who remarks on women's backsides like weather. She's the first person we've met who stands entirely outside the terrarium. Cassie's inside it (she just can't see the glass). Randi built it. Sheri rides past it on a Saturday and says the plain thing that nobody in this whole novel has been allowed to say out loud: "Two of them at once. If that's not already complicated, it will be." Twenty-eight chapters, and the book finally lets an ordinary voice run the arithmetic anybody could run. I actually exhaled. It's not just me keeping the ledger. Somebody on the page is keeping it too.

Second — and this is the one that got me warm — "After you left Thursday, I kept an eye on her." He left the bar. He didn't sulk, didn't hover, didn't do the romance-novel thing where he lurks in the parking lot to make sure she gets home (which some books would sell me as devotion and which would make my skin crawl). But someone he trusts watched, and reported: sat alone, danced with nobody, left alone. And notice what he did with that information: nothing. He didn't text. He didn't drive over. Randi called it exactly — "he leaves you the room and waits to see what you walk in wearing." His care found a way to operate that didn't take up any of her space. That's the whole thing I read this genre hunting for and almost never find, in one sentence about a bar.

Third: "He heard himself going and didn't stop." The walnut chair, the thumb on the grain, the math book she didn't put back "like it had burned her." He's narrating her to a friend the way Vee narrates him to Meg, helpless, past his own guard, and Sheri names it — "Listen to you." That's Randi's chapter 24 stillness confirmed from the other side. He's gone. Whatever this started as, it isn't that.

And fourth, the cold one: "Randi. That's not finished." Flat. Honest — he doesn't lie to Sheri, and I notice he apparently never has, because she knows about the pale sorority chick — but God, the honesty is the indictment. He can say the true sentence to a woman on a bicycle and can't say it to the girl in his bed with her palm over his scar. The truth exists. It's just filed with the wrong people. Vee's whole story is being told in rooms she's not in — Randi debriefs Pace, Pace debriefs Sheri — and she's out here debriefing Randi, closing the circle without knowing it's a circle.

One thing the book is withholding and I felt the withhold: the apology. Randi ordered it for Friday night; this ride is Saturday morning; and the chapter breathes not one word about whether Vee showed up at his door in the black dress and the borrowed shoes. But he's glowing. He's telling chair stories. "She couldn't take your heat" has a lightness in it. I think she came, and I think it went well, and I think the book skipped my scene — the scene I was owed, the make-up scene, the *sure not sorry* scene — and jumped me to the morning after on purpose. I'm a little robbed and a little played and honestly, fine, I'll wait, but that scene had better exist and not just be summarized to Sheri over a handlebar.

Small dread, noted for the record: Sheri watched Vee all Thursday night with an appraiser's eye and filed Randi away with a "huh." This book does not spend characters. She'll be back, and when she comes back she'll say another plain true thing at the worst possible moment, and I both want it and fear it.

Still no wink. Still no engineered yes. Still nobody's paid anything — the glow is now five chapters deep counting the fight-and-repair, and "psychologically merciless" is sitting on the jacket collecting interest. The meter's running, Helen.
