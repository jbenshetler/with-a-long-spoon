**T1 —**

Vee: the book leaves me holding her like something warm I'm not allowed to put down. She ends the volume on the doorstep in that coat with nothing under it, powerful for maybe the first time in her life — and I can't enjoy it cleanly, because I know the scaffolding under that power was built by the two people lying to her. What I feel about Vee right now is protectiveness with nowhere to spend it. She got the best year of her life. It's real to her. Her shame actually loosened, on the page, orgasm by orgasm — "she waited for the shame to crest and close her, the way it always had, and it didn't" — and that growth is *hers*, whatever the source. But I keep coming back to what I wrote at Journal 10: everybody's collecting from her. She's finally hungry out loud and every person feeding her is running a tab she can't see. I feel toward her the way you feel toward a friend who's radiantly happy with someone you know things about. Sick and glad at the same time. Mostly glad, which makes it worse.

Pace: I'm angry at him in a way I can only be angry at someone who's earned it. That's the trick of him. The moleskin, the drawer, the flannel he put on over the shirt he was already wearing, "what happened?" — I trust every single thing his hands do and not one inch of his mouth, and I said so at Journal 40 and I'll stand by it: the real villain of Volume One isn't the conspiracy, it's the withheld sentence. His voice cracked on "I missed you so much" and I felt the deliberate cruelty of the author choosing *that* sentence instead of the other one. So where does the book leave me with him? Wanting to grab him by the collar of one of his two shirts. I don't hate him. I don't forgive him. I want him in a room where he has to talk, and the book knows I want that, and it's charging me for the room.

Randi: Randi is where I don't have a tidy feeling, and I've stopped pretending I want one. The Fairytale chapter broke something in me — a woman who wins a perfect night by putting nothing on the table, alone with a dress for a daughter she doesn't have, and the one scenario she cannot run to the end is Vee's face. I've never felt sorrier for someone I'd never let near my friends, and I wrote that sentence the night I read it and it's still true a day later. She's the character I'd defend at a book club and warn people about at a bar. She is in love with Vee and hasn't told herself, and that makes her *more* dangerous, not less — I trust her less now that she means it more. What I feel, honestly? Grief in advance. That thread ends in fire. I said I'd be there with marshmallows and a broken heart, and a day out, the marshmallow part is fading and the broken-heart part is settling in.

**T2 —**

Yes. Two things, one small and one that's the whole ballgame.

The small one: Cassie's reckoning. She's the only person in the book who wants nothing from Vee, she asks the surgical questions — "is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to?" — she visibly *sees* more than Vee tells her, and the book never lets her act on it. Five debrief chapters and she's used as a mirror the whole way. She's owed a scene where she stops reflecting and moves, and the book kept its hand on her shoulder for fifty chapters. I flagged it at Journal 30 and it never came.

The big one: detonation. Any detonation. I loaded that gun myself in every decade journal — "if fifty chapters end without ANY detonation, not the conspiracy, not the word, I'll feel the hook in my cheek" — and that is exactly what happened. Nothing blew. Not the conspiracy, not the word, not the bench, not Daphne, not Randi's mask (it *slipped*, in chapter 38, on page, and Vee explained it away — the book showed me the pin coming out of the grenade and then put it back). The volume ends on a reconciliation engineered by the person who caused the breakup, wearing shoes she provided, and closes the cover. That's craft, and I admire it, and I also felt the exact thing I predicted: farmed. Not enough to walk — I'm clearly not walking — but the book owes me at least one true sentence spoken out loud between any two of these people, and it paid its whole debt in charged silence. Silence was the currency of Volume One. Fine. It doesn't get to be the currency of Volume Two.

What I did *not* want and didn't miss, for the record: I never once wanted more heat. First book in years where the sex debt is fully paid. Everything owed is emotional, which is its own kind of miracle.

**T3 —**

Sit with me a second, because I want to answer this honestly instead of defensively.

You're right about the facts. Pace deceives her every weekend of the volume. He ends it loved, trusted, forgiven for a fight he let her think was entirely about a word, holding a woman who doesn't know how she was chosen — shopped for, on a quad, in chapter three, and I called it what it was in my own notes: they went shopping for a human being and I was complicit. Nothing lands on him. His worst on-page consequence is a bad night at Chili's and a friend telling him he's being shitty over fries.

Here's how it sits with me: it sits like a stone I *chose* to swallow, and the jacket told me it was a stone before I bought the meal. This is not a book where the deception is a twist I got cheated out of — it's a book where the deception is the reading experience. The whole engine, I wrote at Journal 10, is not being able to tell the ratio of real to game. If Pace got exposed in Volume One, the book I actually read — the one about watching the best year of a girl's life get built by liars and not being able to put either fact down — stops existing. The dramatic irony isn't decoration; it's the load-bearing wall. So no, I don't experience "nothing lands on him" as the author protecting her boy. I experience it as the author refusing to let me off the hook. *I* know. I've known for forty-seven chapters. The discomfort you're describing is the product. I'm ahead of her and I hate how much I like the view — I wrote that at chapter three and the book never once let me stop feeling it.

And — this matters — something *does* land on him, just not exposure. The withheld word costs him the breakup. Sheri guts him in the diner: "then not saying it matters more to you than having her, and from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." He's shown as a man whose control is a wound with furniture, whose dead brother's bench is still in the bedroom he takes her to. The book is not confused about him. It never asks me to think he's safe. Every tender scene sits on top of the bench and the book made sure I never forgot the bench was there.

But your real question is the second half, and here's the honest answer: does it change whether I buy Book Two? It changes *why* I buy it, not whether. I bought it before the pancakes went cold and I'm not embarrassed. But I'm buying it as a creditor now, not a fan. There is a version of Book Two where Pace stays unexposed, where Vee's growth keeps getting attributed to love instead of engineering, where the conspiracy stays a delicious hum under more brunches — and if I get that book, your complaint becomes correct retroactively, about both volumes, and I will feel exactly as farmed as I warned myself I might. The unexposed-Pace of Volume One is a structure. The unexposed-Pace of Volume Two would be a cop-out. The author has one volume of credit with me on this, earned line by line, and Randi's caught breath in chapter 38 tells me she knows the game is destabilizing — she's already losing control of her own monster, on the page, in front of me.

So: it sits uneasy, on purpose, and I think the unease is the best thing about it. Ask me again after Book Two. If nothing's landed on him *then*, I'll be sitting on your side of the table, and I'll be angrier than you are — because I'll have paid twice for it.
