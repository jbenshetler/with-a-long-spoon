T1 — Where it leaves me with each of them

Vee. Tender, and a little sick over it. I finished the book wanting to put a coat on her — not the coat Randi armed her with, a real one — and drive her somewhere no one had staged. What I feel is protectiveness braided with grief, because the last thing she does in the whole volume is walk bare under a coat into a dark porch, unexpected, and call the emptiness of it *enough for tonight*. That word "tonight" is where my heart is. She isn't a fool and the book never let her be one; she's starving, and she found people who feed her exactly enough to keep her hungry. I don't pity her — she's too much a person for pity, she chose and chose and chose — but I ache for the specific loneliness of a girl who has no name for the best thing in her life and therefore can't even tell her friend the truth of it. I love her. That's the plain fact. She's the reason I stayed.

Randi. This is the one that surprised me. I walked in braced to loathe her and I walked out — not forgiving her, but unable to hate her cleanly. Gstaad broke me open on her. The girl who can only be touched behind a locked bathroom door, who built a "face," whose safe perfect life is bought with her father's fraud money, who feels *nothing* beside a flawless man and everything across a nothing-Tuesday table from Vee — and who has arranged her entire existence so she'll never have to be brave. Right now what I feel toward her is a furious tenderness. She's doing to Vee the exact thing being done to Vee, coaching her back into the trap because keeping Vee enchanted is safer than being known — that's cowardice, and it's contemptible, and I also understand it in my bones. She's the most trapped person in the book. I'm angriest at her and saddest for her in the same breath, and I think that's precisely where the book wanted to leave me.

Pace. Coldest for me of the three, and it's a considered coldness, not a reflexive one. I understand him completely — the fifteen-year-old who got the thing that ruined him and calls it *luck*, and so cannot see that he's now the older one shaping a twenty-one-year-old. The Daphne chapter earned him his interiority honestly. But understanding is not the same as warmth, and where the book leaves me with him is a kind of clear-eyed chill: he sleeps easy while she stands in the cold holding a shirt that's gone to nothing. He gives her the drawer, the deeds, the whole of his body — everything but the one word, because the word is where he was destroyed. I can hold that as tragedy and still say: of the three, he's the one nothing has cost yet. He is comfortable. That's what sits coldest.

T2 — What I wanted and didn't get

Two things, and only one of them is a real complaint.

The one that isn't: I didn't get resolution — Vee still doesn't know there was a plan, the word never got spent, Randi never got brave — and I don't feel *owed* that. That's Volume One ending honestly on its own thesis. Withholding it is the book keeping its thumb on the wound, which is the thing I trust it for. If it had paid that off with pancakes and a confession I'd have felt it flinch. So: not a debt. A deliberate deferral, and the right one.

The thing I actually wanted and got shorted on: consequence with *texture* outside the central three. The book gave me two consciences — Cassie and Sheri — and they are the load-bearing proof that the author sees what she's building. But Cassie is thinning by design as the operation isolates Vee, and Sheri arrives late, and the dorm chorus (Kayla, Meg) is cardboard next to them. What I wanted was for the un-enchanted world to have more *mass* — more friction pushing back on the machine, so that Vee's isolation felt like something being taken from a fuller life rather than from an already-narrowing one. And structurally, the scene/debrief metronome in the middle third meant I sometimes got the *knowledge* stakes held perfectly still while the confession-lunch beat repeated. I never wanted the frame to break; I wanted the middle to *advance* the frame more often than it did.

So what the book still owes me isn't a plot payoff. It's proof that the reckoning it deferred is a reckoning it intends — that the deferral was a loaded gun and not just an artful shrug. It's earned my belief that it is. But belief isn't the same as being paid, and Book Two is where the bill comes due.

T3 — Pace ends loved, trusted, unexposed. Nothing lands on him.

Here's the honest answer: you're right about the *events*, and I think you're reading the *book* one layer too shallow — but your instinct is the exact instinct that should be holding the author's feet to the fire, so I don't want to talk you out of it. I want to split it.

You're right that plot-wise, nothing lands on him. He is loved, trusted, and unexposed at the final page, and Vee comes back to him bare under a coat. If that were the whole ledger, I'd be out — that's the grooming-fantasy structure I've been burned by, the one where the narrative quietly agrees the man won.

But look at what the book *does* to him even while the plot lets him win. Sheri sits across from him and says, flat and unanswerable, that not-saying-it matters more to him than having her, and *from this seat that feels pretty shitty* — and he has no charm that works on her. The Daphne chapter diagnoses him as a man reenacting his own abuse and too damaged to see it. Chapter 46 shows us he has hollowed out his own partner. The book strips him naked in front of *me* on page after page. So it isn't true that nothing lands on him — plenty lands on him, in the register the book actually operates in, which is the register of exposure-to-the-reader. What's true is that nothing lands on him *where Vee can see it.* He pays in the reader's ledger and not yet in hers.

And that gap is either the book's whole point or its fatal cheat, and *which one it is is not decidable from Volume One.* That's the uncomfortable thing I have to be honest with you about. The jacket — "Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over. That was the problem" — is a dare. If Book Two lets that line stand as wisdom, if mutual feeling gets used to launder the fact that two adults ran an operation on a kid and never paid the truth, then you were right all along and I was the mark, exactly as designed. If Book Two makes the unpaid word and the unexposed plan detonate — if consequence finally crosses from my ledger into Vee's — then the thing that "sat wrong" with you was the tension the whole design was loading.

So how it sits with me: uneasily, on purpose, and I think that unease is the correct state to end Volume One in rather than a flaw in it. A book that made me *comfortable* with Pace unexposed would be the book I quit. This one made me comfortable with *nothing* — it just deferred the bill instead of forgiving it.

Does it change whether I buy Book Two? No — it's the reason I buy Book Two. I'm not buying it to see the couple reunited; I'm buying it to find out whether this author is the kind who collects the debt or the kind who pockets it. Everything in fifty chapters — Gstaad especially — tells me she knows the debt is a debt. If I'm wrong about that, Book Two is where I'll find out, and I'll leave loud. But I'd bet on her. Warily. Which, honestly, is the only way this book ever let me feel anything.
