T1 — Where the book leaves me with the three of them, and how I *feel*, right now.

Vee: protective, and a little heartbroken in advance. I don't pity her — that's important, because the book never let me pity her. She's not a doormat; she's a woman who took her own kiss on the toes of her shoes, who knocked on the cab glass and took her shirt off because *she* wanted to, who said "I wanted it. And I took it. For once." I love her for that "for once." So what I feel toward her isn't pity, it's something closer to dread on behalf of someone I like. She's the only one in the whole book operating in good faith, and she's the only one who doesn't know there's a game. I keep wanting to reach into the pages and turn her around. And underneath that there's a tenderness I didn't expect — the shoe money, the emergency credit card with nothing behind it, the ladle closing the soup so there's no hole where the truth was. She's learning to keep her realest thing to herself, and that's the saddest thing about her, and it makes me hold her closer.

Pace: this is the one that's changed on me, and I want to be honest about how. Early on I'd have said I was falling for him the way she is — the man who asks, who waits, who hands her back the pencil so the win is hers. I still think he's the most careful lover I've ever read in this genre, and I'm not going to pretend chapter 17 or 31 didn't work on me, because they did. But after the Daphne chapter I can't feel warmth toward him without it curdling. Understanding him did not soften him for me — it made him scarier. A man who was taken like that at fifteen and files it under *luck*, who carries the memory like a full glass so it won't spill, and who is now doing the exact same tender thing to Vee and genuinely cannot see it as harm — that's not a man I feel safe handing my affection to. So where I land with Pace is: fascinated, moved, and cold. I don't trust the tenderness precisely because I believe it's real. That's the worst version.

Randi: fear, and — this surprised me — a thread of pity I didn't have at the halfway mark. Chapter 19 turned her from the glamorous friend into the operator, and I've been afraid of her since, correctly. The kneeling, the thumb on the toenail, the door shut at the exact second Vee's about to hand over the last true thing — that's not friendship, it's craft, and it's the coldest cruelty in the book because it wears intimacy's face. But then "she got past me before I could help it," and Randi going still on his chest when he describes Vee lighting up a room — the jealousy leaking out under the plan. She wants Vee for herself, and I don't think she's told Pace that, and I don't think she's told *herself* that cleanly either. So I fear her and I've started to grieve for her a little, because she built this and it's eating her, and she of all people should have known it would.

T2 — What I wanted and didn't get.

One thing, and I named it in my own margins over and over, so I'll say it plainly: the volume never let Vee and Pace just *be in a room alone* as often as I was starving for. Look at my own gates — I kept clocking the gap. So much of the middle is Randi and Pace talking *about* the seduction, brunch after brunch of confession-extraction, debrief after debrief. Chapters 21 through 24, I was getting full on it. I understand *why* the architecture works that way — the book is showing me the machine — but as a reader who measures her patience in how long since the two of them were last alone, I felt every one of those gaps in my body before I could count them. So yes: it owes me more of *them*, less of the war room.

And the bigger thing it owes me — the thing my whole verdict is hostage to — is that Vee never gets to *know*. The entire volume is seduction and not one ounce of reckoning. She never stands in the room and sees the room. I don't need it resolved in Book One; a first volume is allowed to end mid-breath, and that last kitchen chapter earned its cliff. But the book has now taken a debt out in my name. It made me complicit — it put me ahead of her on purpose, let me fall for him the way she does knowing what she doesn't — and a book that does that to a reader owes her the moment the target becomes a person with knowledge. It hasn't paid. It's promised. I'm holding the IOU.

T3 — Pace deceives her the whole volume and ends loved, trusted, unexposed. Nothing lands on him. How does that sit, and does it change whether I buy Book Two?

Here's the thing — it sits *badly*, and I think it's supposed to, and those are two different facts that I have to hold apart, because your objection collapses them and I don't think they collapse.

If this were the *end* — if the book were a standalone and the credits rolled with him beloved and untouched and me expected to sigh happily — I'd throw it across the room, and I'd tell you never to read it. That's the exact rot I've walked away from a hundred times: the man's disregard dressed up as devotion and the book winking at me to find it romantic. A man ending "unexposed" as a *reward* is the genre's oldest lie.

But it's Volume One of a set, and the tell — the thing that keeps me in the chair — is that the book never once winks. Go back through my own record: it keeps showing me the *cost*. Randi dissociating, the thumbnail into the nail bed, Cassie's flat finished "goodbye" while everyone else performs warmth, Sheri on the road naming the shape of it from outside the spell, the boy Pace was at fifteen. The narration is not on his side. It's on *hers*, quietly, the whole time — it just refuses to rescue her early. So "nothing lands on him yet" reads to me not as absolution but as a loaded gun the book has very deliberately not fired. The horror is *structural* — every yes is genuinely hers, what's stolen is her knowledge of the room she's saying yes in — and you can't detonate a structural crime in the same volume you spend building it. If Vee found out at chapter 28 it'd be a lesser, cheaper book.

So does it change whether I buy Book Two? No — I'm still buying it, and I'd have paid before I finished the sentence. But your problem *is* my problem; I just filed it as a condition instead of a verdict. My yes has a knife at its throat, and I wrote it that way: it stays yes *only if the reckoning comes.* If Book Two lets him stay unexposed, or worse, uses "but it turned into real love" to launder two people building a person's whole year as a secret bet — then you're right and I'm wrong and the book cheated, and I'll say so loudly. The unexposed ending isn't what I'm buying Book Two *for*. It's the exact thing I'm buying Book Two to watch *break*. Ask me again when I've read it — that's the only honest answer I've got, because this volume made the question the whole point and then closed the cover before answering it.
