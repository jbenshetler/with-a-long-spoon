# Capture panel — romance-graduate · volume-interview

*model: claude-opus-4-8 · persona: romance-graduate · arm: volume-interview · chapters: the-bench, standards, the-pointing-game, see-you-later · protocol: capture-panel-v1 · prompt-sha: d45a9a6f526a · run: 2026-09-07*

T1 — Where the book leaves me with each of them, right now.

**Vee.** Tender. Protective, actually, more than I expected. I came in braced to be annoyed by another wide-eyed ingénue and instead she turned out to have rooms she guards even from me — the uncropped induction photo she keeps for herself, the fraud she sees and swallows to shield Randi. That's what did it. I don't feel over her the way I feel over most heroines by chapter 50. I feel *for* her, which is different and rarer. But — and I have to be honest — there's an ache under the fondness that's close to dread. She's smarter than her situation and losing anyway, and she's shrinking in ways she didn't ask for (that weight she never chose to lose gutted me). Right now she reads to me like someone standing in a warm doorway with her coat open, happy, and I want to grab her wrist. So: love, and fear, braided. Same wire as the book's own best trick.

**Pace.** Cold. That's the plain answer, and it surprised me, because for the first ten chapters I was halfway swooning. Somewhere around the Daphne reveal the swoon curdled and never came back. What I feel now isn't hatred — it's the specific chill of watching a gentle man be a monster in slow motion and never once catch himself doing it. "The best two years I had," filed under *luck*. The unshared photograph as a promise that's actually a leash. The one word he will not spend even when she builds him the most beautiful sentence to earn it. I don't find him romantic anymore; I find him *legible*, and what I read is a closed system. The thing I respect is that the book feels exactly the same way I do — it's in love with his sentences and it never lets him off the hook. I'm not either.

**Randi.** She's the one I can't put down even now that the book's over. She's where I actually live. I pity her, I'm furious at her, and I'd follow her anywhere — she is drowning and steering at the same time, and the Gstaad chapter earned every bit of that. A villain who has the perfect night with the perfect man and feels nothing and understands precisely *why* is not a villain I can dismiss, and the book knows it. She owes Vee a truth she keeps paying in seduction instead. Right now I feel toward Randi the way you feel toward the friend who's about to blow up her own life and yours — helpless, tender, a little sick. Of the three, she's the one whose next move I genuinely cannot call, and that's why she's got me.

T2 — What I wanted and didn't get; what it still owes me.

Two things, one fair and one a real grievance.

The fair one: **the gun didn't go off.** Vee still doesn't know there was a plan. The invitation is still uncollected. The unshared photo and the fraud are both live wires nobody's touched. I don't feel *cheated* by that, because the jacket told me this was Volume One and the whole architecture is a held breath — but let's be clear, the book absolutely owes me the discharge. It's structured a debt and hasn't paid it, and I'm holding the marker. That's not a flaw; it's a bill.

The grievance, which is real: **Cassie.** The book spends the whole volume deliberately, visibly edging her out of the frame — every debrief shorter, every "Goodbye, Vee" colder — and it's clearly *on purpose*, which is exactly why I resent it. She's the only clean person in the entire book and she got sacrificed to the machine so Randi could take her chair. I wanted the book to *reckon* with what it was doing to her, not just do it and trust me to notice. It gave me the ache of her disappearance without ever giving her the dignity of a scene that was about her. That, I feel genuinely owed.

And the smaller thing, the structural one: I wanted the book to break its own rhythm and it mostly wouldn't. Scene, then-she-tells-a-friend, scene, then-brunch — I could have lost a third of the debriefs and been a happier reader. It never fully trusted me to have *watched* the thing; it kept having Vee re-narrate it. That's not a debt exactly, it's a habit I wanted it to kick and it didn't.

T3 — Pace ends loved, trusted, unexposed. Nothing lands on him. How does that sit, and does it change whether I buy Book Two?

Here's the thing — you're right on the facts and wrong about what to do with them, and I want to walk you through why, because I had the identical flinch and then it flipped on me.

Yes: he deceives her the whole volume and ends the book adored, trusted, and clean. Nothing lands. But notice *who* the deception even belongs to. The plan is Randi's and Pace's jointly, and the person the book is actually indicting is Pace — through Daphne, through "I don't go back," through the photograph he'll take but never share, through the one word he withholds at cost because withholding it is worth more to him than she is. The book has already convicted him. It just hasn't *sentenced* him. And that's a completely different thing.

What would make your objection fatal is if the book didn't *know*. If the prose thought he was a hero, if it were asking me to root for the gentle mathematician and skip past the fifteen-year-old — then yes, him ending untouched would be the book endorsing him, and I'd throw it. But the book is merciless about him precisely where it looks tenderest. Sheri exists *only* to sit across from him and say, to his face, that not-saying-it matters more to him than having her, and from that seat it feels pretty shitty. The author built a character whose entire function is to name his defect out loud. You don't do that if you're letting him off. You do that if you're loading the charge and choosing not to fire it *yet*.

So "nothing lands on him" isn't the book absolving him. It's the book being the first half of one story. This is *Supping With the Devil, Volume One* — the whole title is a promise that the bill comes due, that you don't dine with him for free. An unexposed liar at the midpoint isn't a failure of justice; it's a loaded gun on the mantel. If he'd been caught and reckoned with by chapter 50, there'd be no reason to open Book Two.

Does it change whether I buy Book Two? It's the *opposite* — it's the single biggest reason I buy Book Two, and I bought it before I finished the last page. The unexposed deception is the entire engine. What I'd tell you to actually watch for is this, because it's the real test: the book has *earned* the right to leave him standing at the midpoint. It will have *betrayed* me if he's still standing at the end of the story — if the volumes go by and the photo never detonates, the fraud never crosses the plan, Vee never learns there was a plan, and the man just… keeps getting loved. That's the promise I'm holding it to. Not "punish him by page 50." "Don't tell me this was a con and then flinch from the cost of it."

Right now I trust it not to flinch, because a book that writes the Gstaad chapter — that hands its villain a full wrecked self-aware interior instead of a mustache — is not a book that's going to protect Pace out of being in love with him. It's in love with him the way I was for ten chapters: clear-eyed enough to know exactly what he is.

So: buy it. But buy it the way I did — as someone holding a marker, not as someone who's been satisfied. If you finish the whole thing and he's still untouched, come find me and I'll eat it. I don't think you'll have to.
