Oh, this is the chapter I didn't know I was owed. After forty-seven chapters of watching Pace only from the outside — the drawer, the dress, the doctrine executed soft — I finally get to sit inside his skull, and the first thing he does is make "the decision before the door had swung shut": *I would not bring her down today.* He walked in carrying something and chose it "could keep." "Long practice at deciding a thing could keep." So even here, alone, off-duty, the man is managing — managing *himself*, deciding what a person gets and doesn't get. That's the tell. The withholding isn't a wall Vee happens to run into; it's a *practice*, a skill he's proud of the way he's proud of a clean proof.

And here's what I've been waiting the whole book to find out: does the author see Pace, or does the author *love* Pace the way Pace wants to be loved? This chapter is the test, and it's Sheri who runs it, and — thank god — Sheri is holding the knife I've been holding. "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her." "From this seat, that feels pretty shitty." She takes his beautiful tower of bricks — *I'm there every weekend, I said the most important thing, I don't lie* — and names it exactly: "that's a hell of a lot of reasons for a man who's already got the girl. She didn't ask you for reasons." YES. That's the read. He answers the question next to the one he was asked, every time, because he *has* answers to that one. She clocks the dodge. The author clocks the dodge. Sheri is the true sentence at the table I've been praying for since the pie showed up, and she got to say it — just not at Thanksgiving, and not to Vee, but to *him*, which is almost better because it means the book knows.

But — and this is the vigilant part — watch what the chapter also does. It's warm. It's *so* warm. Sheri gives him hell and steals his fries and the fog keeps the cold out and he laughs his first full laugh, and the whole thing is engineered to make me *like* him again right as it's indicting him. "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice" — he hands Sheri the one genuinely kind, un-transactional thing in the chapter, and I felt it, and then I got suspicious of feeling it. Because the diner is doing to me exactly what the porch does to Vee: build the warmth, land the tenderness, and let the withholding sit inside it looking like integrity. "I'm not them." "Words are cheap." He's got a whole *philosophy* of why the one thing she needs is the one thing that would be beneath him to give. And the book lets Sheri say that's shitty. That's the difference between this book and the ones I leave. It's not asking me to admire the withholding. It's showing me the man admiring it and putting someone across the amber table who calls it what it is.

Two things chilled me. One: "the philosophy of your food. Lord almighty. Get over yourself." Sheri deflates exactly the thing Vee reveres — *she gets what it's for, the philosophy of it* — and I realized Vee's deepest intimacy with him, the seeing, the getting-what-it's-for, is the thing his own friend finds pompous. Vee's specialness to him is real AND it's the same grandiosity Sheri's laughing at. Both true at once. Two: "She find out about the brunette, then?" So Sheri knows about Randi as a *thing* — "the brunette," a known quantity in Pace's life, someone he brought dancing — and Sheri does NOT know it's a plot, a game, a purchased girl. Sheri thinks it's a harem. The chapter's whole irony is that Sheri is close enough to give him hell about two women and has no idea the two women are a *scheme he's running with one of them on the other.* The book just showed me the limit of even the honest witness: Sheri sees the philanderer, not the architect. Nobody at any table can see the actual shape.

And the thing I can't put down: he *misses her.* "I want Vee. I miss her." It reads true. That's the merciless part the jacket promised — "as true for the two who started it." He's not faking the missing. He just won't pay the one coin, and he'd rather lose her than spend it, and Sheri names that trade to his face and he looks down. A man who genuinely wants her, genuinely can't, and knows — because Sheri told him — that the can't is a choice. That's not naive writing. That's a book that knows exactly what its man is.
