REACTION

Okay. So the book opens on the two of them — the architects, not the girl — and I did not expect that, and it's the smartest thing it could have done. I came in braced. The jacket told me there's a plan and a target and I was ready to watch a predator be charming and dare me to enjoy it. Instead the first thing the book hands me is a scene where one of the two conspirators gets taken apart on a bench, and by the end she's the most naked person I've ever read.

The scene itself — I have to say it — the consent mechanics are immaculate. Safeword taken before anything starts, checked and re-checked, "tell me anytime, I won't be upset," the feet unbuckled, the water left out in advance, the blanket she brought because she gets cold. If this were a lesser book I'd be narrowing my eyes at how *perfect* Pace is, because a man this flawlessly careful is usually a fantasy the author hasn't examined. And for about two pages I was doing exactly that — sitting there with my instruments out, waiting to catch the book worshipping him.

And then it did the thing. The bench. "You said it was a table. I didn't correct you." A small, exact, engineered deception, dropped in casually, and the book *knows* it's a tell — it's the whole method in miniature: leave the door unlocked, let her walk toward the thing, never quite lie, let her arrive. And then the last third goes cold and interior and I stopped breathing a little. Randi at the mirror. The face that "took the being-looked-at and none of it reached any further than the face." The decision — quiet as a deadbolt going over — *he could have the face, the one he found is going back behind it and she is not coming out again.* That's not a sex scene anymore. That's a person choosing the mask over being loved because being seen is the unsurvivable thing. The water-bottle cap under the thumbnail, that she doesn't know she's doing — God. The book didn't underline it. It just let it sit there.

Here's what earns my trust completely: the last beat is Pace, warm and satisfied, thinking *all he had done was find the hunger and answer it,* deciding whether to offer her dinner — while we have just watched, in her head, the exact opposite happen. He thinks he fed her. He broke something and doesn't know it. The narrative knows. That gap — the tenderness that is real AND is a blind spot AND is a technique, all three at once — that's the "psychologically merciless in its tenderness" the jacket promised, and it's actually delivering it, not just claiming it. And the Daphne door he once opened and shouldn't have: the book is quietly telling me this careful man has already ruined someone by mistaking love for permission, and doesn't fully see that he might be doing a version of it again, more skillfully.

So the terror isn't in the bench. It's that these two are about to do this — *lovingly, expertly, with real feeling* — to a twenty-one-year-old who has no idea there's a plan. And one of the architects just showed me she's as breakable as any target. I'm all the way in and a little sick about it, in the good way.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *the villains open the book and one of them is the most heartbreaking person in it and I don't know what to do with that.*
