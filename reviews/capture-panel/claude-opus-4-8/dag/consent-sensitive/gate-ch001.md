Okay, first: the switch. I spent the whole first section assuming this was Vee — the jacket handed me Vee, twenty-one, starving, and here's a girl being fed thin apple slices by a courtly man who asks before he takes. The book opened not on the mark but on one of the two people who supposedly authored the mark's whole year — and it opened on her getting authored right back, without her consent to the meaning of it, if not the acts.

That's a genuinely smart move and it changed the temperature of everything I'd just read. Because the consent scaffolding in the scene is *immaculate* — safeword established, "tell me again if you forget, I won't be upset," the check-ins on her hands and shoulders and back, the water left out in advance, the blanket she brought weeks ago, the aftercare where he becomes "the thing she sat against." It shows me, in the same breath, the cost. The flinch at "beautiful." The water-bottle cap pressed under her thumbnail into the nail bed where it hurts, and "she did not seem to know she was doing it" — twice the book catches her self-harming in these tiny invisible ways and tells me *she doesn't know*, which means the author knows. The lock turning over on the bathroom door "which was not a thing she had ever done before." The dissociation architecture — the Face as a thing that is not her, that receives being-wanted and passes it inward in amounts she decides, and tonight there was no deciding. That's not a book naive to what it's depicting. That's a book building a whole clinical, precise portrait of a woman whose entire selfhood is a managed perimeter, and showing me the perimeter breached, and showing me her decision — quiet as a deadbolt going over — to wall it back up *forever*. "He could have the face. The one he had found was going back behind it, and she was not coming out again." God.

So who's the predator here. That's the delicious, unsettled thing. Pace is scrupulous — Daphne taught him about the door, he respects the lock, he doesn't interpret her for her. By every consent metric he's doing it right. And yet the dramatic irony is brutal: he ends the night "quietly happy," thinking he *found her hunger and answered it,* thinking he'll answer it again as often as she brings it — while she's next door deciding he's the one thing she can never allow again. He read her body perfectly and her interior not at all. He thinks tenderness landed; it landed like an injury. The book is dead level about this gap and doesn't rescue either of them from it. That's the "psychologically merciless in its tenderness" line delivered, not promised.

What I'm suspicious of / watching: the frame tells me these two engineered a *game* about Vee. So Randi is not a victim in the plot sense — she's a co-architect. And this chapter is quietly informing me that the architect is herself already hollowed, already dissociating through scenes, already someone who "portions herself." Which reframes the whole coming seduction of Vee: is Randi recruiting Vee into the thing that's eating Randi? Is Pace going to do to Vee what he just did to Randi — find the real one under the face — and will that be salvation or the same quiet catastrophe? The jacket says "as true for the two who started it as for the girl who never knew" — this chapter is the down payment on that. It got real for Randi tonight, before Vee's even on the page.

The one thing keeping my guard up: I need the book to keep *knowing.* Right now it's holding the awareness beautifully — the prose sees Randi's damage even as Pace doesn't. If it ever tips into wanting me to just swoon at Pace's careful-dom competence and forget the thumbnail, I'm gone. But it hasn't. It closed on the bench, damp in two places, waiting — "full of what it was" — which is the book refusing to let the tenderness launder the scene. That's the right note. That's a book I trust enough to keep reading, warily.
