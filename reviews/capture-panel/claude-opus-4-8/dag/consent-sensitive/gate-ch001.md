REACTION

Okay, so I came in braced. The jacket told me who to watch: Randi and Pace, the two who "planned the best year of her life," Vee the mark. So I sat down expecting to meet the machine from the inside and I spent the whole first half thinking *this is the grooming, this is the engineering, show me your hand.* The apple sliced thin because she won't eat thick. The lights he keeps up because there's a way he wants to look at her she's never been looked at in before. The furniture whose price tag she can't read. I was cataloguing all of it as the toolkit — this man manages a woman down to the cut-side-up of an apple slice, and the prose *knows* he does, it's naming every instrument.

And then the last section put the knife in and I actually sat back. It's *Randi.* The "she" is Randi. The woman on the bench, red and begging in a voice that came from under the face, is one of the two architects — and the book just spent a whole chapter taking the schemer apart. That's the reversal the jacket half-promised ("as true for the two who started it as for the girl who never knew there was one") and I didn't expect it delivered in chapter one, from *his* POV, before I've even met Vee.

Here's why I'm still here and not gone: the awareness is *everywhere.* This could so easily have been a book that wanted me to applaud Pace — the courtly dom, the safeword, the water bottle left in advance, "tell me anytime, I won't be upset." All the consent scaffolding is real and I clocked it and it's genuinely well done. But the book does not let his tenderness off the hook. It shows me Pace at the end, warm and satisfied, thinking he *found her hunger and answered it,* deciding whether to offer dinner — and it shows me Randi at the sink deciding, quiet as a deadbolt, that the one he found is going back behind the face *not ever* coming out again. The gap between what he thinks he did and what it did to her is the entire point, and the author is standing in that gap holding a flashlight. That's the thing I read for. That's the book knowing.

And the self-harm tells — the water bottle cap pressed under her thumbnail, the earlier finger closing on nothing — dropped in without comment, without him seeing. The lock turning over that she's never turned before. The flinch at "you're beautiful." This woman has interiority for days and it's *bleak* interiority; the book isn't eroticizing her unraveling as triumph, it's showing me a person who experiences being truly seen as a catastrophe and is going to reweaponize herself because of it. That's not naive. That's merciless, exactly as advertised.

What I'm suspicious of — in the good, leaning-forward way: the Daphne line. "He had gone in where he had not been asked, and it had cost him more than he had known." So Pace has a history of not respecting a door and has *learned* — and now he respects Randi's lock. Except the book is quietly showing me that his respecting the door isn't safety either; it's just that the thing she's fleeing *is his seeing her,* and there's no door that keeps that out. I don't trust him. I don't think I'm supposed to. And I love that Randi — who I was told is a predator — is the one I'm suddenly aching over. The hunter walked into her own party.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *it's the SCHEMER on the bench crying. chapter ONE. and the book knows the man thinks he did something beautiful and it will not let him be right.*

The heat worked on me, for the record — the slowness, the apple game, the bench reveal done as a reading-of-purpose. But the capture wasn't the heat. It was the last three pages doing surgery.
