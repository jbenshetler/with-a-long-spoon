REACTION

Oh, they got me. They got me with the *name*. The whole chapter I'm reading this as the seduction the jacket promised — courtly exact man, girl choosing every step, consent so clean it's practically a checklist — and I'm braced, because "asks before he takes and leaves her room to choose" is exactly the sentence that predator-apologist books hide behind. And then "There you are, Randi." Not Vee. This is the *huntress*. This is the woman the jacket told me steers the girl into the trap, laughing, as if by accident. Chapter One is the co-conspirator getting split open on a bench she didn't see coming, and deciding, in a locked bathroom, to seal herself shut forever. That's a merciless place to start a book, and it told me more about what I'm actually holding than the whole blurb did.

Here's why I trust it, at least tonight: the book is not asking me to cheer. It would have been so easy. Pace is the fantasy — the too-warm house, the pre-sliced honeycrisp cut thin because he *noticed* how she eats, the safe word offered twice, the aftercare blanket she left there weeks ago, the water he knew to leave out. And the narration lets him have his little male glow at the end — "all he had done was find it and answer it," "a remarkably good evening" — which is *exactly* the register that makes me want to throw a book across the room, the man who thinks seeing a woman's dissociation is the same as loving her. That was the closest I came to being suspicious the author didn't know what she'd built. But she does. Because she gives Randi the last word, and Randi's last word is a full-throated *no*. The flinch at "you're beautiful." The bottle cap pressed under her thumbnail where it hurts, and *she does not seem to know she is doing it* — that's the book showing me damage the character can't see while the prose sees it perfectly. That's the whole distinction I read by, laid out in one gesture.

And the thing that actually unsettled me most wasn't the spanking — the spanking was hot and scrupulous. It was the interiority in that bathroom. "Nothing happened to her. That was what the face was for." A woman who has weaponized being looked at, who portions herself down to thin apple slices, who got *seen* for one evening and experienced it not as intimacy but as a breach to be walled off — "he could have the face." That's psychologically exact and it's genuinely sad and it complicates every warm thing in the chapter. She is the manipulator AND she is a person with a wound the size of a locked deadbolt. The book knows both at once.

What I'm circling warily: Pace and the Daphne backstory. He "learned" not to open a door he could hear through — good, he respected the lock. But a man this precise, this practiced at finding the real self behind a woman's performance, who is (per the jacket) about to co-author a *plan* around an unsuspecting twenty-one-year-old? That's a chilling skill set to have established this well in chapter one. He found Randi. He'll find Vee. And Randi just decided nobody gets to find her again — so what does she do with a girl who might let herself be found? I want to text a friend: *the villain is the one who got hurt first and I don't know whose side I'm on and I think that's the point.*
