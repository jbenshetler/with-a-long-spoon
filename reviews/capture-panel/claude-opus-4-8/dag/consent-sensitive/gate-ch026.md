REACTION

Okay so first — the dance itself got me, genuinely, before any of the knives came out. The three-step lesson on the cold concrete pad is the most disarming thing this book has done in a while, and I clocked that, because disarming is exactly when I watch hardest. "Give me something to push back against. Otherwise you cannot feel my lead." "The space you're keeping. Close it — come all the way in — and my feet can't land anywhere but past yours." Read that again with the whole book behind it. He is teaching her, in physical form, the entire thesis of the con: stop bracing, stop guessing, give me your weight, and I will make you feel like you're the one moving. The lead arrives *before her feet know what to do*. That's the whole seduction rendered as a folk dance, and the chapter is called "Lesson," and I don't think that's an accident. The book knows what it's staging. It handed me "how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd been building out of wanting him, wish laid over man so neatly she couldn't find the join" — that's MY audit, put in her mouth, for one clean second — and then let the floor take the question. That she can't hold the question is the tragedy; that the book can articulate it is the reason I'm still here.

And then the jealousy. This is the part I need to be careful with, because it's the closest the book has come to asking me to boo the heroine and cheer the man. Vee lashes out, unfairly, at a good-on-the-surface thing, and gets the full corrective: the bartender's exoneration, "Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey," and then the 3 a.m. aria of self-flagellation — "She'd punished him for being the man she wanted him to be. She'd done it to a good man." If I read that straight, it's a naive book teaching a jealous girl her lesson and rehabilitating Pace's halo. But I'm not going to read it straight, because one chapter ago the book itself told me he "handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck" — Daphne, nineteen to his fifteen, two years, the assigned tutor, the teacher/student thing that this very chapter echoes back at me through the blonde's mouth: "Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to." The book put that echo there on purpose. So when Vee decides she was simply wrong and he's simply good, I get to hold what she can't: she's right that he didn't cheat and dead wrong about the size of the danger, and the chapter's whole function is to convert a real flare of self-protection — the first uncoached thing she's done in ages — into shame, and route her, at a dead run, back to the handler. "Go to Randi's. She'd been wrong. She was going to fix it." That's the machine absorbing a malfunction and coming out the other side stronger. Chilling. And I think it's chilling on purpose.

The misogyny vocabulary roaring back the instant she's threatened — "spread her legs like a slut," "floozies," watching the bare-midriff girls with her mother's voice in her head — that's not the author talking, that's the wound talking, and the book has sourced that exact vocabulary as the injury since page one. It's consistent. The shame was never gone; it was soothed. Threaten her and it's right there.

What I'm sitting with: nobody engineered the jealousy, and that's almost worse. The blonde was probably just a real jealous girl, not a plant — but the effect served the plan perfectly regardless, and Vee's self-correction serves it more. The apparatus doesn't even need to run this one. Her own shame does the work. That's the most efficient cruelty in the book so far.

The thing I'd text a friend: "the love interest taught her to dance by literally telling her to stop resisting his lead and it was the hottest and most sinister scene yet and I hate that both are true."

The one place I exhaled: he left the beer untouched and walked out. He didn't manage her tantrum, didn't smooth it, didn't deploy the consent-voice to win. He just — went. That's either the one genuinely un-strategized moment he's had, or it's the deepest move of all, because it's the one that makes her chase. I can't tell yet. That I can't tell is the book still being in good hands.
