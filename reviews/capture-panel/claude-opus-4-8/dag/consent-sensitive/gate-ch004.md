Okay, so this is the chapter I've been waiting for and dreading in equal measure, and I have to hand it to Helen Rivers — she knew exactly what she was doing switching the camera here.

Because here's the thing. I spent three chapters inside the machine. I watched Pace build a bench. I watched Randi get taken apart on it and then reassemble her own face in the mirror and decide, quietly, *never.* I watched the two of them stroll a quad *shopping for a girl* like she was a dish they hadn't ordered, and I watched Randi's whole trained instrument aim itself at Vee's shoulder and make the collision look like Vee walked into *her.* "Do you think she'll like the bench?" That line has been sitting in my stomach for a chapter.

And now I'm inside Vee. And Vee is walking back to her dorm *not feeling her feet.* Vee thinks she got let borrow an hour inside a brighter life. Vee is running *we should sit together* both ways to wear a groove in it. Vee is a kid — a good, sharp, lonely, class-reading kid who finds her place in every room by taking its measure, which is a survival skill, which means somebody taught her early that rooms were dangerous. And I know, because I was in the other room, that the collision was engineered, that "oh my god we should sit together" was reconnaissance, that Randi turning the wattage on Cassie and swinging it back to Vee is a manipulation pattern I could diagram. Dramatic irony as a weapon. The blurb *told* me — "the reader, ahead of her from the start" — and I hated that it was right and I kept reading anyway.

Here's what's keeping me in the chair, though, and it's the whole ballgame for me: **the book knows.** It is not asking me to swoon at Randi's charm. Vee's interiority is *dense* and it is real — the granola bar, the right pen landing like earned praise, the transcript-blank hour where she kept the feeling and lost every word. "The leaning-in was the whole content." God. That's not a seduced narrator; that's the book showing me precisely the mechanism by which a lonely person gets hollowed out and calls it the best hour in months. The Chi Latte joke she'd stood outside of — being *let in under the sign* — that's ache, rendered with full awareness of how cheaply that ache can be bought and how much it costs the person feeling it.

And then Cassie. **Cassie is the instrument I check by, and Cassie read the room.** "Goodbye, Vee." The whole word. The word for a longer parting than a Wednesday. She declines the bit, she declines the coffee, she puts her name down flat, and she says *goodbye* like she's watching someone get walked off by something. The look that passed over her face that Vee "would not have been able to name and did not try to." That's the author planting a witness who sees exactly what I see. That tells me Rivers is not naive to this. There's a sane person in the frame, and the tragedy is that Vee smooths the wrongness over — *it's coffee, weirdo* — because reading it straight would cost her the brighter life.

What I noticed and clocked with a chill: the *floozies* beat. That's the cleanest thing in the chapter. Vee looks at the two half-dressed girls, her mother's voice arrives whole — *floozies* — and then *underneath* the word, the longer beat of looking she doesn't look at, the sprinter's stomach, the low waistband, *she could wear that.* And the cardigan buttoned over the better shirt "on purpose." The book is showing me the exact shape of the appetite the whole scheme is built to exploit — a want walled off in a mother's voice — and it's showing it as *Vee's,* interior, hers, not as a leer. That's the tell that this is dark-content-the-book-knows-about. The predators want her hunger; the chapter shows me the hunger is real and was caged long before they got there.

What I'm suspicious of — and I mean suspicious in the good, alert way, not the bail way: I need Cassie to *stay* a live wire. If she becomes a one-note disapprover who gets narratively dismissed so we can enjoy the seduction, the book loses me, because that's the move a naive version would make. Right now she's the conscience of the thing. And I need Vee's interiority to keep this much room. The danger with this setup isn't the dark material — it's chapters of warmth with the awareness switched off. This chapter did *not* switch it off. It's practically flashing a hazard light on every warm beat.
