WHO'S WHO

Vee (Vivienne Thorne) — the redhead on the cover, twenty-one, econ major, lonely and starving in the way that has nothing to do with food and everything to do with it. Sharp — she reads rooms, she reads people, she has the exact instrument (the gap between what people say they're doing and what they're actually doing) that should catch what's being done to her, and she trains it on Pace and concludes he's genuine. The one place she's safe is underwater. Learned young that her body was a problem. This is the girl who never knew there was a game.

Pace — grad-student mathematician, finishing a PhD, lives alone off six-fourteen past where the road goes to gravel. Courtly, exact, patient, "may I" and "may I choose" and the safeword. Not a face-type, a technique. Built the bench. Has a history — Daphne, the door he didn't respect when he was young, "went in where he wasn't asked." He runs the same play on Vee he ran on Randi: find the caged appetite, answer it, make the engineering feel like being seen. He is currently managing both women, and each thinks she's the one with him.

Randi — the "glamorous friend," co-architect with Pace of the whole scheme. But she's the saddest person in the book: eating-disordered, dissociative (the bottle cap under the thumbnail), a whole self built out of "the face." Got taken apart on the bench in ch1 and decided in the mirror to go back behind the face forever. She pointed Vee out. She's the one running the honeypot friendship — the "we should sit together," the arm touches, "love you, girl." And she's jealous — she goes level, not soft, when Pace talks about Vee. She's also being handled from above (by Pace, who withholds Vee's confession from her; by Brooke, in the house).

Cassie — Vee's real friend and the moral instrument of the whole book. Deadpan, boxy, at home in her own body, no agenda. Sees everything: "Goodbye, Vee" like a finished word, "that's not a boy, that's a man," "how much did he give you to drink," "off six-fourteen — that's the whole address," "is he rich." Every alarm I'd trip, she trips. She's grieving in advance and has no leverage. As long as she's sighted, I trust the book.

Brooke — chapter president, runs the sorority house, warm-voiced, handled Randi in a four-minute smiling interrogation about Pace. Proof Randi's a predator who's also prey in her own food chain.

WHERE THINGS STAND

Ch1–3 are the machine's origin, shown before the mark: Randi and Pace are lovers/partners; on a quad they literally shopped for a girl and pointed at Vee, engineered the "accidental" collision so Vee apologized to Randi. "Do you think she'll like the bench?" Then the book switched to Vee's POV (ch4 on) and we watch the trap close from inside her: the "chance" coffee-shop meeting where Pace tutored her calculus and got her number, the froyo date, the tannin-kiss debrief where Randi comes for the report, and the first real dinner date where Vee — after saying "I don't kiss a man I've known a few days" — pulled him back and kissed him herself. Now a hiking date is set at his isolated house. Vee thinks all of this is hers, freely chosen.

HOW I FEEL ABOUT EACH OF THEM

Pace: dread, not swoon. His competence is not goodness and the book has never once let me confuse them. Daphne is the flag. I'm watching for the moment the book stops undercutting his tenderness and asks me to melt — hasn't happened yet.

Randi: pity, mostly, and unease. She's a wounded thing pointing her wound at someone else. I don't excuse her; the book doesn't either. She owes Vee everything and Vee owes her nothing but trust she shouldn't have given.

Vee: protectiveness that aches. I'm ahead of her exactly as the jacket promised and I hate it.

Cassie: full trust. She's my insurance policy. My real fear is her getting frozen out or sidelined so the seduction can run unchecked.

The book itself: good hands, warily. It keeps the awareness switched on. That's the only reason I'm still here.
