So the title's a feint. That's the whole chapter, really: her building the case for him out of his own behavior, and me sitting here watching her lawyer for the prosecution and call it a love letter.

And okay. I have to be honest about the thing this chapter did that I didn't want it to do. The weight loss. That paragraph nearly got past my guard because it's *good* — she's eating like a field hand at his table, the woman who spent years pushing plates away half done, and her body is quietly burning down whole weekends of him, and the *bras keep the secret*, the band riding higher. That's tender and it's true to a person and for half a second I felt the warmth of a girl being fed by someone who wants to feed her. And then the cold part of me that's been reading this whole book went: he is recomposing her body and she experiences it as being cared for, and she never asked it to, and neither did I. "She'd been losing weight since she met him." The book put that sentence on the page on purpose. It knows. It always knows — that's the only reason I'm still in the chair. But God, it's using the knowing to get closer, not to warn me.

The scrunchies. Let me talk about the scrunchies, because this is the master stroke and it's vile. Ch32 gave us the form of green scrunchies on the counter, no note, "more of them than a person could go through quickly" — he means to be looking at her hair a long while. Fine, sweet, a claim dressed as a gift. Now: she leaves her sprawl across his one clean surface, the surface that every other hour of the week holds nothing but his razor squared to the edge. He *sees* it in the mirror. Says nothing. And a week later he's cleared the counter — and her stomach drops, "he'd minded after all," she's braced to learn where a guest's things go once the guest has been too much — and then she opens the drawer and he's *given her the top one*. The easy one. The one your hand goes to first. Folded his whole ordered self down into one crammed drawer to make her a place.

Here's why it iced me. That is a real romantic gesture. In a different book I would melt. But I've read thirty-four chapters of this man and I know his engine: he never says the thing, he *builds* the thing, so that you furnish the meaning yourself and the meaning is always "I belong here now, permanently, and I did it without ever asking." He didn't say "move in a little." He installed a drawer. He watched her worry about being too much — he *let* her have that fear, watched it in the mirror and said nothing — and then answered it days later with an architecture that means she can never leave without it being an eviction. The drawer is a claim. "Room for more of her, laid out like something prepared and waiting." Prepared. He prepared it. He always prepares it. The debrief, the dance lessons, the dress — everything with this man is prepared in advance and sprung as though spontaneous, and she reads the spring as tenderness because she never sees the preparation. The jacket told me: "the invitation reached her long before she knew there was one." The drawer is that sentence made furniture.

And the "no note. There was never a note" refrain — the book keeps calling attention to his silence as the medium of control. He says love in nothing and everything, and she's decided she "could live clear inside the everything-he-did and not go hungry for the saying." That's her adapting to a man who will never be accountable in words, and framing the adaptation as spiritual growth. She loves him and he has never said it and she has made peace with never hearing it. At twenty-one. That's not maturity, sweetheart, that's you learning to need less.

She's on his side of his own secrets.

Where am I? Still whole, Vee's still whole inside, still fully hers — that's the thread I've held the whole book and it hasn't snapped. But this is the chapter where the trap stopped hurting her and started comforting her, and the book knows that's more frightening than any spanking, which is exactly why I trust it enough to keep going. It's not asking me to swoon at the drawer. It's showing me a woman swooning at a cage that fits, and letting me feel the pull of it so I understand her. That's the book being awake. If it had wanted applause for the drawer I'd be gone. It wants my dread. It has it.
