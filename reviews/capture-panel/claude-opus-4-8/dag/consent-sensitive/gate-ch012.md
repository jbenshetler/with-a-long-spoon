REACTION

Okay. So this is the chapter I was bracing for — the one where the seduction gets to run with nobody sighted in the room. Cassie folded her look away and left at the top of eleven; up here on the mountain there's no Cassie, no Randi except her voice as a party favor (*ravish*, handed up like a dare Vee's been walking under ever since). Just the two of them and the weather. This is precisely the stretch I said would lose me if the book switched the awareness off and coasted on how nice Pace is. And I want to be honest: it did NOT switch off. It just moved the instrument. The watching used to live in Cassie; here it lives in Vee's mother's voice, and the book never once lets me forget that her heat is running through her damage. "The heat that knew the voice was right, that wanted to be the very thing the voice said." That's not a naive book. A naive book gives you the striptease clean and asks you to cheer. This one keeps its thumb on the bruise the whole time.

And still — God, the pattern. Three times in one chapter he does the move. The boots: he takes her raw heel in his hand and fixes it and says *should've done that at the lot, I'm sorry, I didn't think to ask.* The cherry pit: she spits it, the shame-word comes up, and he lays his own pit down beside hers and doesn't look at her doing it, so she gets to learn without being caught learning. The dress: she reaches for the russet silk, hears the reach in her own voice, sets it back on the shelf — and he doesn't pick it up either, he just says *that sounds like a beautiful dress* and lets it lie. Every single time: a small shame surfaces, and he absolves it without ever making her small, and the absolution is the hook. I've watched him run this exact engine since the menu — *may I choose.* The difference is I now think the book is running it ON PURPOSE and knows I can count. He banked that dress. He knows the color, the occasion, the "I can't afford it, obviously," the black thing in the back of the closet. That got filed. I'd bet the volume on it.

The flannel. This was the closest I came to setting it down, because for about a paragraph the book handed me the pure swoon object — the selfless man giving up his only dry shirt and walking back out into the rain with his back to me — and I felt the melt being requested and I got my hackles up, because THAT is the naive move, the grand engineered gesture played straight for applause. And then. His hand is shaking when she reaches for it. "He'd been a long way from the question of whether he was cold." That pulled it back off the edge for me, and it's the most interesting thing in the chapter, because it cuts two ways at once and the book won't resolve it: either he was so deep in watching her he lost his own body — control, appetite, the man who "went in where he wasn't asked" — or the machine is starting to cost the operator something real, exactly like the jacket promised. I can't tell yet and I don't think I'm supposed to. That ambiguity is the book keeping faith with me.

The undressing itself is genuinely, annoyingly good, and it's good the way this book has decided to be good: she calls HIM back, she raps the glass, she crooks the finger, she does the buttons, he holds absolutely still and doesn't lean in or lift a hand, and the gladness in him is "the whole of the answer." That's the erotic thesis the jacket sold — choosing as the hottest thing — and it landed on me, I won't pretend it didn't. But I can also see the trap breathing underneath it: he built every condition. The isolation, the rain, the one dry shirt, the back turned. She "freely" chose the thing he arranged for her to be able to choose. Both things are true in the same beat and the book is holding both. That's the good hands. The dread didn't leave; it just got warm.

Two small things I'm keeping. Meg's boots — a half-size big, wrong, rubbing — a borrowed girl I don't have, and the wrongness of the borrowed thing is doing quiet work. And the cop at the end, squawking her big declaration right out of her mouth before she could say it, Pace driving off with lights behind him into the rain. She never got to complete the surrender-speech. The book took it back the way the water was the one thing that had never been taken. I noticed that.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *he gave her his only dry shirt AND his hand was shaking and I don't know if that's the sweetest thing or the scariest and I hate that I can't tell.*
