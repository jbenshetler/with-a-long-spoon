He's not there. That's the whole chapter, really — they built the dress for exactly this room and he went to Berkeley, and so the one time the silk does the thing it was made to do, the architect isn't in the seats. And my first stupid hopeful thought was: good. Good. Let her have one that's hers. Let the room be hers, the walk be hers, the leg out of the slit be hers.

And the book let me have that for about a page and then closed its hand.

Because the room wasn't the room she'd pictured — no cinematic turning, no late entrance, just navy girls and somebody's parents and warm dull kindness, nobody watching anybody longer than the walking took. She had to MAKE the room wake up. And she did it herself, on the second step — "she took a breath, and made the second step long." That's the part that got me in the chest, because it's genuinely her choice, her nerve, her decision a half-second ahead of her head, and the heat came up to meet it exactly the way it came up in his mirror. It's real autonomy. And it is, precisely, the shape they trained into her. The book doesn't make me choose between those. It holds them both and then, in the last sentence, names the fusion out loud — she couldn't tell you whether it was pride or the same heat still running, "or whether, by now, the two came to the same thing." That's the harvest, stated. That's the conditioning reaching the floor of her: her own triumph is no longer separable from the appetite they installed. Thirty-one chapters and the author still sees it. I keep waiting for the chapter that's naive and it keeps not coming.

And here's the thing that actually scared me, quietly: she's doing his move now. Small careful steps to hide the slit, then the deliberate reveal — taking all that trouble and then the further trouble to hide the trouble, except now SHE'S the one running it. She sent him the photo uncropped, knowing he'd see only the dress doing its job and never what else was in the frame — "That part was hers." She thinks she's keeping a private self he can't reach. Same as "getting away with something," same as the thief who doesn't know the door was left open for her. Every time Vee thinks she's holding something back just for herself, I feel the jacket's hand on the back of my neck: the invitation reached her before she knew there was one. Her secret self isn't a secret from them. It's the product.

But — Cassie. Cassie walked her to the doors, held her phone, said "You look—" and didn't finish it, and came back after for the terrible sandwich and "be famous at me" and "streetlight." That's the only uncomplicated love in the book and it's still here, bright at the corners, denying it to the grave. As long as Cassie's in the frame eating a bad sandwich with her I can breathe a little. I'm braced for the chapter where she's gone.
