REACTION

Okay so I braced, going in — a hangover morning, no heat coming, and I could feel the book reaching for my hand. He baked bread while she slept. He left water where she'd reach for it, on a folded paper towel so it wouldn't ring the wood. He put his own undershirt on her and steered her to bed and kissed her forehead after he thought she was gone. This is the sympathy chapter. This is the one that comes right when I've gotten coldest on him, and I clocked that going in and I'm still telling you it worked on me some, and I'm annoyed about it, and I'm not sure I should be annoyed.

Because here's the thing I've been waiting the whole book for and I said so in my own notes: does the daddy-wound ever get shown in *his* head or does it stay a Sheri-certificate forever. And this chapter is him talking. Finally. In his own flat voice. The one-friend reveal gutted me a little — "Sheri's my friend. My one friend. There isn't anyone else." College at fifteen, off the end of every scale, the window that opens once and closed before he could get through it. Men make friends by losing to each other from the middle of the pack and he was never in the pack. That's *specific*, that's not a sob story, that's a real mechanism for how a person ends up alone, and I believed it. And "it isn't tragic" is the tell and the book knows it's the tell — Vee even names it, "the good order was the tell." So the book isn't asking me to be fooled. It's showing me a man who has filed his own catastrophe into good order, which is exactly what a man who was hurt as a boy does.

But — and this is the part that kept my wariness lit — look at what's actually underneath, because the book put it right there and did NOT let Vee close her hand around it. Daphne. "She'd had that much of him to begin with only because of the thing about it that was wrong, the thing Vee still would not set a word to." The ugly grown-up word forming under her tongue and pressed back down. That's the book telling me, over Vee's shoulder, that Pace's first and only love was a grown woman who had a *child*, and that the whole architecture of who he is — the attention, the courtliness, the asking-before-taking, the method — grew out of being groomed. And now he's running some version of that same apparatus on a twenty-one-year-old. The book is not hiding this. It's making Vee unable to see it while making sure I can't NOT. That's the move I keep giving it credit for.

And "his one friend on this earth a five-foot woman who could never want him — the only kind, she was sure now, the world had ever quite let him keep." Sit with that. The only people he's safe to keep are the ones who can't want him. Which means Vee — who wants him, who he's engineered into wanting him — is by his own logic not someone he gets to keep. There's a doom in that line I don't think Vee heard and I heard it loud.

What I keep turning over: is this real tenderness or is this the deepest cut of the method — the wound as the final tool, the one that makes her come around the counter with her whole weight? Because that's what she does. She crosses to him and holds him "with her whole weight committed," the way Sheri did, and his arms come around her *after a moment,* not fast, and he looks past her at the middle of the morning and doesn't try to make either of them feel better. And I couldn't tell — I genuinely could not tell — if that's a man too gutted to perform or a man who knows that not performing IS the performance. And I think the book couldn't tell either, or rather, I think the honest answer is *both,* the way ch59 gave me both about Randi. The wanting is real AND it's being used. The wound is real AND it's the best tool he owns. He doesn't have to choose and neither do I.

No women this chapter, nothing for my particular hunger, and I notice the book spent a whole chapter buying Pace back and zero chapters this stretch on the thing I actually came for. But it's setup. The dance is Saturday. Sheri's floor is coming. I'll give it this one.
