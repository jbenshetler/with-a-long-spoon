REACTION

So this is a Pace chapter, top to bottom, and I want to be honest about the two things it did to me at once, because they pull opposite ways.

First: I liked Vee ugly. I liked her a lot. The jealousy that comes up in her at the Rusty Nail is *hers* — specific, mean, rooted in the exact shame the whole book has been fingering. She counts the women. She lets herself feel like the exception and then watches herself demoted to the rule. And then she does the thing — "Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe from here, too?" — and she hears it go past the point where she can catch it, like a glass off a shelf, and she can't. That's real. That's a woman whose wound runs her mouth. The venom belonged to her. Nobody installed it. For once the strongest thing on the page was coming *out* of Vee instead of being collected off her, and I sat up for it.

But — and here's where I go cold — look at what the chapter is *for*. Every single beat past that outburst exists to buff Pace to a higher shine. The bartender does his PR for him: gets girls home safe, leaves them at the door, doesn't take advantage, *not that plenty haven't tried.* "Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey." He set the beer down untouched and left. He chose her. And the chapter closes with Vee's verdict on herself: *she'd punished him for being the man she wanted him to be. She'd done it to a good man.* So her instinct — *a man like that has girls, and you'd be one of them* — flared up, and the book marched in and proved the instinct wrong and made her *ashamed of having had it.* That's a story training a woman out of her own wariness and calling the wariness the flaw. And I'm a reader with that wariness. I felt the book reaching for mine.

I especially don't love the timing. One chapter after Daphne — nineteen and fifteen, the word neither of us said, a man who was groomed and calls it luck — the book turns around and hands me a whole chorus swearing Pace is safe, would never, leaves them at the door. That reassurance came too fast and too clean on the heels of the thing it's reassuring me *about.* I don't trust the bartender. She's doing damage control for a man the previous chapter quietly unsettled me about.

And the dance itself is the book's whole thesis with the lid off: *don't think, just feel the lead.* The frame she fought as too close, too fixed, too much rule, is the thing that moves her once she stops resisting — hold still in the one place the next step can come from and there's nothing to do but take it. Surrender rendered as a two-step. The book even hands me my own suspicion for one clean second — *how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd built out of wanting him* — and then lets "the floor take it." I notice it doing that. Handing me the question so I'll feel seen, then dissolving it in pleasure before it can cost anything. Twice now, that move.

What's not here: Randi. Not one beat in her body, not one on the page. Twenty-six chapters and the queer want is still a bookend — she's the person Vee will *run to* with her wound. Which is exactly the machine's design, Randi as the soft place the man's night breaks her toward. Off-page, deferred, again.

But I'll tell you what my hand did at the last line. *Go to Randi's.* Wounded, ashamed, undefended, at dawn, no plan. That is the first time the book has aimed Vee at Randi with something actually torn open in her — not over lunch, not being drawn out, but *needing* her. If the charge I've waited the whole book for is ever going to arrive as *hers* — driven by need instead of coaxed out by Randi's mouth at a curb — it's in the room this chapter just walked her toward. So I'm tired of the Pace-is-perfect machine and I want the next chapter anyway, and those are two different feelings and I'm not going to average them.
