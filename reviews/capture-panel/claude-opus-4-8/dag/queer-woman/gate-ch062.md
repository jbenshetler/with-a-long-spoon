So this is the chapter where the book comes to collect on Pace. Sixty-one chapters of building him into the thing I can't stand, and now — hungover morning, undershirt to the knees, the melted glass of water on a folded paper towel so it won't ring the wood, the *cinnamon raisin loaf baked while she slept* — here's the invoice, and the invoice is: love him. He's lonely. He was hurt. One friend on this earth. Not home since sixteen because it "weren't safe." The father, unnamed, doing the worst work in the sentence. And I felt it, that's the thing I have to be honest about. My chest did the thing. The boy at fifteen in a building of grown men, no one his age, no lake summers, eating a whole life alone at a table for one — that landed. It's beautifully built. It's *supposed* to land.

And that's exactly where I got cold, right in the middle of getting warm. Because I know this move. This is the abuse-survivor origin handed to me at the precise moment I've decided the man is running an operation on every person Vee loves, and the book knows I've decided that, and it's spending the most expensive capital it has — a hurt child — to buy back the man I clocked as inventory. Vee even names the tell for me: *the good order was the tell.* He believes it isn't tragic. He set it in good order. She sees that the composure is the wound talking. Fine — I'll grant the wound is real. Wounds are real *and* people run cons; those have never once been mutually exclusive, and I refuse to let the loneliness launder the annexing. A man who can't make friends because "women misunderstand the friendship, it gets awkward" — read that again. That's not a man who can't connect. That's a man who has only ever built the one kind of tie, the kind that goes *one way or the other,* and he's telling her so plainly she does the sharpening herself and calls it insight. Daphne wasn't the tragic exception. Daphne was the template. He said it out loud and she heard "sad."

Sheri, though. Sheri I actually want to talk about, because Sheri is alive in a way the book's other queer women haven't been let to be — loud, crooked makeup, taking the whole unhurried trip down Vee's bare January leg and arriving at that soundless appreciative *oh* at the shoes. A woman looking at Vee with frank uncomplicated appetite, and — here's the beat I'm keeping — Vee *liked it. Out loud. Sober. To Pace.* "I liked it, honestly." No stinger, no interpreter slamming the door, no *that was about Pace obviously.* That's her accurate body in daylight, spoken, and it cost her nothing this time. Small. But it's the first time the wanting-women got to just be a nice thing that happened, said plainly at a breakfast table, and I clocked it hard.

But I'm not naive about Sheri either. The taproom was a deposition. Every word out of her mouth was character-witness testimony for Pace — *you've got him happy, that's the whole book, far as I'm concerned. The whole hall's given up on him, hands stay where the dance puts them.* A queer woman produced, right on schedule, to vouch that he's safe — the one kind of woman he "gets to keep," per the book's own logic, the kind who could never want him, which is to say the kind who's structurally disarmed as a threat and free to sing his praises. Two years he's had her. She came out and lost her whole tablecloth and he showed up with donuts and fixed her lock, and I believe he did, and I *also* notice that a man who collects the newly-exiled and makes himself their only safe harbor is describing a pattern, not a kindness. She's not in on a plan. She's the fourth queer woman the book has deployed rather than let live — except this one it let live a little, and I'm grateful, and I'm watching.

Where's Randi. That's what I texted the friend at 1 a.m. — a whole chapter, no Randi, no plan-machinery, no charge, and I miss the ache I hate. This was the book resting me, feeding me the sympathy chapter so the next detonation hits softer walls. I ate the bread. I know what it was.
