WHO'S WHO

Randi — the huntress the trap finally caught, and this stretch is where I stopped hedging and wrote it down for good. Two chapters did it. In Pace's kitchen (23) she wouldn't eat — drew one line through the top of the sauce and left the bowl to skin over, said "it's *selfish*, Pace," rode him with her eyes shut and no *tell me* and turned her back and rolled into the cashmere. That was the withholding, hers for once. Then four chapters on (30, but really the rhyme is with 27) she ate the whole plate, couscous to a ruler-line, got up and pulled his undershirt out of the second drawer without hunting for it, and said the thing: "I keep waiting to get bored of her — you always get bored — and it doesn't come. She got past me before I could help it." She's jealous and I can't name of whom — of Vee for having him, of him for having Vee, of the two of them for falling into the thing she built and can't get inside of. Still the black hair, the white-gold band, the dark-green Mercedes, the bitter-orange-and-green scent, no sweetness in it. Still runs the same play — reframe your worst night into your win, hand you your own feeling pre-chewed, walk you down the staircase — and now the staircase ends in debt and a borrowed shoe. Blue toenails, lately. She's stopped fleeing his bed. I can't tell if that's healed or fully machined.

Pace — real name Peter, and that name is the biggest thing I got this whole stretch. Math PhD at twenty-two, at college since fifteen off a test score, and the fireside chapter (27) finally opened him: seventh grade, taught himself trig for a graphics program, asked too many questions, and the teacher moved his desk into the hall and gave him long-division worksheets for a year. They took the thing he loved and made it the punishment. And Daphne — the tutor they assigned the fifteen-year-old, nineteen to his fifteen, two years, "the best two years I'd had," she left when she turned twenty-one and the rooms she walked into were rooms he couldn't stand in for four more years. He laid it out in good order, every reason slotted, and named it luck, and the good order was the tell. The scar high on his inner thigh — she found it again, ran her fingers up more of it than she'd known, and he pressed her hand flat over it and said "not tonight." A door with a time on it, not a wall. Still the maker — the burgundy dress finished at some hour with no business being awake in it, tenons trued in the cold shop. Still keeps the too-warm house, still ladles her the small bowl, still won't text, "not a thing he decided, only what he is."

Vee — twenty-one, econ, red-copper hair, and this stretch is where she started doing to others what's being done to her, and where she moved herself hardest and wrecked something herself for the first time. She put a credit card with nothing under it on a boutique counter and called the shoes "what the dress was owed." And she blew up the whole Rusty Nail night herself — took the one true good thing about Pace and threw it in his face over a blonde's poison — and then ran to Randi to fix it. Frosted-plum toenails, chosen to catch the light the way the silk does.

Cassie — still the clean water, still the only working pair of eyes, and now being quietly written out of the frame. She read the made bed before Vee sat down (25) and let Randi have the reveal. The blonde at the Rusty Nail — the plant. "He's so good about getting a girl home safe." Gone in a breath, did her whole job. And the older bartender — flat, credible, "wasn't him they were jealous of, honey," the closest the book's come to arguing Pace's case *to me*, and I felt handled by it.

HOW I FEEL ABOUT EACH OF THEM

Randi — the ache is at its deepest and my suspicion is at its sharpest and they no longer cancel. The warmth she spends on Vee is real — the laugh that costs her something, "dinner in Paris," on her knees tending Vee's foot — AND it's material, harvested, driven back to Pace's mattress to come on. Both true at once, and the book keeping that knife balanced is the one thing it hasn't dulled. She said "it's selfish" for me, out loud, and I loved her for it and knew she was only half aiming at Pace and half at her own jealousy. She owes Vee the truth chosen before Vee was anything, and she's paying it back in leashes she calls friendship.

Pace — Peter cracked my guard more than anything in twenty-seven chapters. The hall, the desk, the year of long division; Daphne at nineteen with the fifteen-year-old and him calling it luck. I don't forgive the pocketing — he still hands Randi the true things and himself decides Vee's pace — but I finally believe the loneliness under it, and I believe he's a man who was taken apart young by the exact machine he now runs gently on a girl. The bartender chapter tried to hand me "good man" clean and I bristled; the fireside earned me back by making him strange and hurt instead of exonerated. When the current reverses — his hand over hers on the scar, "not tonight" as a door not a wall — I think the book knows.

Vee — plain love, real admiration, and now real fear *for* her, the money kind. She's funniest and most herself and most gone all at once. "I took it, for once" and the Edna Mode laugh are her moving herself; the empty credit card and "because Randi had said so" are her being moved and grateful for it. Who owes whom: unchanged and worse. She's the only one who hands over more than she's asked and the only one nobody's told a true thing.

Cassie — full trust, quiet grief. She's being subtracted, not attacked, and that's crueler and harder to point at.
