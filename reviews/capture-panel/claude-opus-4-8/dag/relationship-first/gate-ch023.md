REACTION

Oh, this one landed. This is the chapter I've been waiting three chapters for and it came in from the side I didn't expect — not the panties, not the report handed over, but Randi *not eating*.

The bowl. The one line drawn through the top of the sauce with the spoon and then the spoon set down, and it just sits there the whole chapter, going to a skin over the peas, and at the end he covers it with a plate and puts it in the fridge for her. That's the whole chapter, actually. He's the one who ladles the portion, always the small bowl, the one he slices her apples into — and for once she won't take what he portions out. I felt that before I could explain it. She said "it's wonderful," and *he doesn't know where to put it*, because she's never once said that to him — she says it to waiters, to Vee — and that's the tell that tonight she's treating him like a stranger, like a man she's performing politeness at. The compliment is the coldest thing in the room.

And then she picks the fight, and — this is the part that got me — she picks the fight using *my* argument. "It's selfish, Pace." She says the thing I've been muttering at this man for twenty chapters. He's dressed it up as patience, as *she chooses every step*, as the courtly ask-first, and Randi walks into his kitchen and calls it what it is: he's got the girl Friday to Sunday every weekend and he's in no hurry because the slowness is the thing he likes, it's *for him*. "That's how it works for you." God. She's right, and she knows the machine better than anyone because she built half of it. But here's what's underneath, and the book was smart enough not to say it: she's not actually mad about Vee's pace. She's jealous. She rode him tonight with her eyes shut and no *tell me* — she cut off the exact ritual that was always about extracting Vee through him, eating Vee through his body — and this time she didn't want the report, she wanted him, for herself, badly and meanly, and then rolled into the cashmere with her back to him. "I want us," said again, but this time in the negative, in everything she withheld.

The reversal of the whole surveillance apparatus — *he* studies *her* now. He sets each piece beside the next, the speed, the shut eyes, no tell me, the back, and they won't go together into anything he can name. The huntsman can't read the huntress. He goes back through the evening from the gravel forward looking for what he did wrong and it isn't there. Because it isn't a thing he did — it's that she's caught, same as Vee, and he can't see it either. Two people who plan everything, both blindsided by the real thing getting in. The jacket promised me exactly this — "as true for the two who started it" — and this is the first chapter where I *felt* it instead of being told it.

What I loved craft-wise: the book shut off its own tics for once. No *floozie*, no *yeah/yeah/yeah* ladder, and crucially no narrating-its-own-technique — in fact it did the opposite, it showed us the technique *failing*. She didn't do "the inch and the stop and the eyes." The absence of the move was the whole scene. That's the book trusting me, finally, and it's the most alive Pace has ever been to me precisely because he's lost.

What I'm suspicious of / carrying: the panties never came up. I was sure Friday's trade would surface in this room and it didn't — she told him "she told me about Friday" and he goes glad all the way down, and that's it. So either the report economy is genuinely breaking down (Randi's stopped collecting) or it's being held for later, and either reading works for me. And I notice: nobody's told Vee a true thing still. But now nobody's telling *each other* the true thing either. The two conspirators have gone opaque to one another and that's a much more interesting problem than the one I thought I was reading.

I'd text a friend: *he put her uneaten dinner in the fridge for her and I had to put the book down.*
