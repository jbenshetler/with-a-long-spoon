WHO'S WHO

Randi — the glamorous friend from the back cover, and the one who actually broke first. Black hair, startling blue eyes she's built her whole life around, sorority, sits on Standards, has the single room. Runs the game on the outside; on the bench in chapter one she came apart, begged in a voice that wasn't hers, cried, and then went to the mirror and decided *not ever* — she'd give Pace the face and take the real one back behind it forever. Portions herself: apple slices, egg-white mother. She's the huntress who thinks she's assembling Vee as a gift for Pace, and doesn't know how much she now wants both of them.

Pace — the math PhD, "finishing, more or less." Older-seeming, small, moves like he's never had to think about where his body is. Not the boys' clothes — soft henleys, washed shirts too good for a handyman. Runs the same play every time: back off at the door, "May I," give the pencil back at the exact line, leave the last inch so *she* closes it. Built the bench. Keeps the too-warm house, Steely Dan low, right hand always the eating hand. Has a dead-or-gone woman named Daphne behind him — built a door once, didn't respect it, learned. Tender as a weapon. I don't trust him and I don't think the book fully knows how much I don't.

Vee (Vivienne Thorne, "V.T. like the university") — the girl on the cover. Twenty-one, econ, hungry in every sense. Tall, lush, red-copper hair, freckles, laughs with her whole body forgetting she can be seen — which is exactly what got her pointed at across the quad. Portioned herself too (the mother's voice, the cardigan over the better shirt). Reads rooms, keeps her own end up socially, borrows real shoes for a hike she's never taken. Loves the water because down there nobody wants anything from her.

Cassie — Vee's roommate, four words a day, ball cap, boxy navy suit, won't perform. The only person in this book with no angle. Says "Goodbye, Vee" — the whole word. "That's not a boy. That's a man." Keeps her jokes *for* Vee instead of pitching them into the bleachers. My proxy and my favorite.

Brooke (chapter president, doesn't stand to stop you), Sloane, Kinsey — the sorority net that audited Randi Saturday morning and is quietly tracking Pace (the white pickup behind Hahn).

WHERE THINGS STAND

Chapter one: Randi on the bench, undone, decides to disappear behind the face. Two: she gets home in last night's clothes and survives Brooke's four-minute interrogation, then lies under a synthetic duvet not-crying with her shoes on. Three: Pace and Randi walk the quad playing the pointing game — he points, she picks, she thinks she's humoring him — and it ends with Randi choosing Vee (engineering the shoulder-check herself) and asking "do you think she'll like the bench?" Four through ten are the seduction of Vee, from both girls: Randi love-bombs her (stats seat, Chi Latte, "love you, girl"); Pace "accidentally" solves her calculus in the coffee shop (Euler's — "it's for this"), takes her number with a "May I call you." Froyo date, then the Lebanese dinner where she eats without watching herself and takes the first kiss herself under a tree. Chapter seven is the one behind the glass: Randi at Pace's, using the wine like the apple, extracting a report on Vee while her body tells on her — and Pace, crucially, *pockets* the one true thing Vee said and doesn't hand it to Randi. Ten: Vee tells Cassie everything, counts off "off six-fourteen" as proof she's safe. A hike is coming. So is the house. So is the bench.

HOW I FEEL ABOUT EACH OF THEM

Randi — this is where the book has me. I came in expecting to distrust the seductress and instead I ache for her. She's the most frightened person in the story and she's about to do to Vee exactly what was done to her. Both true. I want to protect her and I'm watching her set a trap.

Pace — cold admiration, rising dread. Every tender thing is engineering. The moment he kept something back from Randi is the moment he got more dangerous, not less — the two conspirators aren't one unit, they're managing each other. I want the book to know his certainty ("he took the body's answer for the true one; he usually did") is chilling, and I'm not always sure it does.

Vee — plain love and fear. She's not a dupe. She's *right* about her own yes ("I did it because I wanted to, on purpose") and still caught, and that's worse. Who owes whom: Randi and Pace owe Vee the truth they chose to withhold weeks ago. Cassie is owed nothing and is the only one paying attention.

Cassie — trust, full. The floor of the whole book.
