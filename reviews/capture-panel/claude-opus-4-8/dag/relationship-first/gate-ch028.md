REACTION

Okay so this is the first night the book let Vee be stupid, and I have complicated feelings about it.

The dance lesson — that's mine, that's the reason I read. Two people on a cold concrete pad under the field lights, and him breaking the thing down into "give me something to push back against," "close the space, come all the way in," the lead arriving in her body before her feet know what to do. "Being jelly doesn't work." I ate every second of that. It's the fitting scene again in a different key — his competence pointed at her, patient, exact — and it's the cleanest clothed heat the book's given me in a while because it's *doing* the thing instead of narrating that it's doing the thing. When she stops guessing and it takes her and he says "There it is," quiet — yeah. That's the good drug. And I loved that she reached, mid-floor, for the bill of it: "how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd been building out of wanting him." She's still the girl who moves herself. Even inside the swoon she's auditing it. I trust her more for that than for anything.

And then the plot showed up, and I felt the book change hands. The blonde peeling off the rail to plant the poison — "he's so good about getting a girl home safe" — that's machinery. That's the manufactured-misunderstanding engine that runs every romance my friends lend me, and I never signed that contract. Vee gets jealous, counts the girls, lashes out with real venom, flees, and then learns from a wise flat-voiced older bartender that she had it exactly backward: he doesn't bring dates, he doesn't take advantage, "Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey." I saw the shape of it coming from the first hug at the door and it delivered the shape and nothing but the shape. That's the thing I said I'd leave a book for — the plot moving the people instead of the people moving the plot.

But. Two things held me. One: "It came back smelling of fabric softener and no one." She rationed the smell of him off that shirt until it went stale and then washed it and killed it and put it on anyway. That's the truest line in the chapter and it's about the whole shirt-and-panties economy I've been tracking — the girl who keeps the evidence. Gutted me a little in one sentence. And the *floozie* wiring, which I've been calling wallpaper, is at least load-bearing tonight — it's the actual mechanism of the jealousy, her shame turned outward as cruelty at a man who's done nothing. So it earned its firing this once, even if I'm bone-tired of the word.

Two — and this is the whole reason my hand's already turning the page: she's not going to Cassie. She *can't* face Cassie. She's going to Randi. And I know what Randi is. I know the two of them chose her before she was anything. So this "I was wrong, I'm going to fix it" isn't repair, it's Vee walking straight into the arms of the woman who will *manage* this, who will get to be the one she confesses the whole ugly night to, who profits from exactly this. The book doesn't know I'm dreading the right room — or it does, and that's the withholding doing its job. I want that scene badly, not because I think it'll be tender but because I want to watch the machine catch her. That's dramatic irony pulling, not the romance plot.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *she's going to RANDI. not Cassie. RANDI. I need the next chapter, I don't even want it, I need it.*
