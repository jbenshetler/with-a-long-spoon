REACTION

Oh, this is the chapter I stay for. Two girls in a too-hot dorm room with the window cracked, sock feet, a pillow thrown, one of them spinning a clumsy half-dance into the closet door — this is the exact texture I came to this whole shelf looking for. Nothing "happens" and I didn't want anything to happen. I wanted the debrief. I wanted Vee narrating the brass tea-cages badly, needing a different voice for the tree part, going quiet and small when she got to the kiss. Rivers knows that a girl telling her friend about the good part changes register when she hits the real thing, and she wrote that drop in the voice without announcing it. I felt it.

And Cassie. God, Cassie. "How much did he give you to drink?" is the best line in the book so far and it's a line that loves Vee. That's the thing — Cassie's dryness has a floor of pure tenderness under it, "Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman," and she keeps the joke *for* Vee instead of pitching it up into the bleachers. She's the one person in this book so far who isn't running an operation. Which is exactly why the state-trooper bit landed on me like a cold hand and not a joke. "What's his name, so I know what to tell the state troopers when you go missing on a mountain with a man you met at a coffee shop." *Off six-fourteen, past where it goes to gravel.* That's the whole address. Cassie says it twice — "That's the whole address" — and she's playing, but I'm not, because I've read chapters one through nine and I know things Cassie doesn't and things Vee doesn't. I know about the bench in the middle of that house off six-fourteen. I know the pointing game. I know Randi.

So the thing this chapter did to me — and it's the merciless thing the jacket promised, the "ahead of her from the start" — is it took the single sentence Vee most wants Cassie to believe, "Nobody did anything to me. I wasn't talked into it. I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose," and it let her say it plain and glowing and *true*, while I sat there with the whole apparatus in my head. She did want it. She did do it on purpose. And it was chosen for her weeks ago by two people over lunch. Both of those are true at once and that's the knife. The book isn't making her a dupe. It's making her right about her own yes and still caught. That's harder to watch than if she were being fooled.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *she just told her roommate she knows where he lives — "off six-fourteen, I know where he lives and everything" — as PROOF she's safe, and I wanted to climb into the book.* The dossier she counts off like plenty — his water bottle, the trail, the real shoes — is the exact list of things that are nothing. And Cassie, who has no idea about any of it, is the only one whose instincts are pointed the right way. "Rich people hike. Poor people walk." She keeps circling the thing she can smell but can't see.

I'm not turned on by this chapter and it's not trying to be. It's a warm-down, a breath. What it did instead was make me love Vee more and dread more, in the same beat, which is a harder trick than the bench pulled. I noticed there's no repetition rattling me here, no sentence explaining the feeling the last one gave me — the withholding is holding its shape. Vee doesn't know. Cassie almost does. I do. The book knows exactly how much each of us has and it's parceling it out like a person who's done this before.

The one small worry flickering: the "borrow good ones / real shoes" is a plot engine warming up — hiking date next, the house next, we're being walked toward six-fourteen. As long as the walk stays this much about *these two on the bunk* and not about getting Vee to the mountain, I'm all the way in.
