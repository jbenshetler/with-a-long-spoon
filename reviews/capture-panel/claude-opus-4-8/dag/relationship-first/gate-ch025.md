Oh, this is the money chapter and I mean that two ways. After 23 — which gutted me, the uneaten bowl and the one line drawn through the sauce and Randi riding him with her eyes shut and no *tell me*, the huntress finally bleeding — this one comes in light on purpose, a comedy, two girls wrecking a shoe store. And I laughed. The yeti canoe, the pink rhinestone platforms held to the light like serious work, "one brisk walk from becoming a sandal." That's the friendship novel I came here for: two people being genuinely funny together, and Randi's real laugh, the one it costs her to give. For a stretch I forgot to be suspicious, which is exactly the point and exactly why I trust it less.

Because under the laughing, the book did the coldest thing it's done to Vee yet, and it did it with a credit card. She's an *econ major*. She runs TJMaxx math, the most-I-could math, and she's good at it — and then she stands in the brass-and-lacquer hush and can't say the true thing (I can't afford to be inside this door), and puts the debit back, and takes out the emergency card with *nothing under it*, and signs, and won't let it reach her face because the one thing she won't do in that room is flinch. That's the seduction leaving the bedroom and getting into her bank account, and it's the first cost she can't hand back or fold in tissue. Next month it'll still be sitting there. Meanwhile Randi buys loafers for a multiple of that, several times over, without looking down, telling a story the whole time. The asymmetry is the whole trap in one counter. "It wants to walk in late and stop the room cold" — Randi says out loud the thing Vee's been running behind her own eyes all week, and hearing it in someone else's mouth makes it real, which is precisely the machine. She doesn't sell Vee a shoe. She hands Vee her own daydream back, clean, so Vee thinks she chose it.

And then Simpson's paradox, up on the board, the little red dot on one number. Same numbers, nobody touched one of them, put the two years together and the answer reverses. The book handed me its thesis in a stats lecture and I'm of two minds — it's a touch neon, the kind of self-narration I've been clocking and getting tired of. But it's oblique enough to forgive, because it *is* the jacket: every yes freely given, nothing taken, and the sum of them is a woman upside down. I'll take it as a metaphor rather than a lecture. Barely.

The thing that actually got me, though, quiet in the middle: Cassie turned a few degrees in her seat the whole time, never once looking at the professor, never caring about any dress, watching only what the dress had *done to her friend* — because the friend is the only part of it she's ever been able to see. God, Cassie. She's still the clean water and I'm still braced for what the book's going to do to the one who noticed. And Randi's "he made it *to* you, not *for* you" — I'll grant it, that's a new phrasing and it landed, it named the tears from the sun porch. The toenail beat — Randi on one knee on the pale floor, holding the bare foot to the light, one thumb across the plum nail, and it goes all the way up Vee and she has to grab Randi's shoulder to stop the room swinging — that's the heat between the two women coming in under the comedy, "same size seven, practically a marriage." The window dropping its half-inch on its own again; she rides in cars like this now. She's being furnished, room by room, into their world.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *she just went into debt for the shoes and called it "what the dress was owed" — an ECON major — and I think that's the exact moment they've got her.*
