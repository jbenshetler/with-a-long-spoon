REACTION

Okay, so this is the quiet one. No Randi, no chalkboard, no venom — just the two of them and a week of missing folded into one night, and I felt myself go soft against my own better judgment, which is exactly the thing I keep saying I'll watch for.

The good, first, because it was good and I won't pretend otherwise: he never goes straight at his own finish. "It always came around to her first, brought all the way over and usually more than once before he took a single thing for himself." That's the sentence I've been starving for across a whole genre. That's the *thing.* And there could be an after — a man coming back to her an hour later like she was something he'd been turning over while the salmon cooked. That is care rendered from inside her body, and it landed, and I let it land.

But I'm not able to just sit in the warm bath, because this chapter is doing something quieter and cleverer than heat. Two details snagged me and won't come out. First: the weight loss. "Her body quietly burning down whole weekends of him, and she'd never asked it to." She's *disappearing* by degrees and the book frames it as a happy secret the bras are keeping. A girl getting physically smaller inside a thing two people planned for her — no. That's not romantic to me, that's a symptom, and the prose petting it like a compliment is the winking I flinch at.

And second, the drawer. God, the drawer. On its face it's the most tender gesture in the book — he folded his whole ordered self down into one crammed drawer and gave her the top one, the easy one your hand goes to first, no note, made her a *place* instead of telling her it didn't matter. If this were a different book I'd be crying into the pillow. But I know what building is. That's not a man making room; that's the trap being fitted with a home. You don't clear a drawer for a season. You clear a drawer to make leaving unthinkable — so that one day the absence of the drawer is the thing that would break her. The book wants me to read nest-building as devotion, and the horrible part is *it is both,* the same way "thank you for telling me what she said" was both. I can't get the two apart and it's making me a little frantic.

And "Peter." Again. Into the sleeping shape of him. She's the only one who says it, she guards it like a coin, and every time she does it I remember it's the name the groomed boy took out of the answer. She thinks she's holding his real self. She's holding the wound that's eating her.

She said she loves him. He's never said it. She's decided she can live inside everything-he-does and not go hungry for the saying — and that's the sentence that scared me most in the whole chapter, because that's a woman teaching herself not to need the words, learning to be fed by inference, which is *precisely* how you keep a person on short rations and call it a feast. What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *he gave her a drawer and she's losing weight and neither of them will say the word and I think that's the whole con, the nicest con, the one you'd thank him for.*

Still. It stayed on the right side of my line, barely, because nothing was taken here — she reached first, she came early, every yes was hers, and the tenderness read as real even while it read as architecture. That's the tightrope this book keeps walking and I keep gripping the wire.
