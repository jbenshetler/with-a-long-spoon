REACTION

Okay so this is the chapter where I finally felt the floor under the whole thing creak, and not in the direction the book keeps lulling me toward.

The stats lecture. Simpson's paradox. I actually put the book down for a second. Each year on its own, the first man is the better hitter. Put the two years together and the second man is. Same numbers. Nobody touched one of them. The book put that in the room on purpose — it's not décor, it's the whole con stated out loud in a man's cereal-box voice while Vee is turned happily between her two girls not listening. Every single scene in this book, taken on its own, is tender. The dress made *to* her. Him up all night. Him asking before he takes. Scene by scene the first man wins — the care is real, I keep saying it's real because inside each chapter it IS. But put the chapters together and a different man is winning, and nobody touched one of the scenes to do it. That's the book telling me, flat, that my scene-by-scene accounting is exactly the thing being used on me. And on Vee. I felt cold reading it. That's the closest the book has come to the wink — except it's not a wink, it's the opposite of a wink, it's the book handing ME the math and trusting me to do it while Vee doesn't. That's why I'm still here.

And then — the shoes. God. I watched her put the debit card back. The card she pays her own way with, the one that only spends what's behind it — that's HER, that's the mountain-on-her-own-legs girl, that's the whole thing I love about her — and she put it back and laid down the emergency card with nothing under it, knowing, KNOWING it'll be sitting there next month, and signed and smiled and wouldn't let it reach her face because the one thing she wouldn't do in that room was flinch. And right next to her Randi buys loafers for several times that without once looking down, telling a story about a coat the whole time, and walks out in them like shoes are a thing you trade mid-afternoon. That contrast is brutal and the book KNOWS it's brutal. Vee carrying her box against her chest with both arms like it might get up and leave her, and Randi saying put it in the trunk, it's not going to spoil. One of these women can afford to be casual about beauty and one just went into debt to not flinch in front of her. And Vee thinks: I've never had a friend like this. Oh, baby.

"He didn't make this for you. He made it to you." I felt that land the same way Vee did, I won't pretend I didn't. It's a gorgeous line. And it's also Randi reading the cut off her body with two fingers at the shoulder seam, naming the thing so she owns it, same move she always makes — state the feeling back one degree truer so Vee hands it over. The move's getting easier to see because I've watched it eight times now, but it still works ON me, that's the horror of this book. I believe the tenderness and I can see the harvest and they're the same gesture.

The toe thing on the fitting bench — Randi down on one knee, holding the foot, the thumb across the nail, and Vee's hand shooting out to grab her shoulder because the floor tipped. That's the want the jacket promised, surfacing in Vee's own body now, under someone else's thumb, and she still files it under nothing. "Randi looked up and smiled and said nothing at all." She knows exactly what she just did. The "that's me exactly, same size, it's practically a marriage" — that's not accident, that's the door opening wider and Vee walking through squealing.

And Cassie. CASSIE. The one clean thing in the chapter. Never went down into the numbers, never looked at the professor, spent the whole forty seconds watching what the dress had done to her friend because the friend is the only part of any of it she's ever been able to see. "Was it a good night?" Something eased in her face and she picked her highlighter back up. She doesn't care about the dress, never cared what's on anyone's feet, wore the same fleece through two winters. She's reading Vee and not the plan, and she's RIGHT about Vee and blind to the machine, and she keeps getting one seat and a made bed and then the two architects close ranks around the girl between them and walk her out into the noon. I love her and I'm scared for how alone she's about to be in this.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: the book literally taught me Simpson's paradox and then used it on me in the same chapter and I am not okay.
