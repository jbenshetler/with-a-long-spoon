REACTION

Oh, this one. This is the chapter I'll be chewing on at 1 a.m., and I'd text a friend the single word *Daphne* and then three dots and then nothing for a while.

Because here it is. Here is where he *came from*. The whole first half is the softest thing the book's given me — the quilt, the robe that smells of him and wood dust, his palm just holding the weight of her breast without pressing, without asking, "as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm." The cumin. The unopened saffron old enough to vote. I was *disarmed*, genuinely, laughing with them, and I felt the book doing to me exactly what he does to her: giving the thing back warmer and stranger than I handed it over. The infinite-paint shape, the coastline that runs longer the shorter your ruler — "the language the world is written in" — I fell for him a little right there, God help me, same as she did. That's the trap and I know it's the trap and I walked in anyway because it was *beautiful*.

And then the hallway. Seventh grade, taught himself trig because he wanted to make a picture, and they moved his desk into the hall and gave him long division for a year. "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment." That's when my stomach started going, because I finally saw the shape of him — the man who hides all the trouble so only the effortless part shows, the man who asks before he takes, he was *built* in that hallway. He learned exactness as survival.

But Daphne. The nineteen-year-old "tutor" assigned to the fifteen-year-old boy. Two years. And he sets it down in that flat, good-order voice — every reason slotted, "nothing happened, nobody did anything wrong," "the best two years I'd had" — and names it *luck*. And the thing that gutted me, the thing that is the whole reason I can't put this book down: **Vee holds the word under her tongue.** "The clean ugly grown-up word." She does the arithmetic and it lands heavy and she *doesn't say it* because it isn't hers to say — and the narration is right there with her, calling the good order "the tell," saying he was defending it and didn't hear himself doing it. The book knows. The book knows it was abuse. It is not winking. It is showing me a man who was groomed and taught to call being taken from a gift — which is *precisely* the thing he is now doing to her, and the thing Randi did to her in the last chapters with "you're lucky." The machine has a mother. It got passed down. He's not a monster running a con; he's a wound running the only program that was ever installed in him. That doesn't make it okay. It makes it *worse*, and truer, and I am sick over it in the exact way that keeps me turning pages.

The scar on his inner thigh, "not tonight," her hand turned palm-to-palm over it — the door with a time on it. And her saying "Peter" into the dark just to keep the word. That undid me. She's reaching for the boy under the technique, and the awful part is the boy is real, and reaching for him is exactly how she gets taken.

This kept its nerve. It didn't ask me to find Daphne romantic. It asked me to grieve two people at once. Still here.
