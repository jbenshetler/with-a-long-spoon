REACTION

Oh, this one got me, and in a way the book hasn't managed in a while. Because for the first time in ages it's just her — no Pace behind the door running his unhurried program, no Randi across the table running the inventory check. Just Vee, flat on her back in a twin bed her legs outgrew in middle school, under the little gold swimmers, in a room that "loved a girl who no longer lived inside her." That line put a hand on my chest. I know that exact feeling — coming home grown and finding the house still set for your ghost. She's not disappearing in the starving way here; she's just *bigger than the room now*, and that's a different, cleaner ache, and the book let her have it without once asking me to call it lovely. That matters to me. I've been waiting all book for the chapter where the counter-voice is just *her*, alone, no one feeding it to her, and here it is.

And set right up against chapter 51 — which I read with my teeth together, watching Randi spend a whole flawless night learning that the perfect safe man reaches "nothing" compared to Pace on his worst night — this is devastating. Because I KNOW what Randi knows now, and Vee doesn't know any of it. Vee is lying there at 11:52 building a blonde tan fashion-model girl out of pure spite and handing her to Pace, convinced that's what he wants the second he's free of her. And I'm sitting here going: honey, six hours ahead, Randi just proved with an actual billionaire in an actual fairytale that *you* are the thing none of it can counterfeit one thread of. The dramatic irony is the whole engine of these two chapters and it's doing exactly what good dramatic irony does — it hurts in both directions at once.

The thing that really got under my skin: her own best memory turned on her. She reaches for the fra diavolo night, the sheet slipping, to get *warm* — and the hair goes gold and the leg goes tan and the blonde sits where she sat and "she could not keep her own night." God. That's the cruelest small thing the book has done to her and it didn't need a villain in the room to do it. That's just what fear does at midnight. I've been there in a cold bed doing that exact arithmetic.

And the texts. I read 51, so I watched Randi *construct* that "fairytale out here 💋" reply over a sleeping stranger, every word true and none of it the thing. And here's Vee receiving it as gospel, envying the lit-up out-loud life, not knowing the woman who sent it was lying face-down-cold next to the proof that Vee wins. "Every word of it true. None of it the thing." The two of them are both alone and both cold and both reaching for the same unnameable thing and neither can say it — and the book let me see both sides of the glass. That's the kind of thing I stay for.

What I'm still wary of, and I want to say it plainly so I don't get swept: this is a gorgeously constructed trap, and I can *feel* the construction. Both of them lying cold, both deciding Pace is the realer thing — that's the machine tightening, Vee ripening for exactly "the next door." The blurb promised she'd be driven back through, riper than ever, and this chapter is the engine of that. But — and this is why I'm not mad — the book is not winking at me here. It's not telling me her ache is pretty. It's letting her be scared and small and wrong about the blonde and honest that the bartender's reassurances "went straight through her hands" because "now she did not know she was anything." That last part is the true thing and the book knows it's true: Pace's whole silence is what's leaving her with no floor. The chapter holds *with* her uncertainty instead of papering it.

What I noticed and loved: she's the one who cut her own induction photo off at the waist for her mother's phone — "she was the one who had divided it." Same picture, one house, she did the scissoring. That's her doing the cropping-down-to-what-fits-the-frame to herself now, consciously, and she sees it. That's not a girl being acted upon. That's a girl narrating her own compromises with her eyes open.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: "they're BOTH lying in cold beds on NYE both deciding the math man is the real thing and neither of them knows the other one knows and I want to scream." And then: "Vee invented a hot blonde to torture herself with and he's probably cooking alone thinking about her, I will not survive this book."

Where's the gap? It's been — what — two chapters now with no Pace in a room with her, and this whole chapter is about the gap, so it's not costing me, it's *paying* me. The absence is the subject. That's the version of apart I come back for.
