I liked being here. That's the thing I want to say first: I liked the small room tonight the way Vee did, missed the draft the way she did.

But I read this one with my eyes a little narrowed, and I think that's the book's own fault for teaching me to. The sweater. She lied — the cranberry sweater shrunk to doll size, the dye ran, that's why she was in his t-shirt. And Cassie, my Cassie, who hears the size of things, says it flat back at her — "Pace. Shrank your sweater." — and then laughs and lets it stand. I know what that laugh is. It's Cassie choosing not to pull the seam, same as always, except tonight it's not hurt feelings she's stepping around, it's a fib about a coat with nothing under it, and she clocks it and loves Vee enough to let the lie be the record. I didn't hate it. I almost loved it, honestly — it's the truest thing about those two, that Cassie will hold a door for Vee's dignity and ask for nothing. But it's also Vee learning to narrate herself in cropped versions to the people who love her, same as she scissored the leg off the photo for her mother, and the book keeps showing me her getting fluent at it. I notice. I'm keeping the count.

The thing that actually warmed me, clean, no asterisk: the card. Cassie's mother says thank you for the card, wants to know when she gets to see Vee again, and it goes through Vee like a hand cupped at the back of her neck. That's a love with no machine behind it. Vee has a whole other tether in this book — a plain, un-schemed, Ohio-adjacent, boxes-in-a-closet kind of belonging — and the book keeps it alive in the corner, and every time it shows up I breathe easier, because it means Pace and Randi don't own the whole board. The snowshoes in the house, the Play-Doh cookies, the baby who is a baby — that's a real family being funny, and Vee gets to want it a little, and I got to want it with her.

And the hand on the cold cinderblock at the end. Two nights of all that acreage and his heat at her back, and now one girl wide and the wall a hand's width off. She left her hand there. That's the gap I feed on, and the book knows it's the gap, and it's not even sad about it — he's west of town in the wide bed, hers again, Friday will come. For once the door that closed was a Sunday-night door and not a wound. I'll take it.

What I'm sitting with, uneasy: she's going to tell Randi everything. Nearly everything. She's going to hold back the tasting thing — the thing from the other night that settled in minor-key and wouldn't go flat — and find a way to say it that doesn't sound like what it sounds like, and let Randi tell her what to make of it. And I thought: no, Vee. Don't hand *that* one across the cloth too. That's the exact reflex that got her here — the harvester gets the uncropped length every time. The one question that's actually hers to sit with alone, she's already planning to outsource to the woman who, I happen to know, chose her off a lawn. So this quiet chapter left me more worried than the loud ones did, which is a strange trick to pull with mushrooms on toast.
