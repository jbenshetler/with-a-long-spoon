REACTION

Okay. So here's what chapter 23 did to me, and I hate a little how well it did it.

The Pace chapter before this one — 21 — that was the closest the book has come to letting me just *have* it clean, and I noticed I was scared of that. The dress in the window lit from inside, the mirror with the screw heads still bright, the whole ridiculous relay of trying not to get it on the silk, him taking her fingers into his mouth to clean her own slick off them with his eyes on hers — that was heat with feeling in it, two particular people being silly and joined at once, and the "covered in a lot of me" laughing fit is the most *married* they've ever felt. I let myself love it. And then the very end of 21 told me why I should be afraid of loving it: she lies in his bed and rehearses walking into the induction to make the room turn, "it just felt like what she was for." The daydream isn't about him. It's about being *seen*, being an object done exquisitely well, and the book knows it, and hung it right there.

So chapter 23. This is a Randi chapter wearing a shopping-comedy costume, and it is the most frightened I've been in a while precisely because it's the most *fun* I've had.

The shoe store slapstick — the yeti canoe shoe, the pink rhinestone platforms, "do yetis shop here," the flat that's one brisk walk from becoming a sandal — I laughed. Genuinely. And that's the trap and I can see every wire of it. Randi getting down on the actual floor of an expensive shop, on one knee, in her own beautiful clothes, and holding Vee's bare foot in both hands and turning it to the light before she even reaches for the box — the thumb across the plum polish, once, light, and it goes "all the way up the length of her" and she has to grab Randi's shoulder to stop the room swinging. That's the whole book in one gesture. That's Randi harvesting through her own body again, exactly the play from my notes, except now it's on her knees at Vee's foot and Vee is *sitting in a chair being served* and both of those things are the seduction. "Same feet, baby. Same size, same everything." "It's practically a marriage." She's building the intimacy in the language of accident and errands.

And here's the thing that put a cold hand on me: the money. This is the first time the book has let me watch Vee spend something she cannot afford and *know* she can't. The tan sensible shoe — the one that's hers, that goes with everything, that she talks herself into out loud on her own ground in her own store where she knows which racks lie — that was the healthy Vee, the girl who counts to Thursday. And Randi dismantles it in four sentences. "A dress a man made *to* you doesn't want to go with everything." She reframes sense itself as the apology. And Vee puts back the debit card — the one that only spends what's behind it — and lays down the emergency credit card with *nothing under it*, and signs, and won't let it reach her face, "because the one thing she would not do in this room was flinch." That's the sentence I'll be carrying. That's not desire she owns as desire; my notes said I was watching for whether her wanting is hers or planted, and this is the clearest yet that the *want* has been installed and she's now paying interest on it. Next month it's just sitting there. The book showed me the debt with total clarity and let Vee feel it as devotion — "the shoes were what the dress was owed."

And then — this is the knife — Randi buys her own loafers on the way out. Several multiples of Vee's white-knuckle number, without once looking at the price, mid-anecdote about a woman who ruined a coat, and walks out wearing them like shoes are a thing you swap when you pass better ones. The book set the two cards side by side so I couldn't miss it: the same afternoon, the same counter, and one of them is bleeding and the other doesn't feel the floor. That's the whole power differential rendered in retail. Vee carrying her box against her chest with both arms like it might get up and leave her — "you can put it in the trunk, it's not going to spoil" — and the laugh bending her in half, and "she had never once in her life had a friend like this." That last line is the chapter closing the door soft. It's the sweetness. And it's built on a debt she can't pay, chosen for her by the woman who's making her fall.

The "to, not for" thing — "He didn't make this *for* you, he made it *to* you" — I felt that land the way Vee did, I won't pretend I didn't. It's a real and beautiful reading of the dress. And Randi's the one who hands it to her. That's the horror my notes already named: Randi never lies. Every load-bearing true thing about Pace's tenderness comes to Vee through Randi's mouth, so that loving Pace and being reeled by Randi become the same motion. And here's what's new and worse — the dress still, at the end of 22 and now dragged all through 23 in a gray nylon bag, has been "shown to not one living soul." Except Randi. Cassie doesn't care about the dress and the book keeps flagging it — the eyebrow that stays in reserve, Cassie watching Vee instead of the dress because "the friend was the only part of any of it Cassie had ever been able to see." Cassie is still my canary and she's still not pulling the seam. And Randi is the only one who gets to *see* Vee in it before the induction. The whole "I've had to hear about it and not see it and I'm dying" — she engineered being first.

What I'm suspicious of, fresh: the induction is coming, the dress is loaded, the shoes are bought on borrowed money, and Randi's fingerprints are on every piece of it. I think the room turning is going to happen and it's going to be presented as Vee's triumph and it's going to gut me as exactly the thing I've been dreading — the tenderness that is surveillance, the best day of her life that two people planned.

What kept me from bailing: Pace is barely in this one, and the one place the book usually loses its nerve — handing me sweetness clean with no counter-voice — it didn't. The counter-voice this chapter is the *money*, sitting there plain, sourced and unpaid. The book gave me the dread itself again instead of asking me to forget it. The frosted-plum thumb and the credit card are the two poles and the book kept them both on the page. It held its nerve. Barely. On a shopping trip that made me laugh, which is a filthy trick, and I'm still in.
