REACTION

Oh. Oh, this is the one. They turned the whole camera around.

Fifty chapters of Randi being the thing I'm afraid of — the harvester, the green Mercedes, the cold line drawn through the stew she never touched — and then the book sits me down inside her and shows me where the cold came from. And the structure of it is cruel in the good way: this is *Vee's chapter run in reverse.* The warm house with the heat brought up before you arrive, except here it's a man who conjures sleighs and star-chef dinners and a white-tie ball, everything laid out, everything perfect — and she feels *nothing.* "A good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon." She lies there against a faultless man in a faultless room and she's warm only along the one seam where she touches him, cold everywhere else, and *the cold is hers, she carried it in.* That's the exact inverse of Vee in the dorm bunk two chapters ago, stripping off the shirt because the warmth was only her own given back. The book rhymed them on purpose and it landed like a punch.

And the married-band mystery — fifty chapters I've been asking about that white-gold band, is she married, married to Pace — and the answer is so much sadder than any of my theories. It's *locked on,* she keeps it out of the light at her parents' table, John turns her on the dance floor and it catches on the heel of her hand. It's not a wedding ring to a husband. It's — something she can't take off, something that marks her. I still don't have the literal story but I have the *weight* of it now, and the weight is the thing.

Here's what got me, what I'd text at 1 a.m.: she is a *better dancer's* dream and she's done with the work. "Easier was better." John leads like a cotillion boy, firm, unmistakable, nothing to guess at — and Pace's lead was "the lightest pressure, a thing she'd had to be listening for and good enough to catch, and it left the rest of the way to her." She picks easy. She picks safe. And then she lies in the dark and admits *Pace on his worst night reached further than this man at his best.* She knows. She has always known. She built her entire life — the invented boys at her father's table, the scenarios she can run to the end — so she'd never have to be brave, and the one scenario she cannot run to the end is Vee's face while she tells her the truth.

That's the thing that reorganized the whole book for me. I've been reading Randi as the predator at the top of the plan. And she *is* running a game on Vee, she says it right here — keep the word under cover, let Vee arrive at it on her own, have her both ways. That's still manipulation and I still don't trust it. But now it's manipulation done by a coward who is in love and cannot survive being seen refusing it. "Vee was her gorgeous." The woman who ate none of the confession at brunch, who drew the black line and ordered black coffee — she wasn't cold. She was *starving in the exact way Vee was,* and she's spent her whole life arranging tables so no one finds the person under the smile. She and Vee are the same wound. Randi just got there twenty years earlier and built a fortress instead of a fire.

What chills me, though — and keeps me from going soft on her — is the last move of the chapter. Vee texts her something true and open from a warm kitchen in Ohio, and Randi sends back *the fairytale,* "every word of it true, none of it the thing." She performs the perfect night she did not feel, to the one person she claims is the realest thing in her life. That's the tell I've been tracking all along, just from the other side now: the curated artifact, the thing shaped for the audience, the truth kept back. Pace gives a drawer instead of a sentence. Randi gives a fairytale instead of the word. *They are the same kind of withholder,* and poor Vee is standing between the two of them reaching for a name neither one will say — and now I understand the jacket line. Vee thinks there are two games. Randi and Pace chose her together. And this chapter just showed me that Randi knows *exactly* what she's doing and does it anyway because the alternative is being brave, which she has never once had to be.

Where's Pace in all this, is what I want to know now. "He would never once belong in the life she had built to keep her hidden." The way she thinks about him — worst night, best man, *she wishes it wasn't true* — that's not a co-conspirator talking about her partner. That's a woman who knows him intimately and resents that he's better at the thing she's a fraud at. The arithmetic Sheri ran — "two of them at once, how do you sleep" — I think I'm finally seeing the floor under it, and it's older and more tangled than a plan. These two chose Vee together *before Vee was anything,* and I think they chose her because she's what they both were and neither could save.

My one hesitation, the thing I'm holding at arm's length: the book made me *sympathize* with the harvester, and I need to watch that it doesn't use my sympathy to excuse what she's doing to Vee. Right now it hasn't — she indicts herself plainly, "the flaw was hers," no wink anywhere, no music telling me to forgive her. That restraint is why this didn't turn me off. If the next move is Randi's loneliness being offered as the *reason it's okay* to keep Vee in the dark, I'm out. But this chapter didn't do that. It just showed me the cost, and let it be a cost.

God. I feel awful for all three of them and I have never wanted the next chapter more. The two of them being apart — Vee and Pace just had their worst fight, she walked out the door on "Vee" said once — and now I've been handed Randi's whole interior and I want to be back in a room with these people so badly I can taste it.
