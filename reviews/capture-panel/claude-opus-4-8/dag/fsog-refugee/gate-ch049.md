REACTION

Oh, she did it. She finally did it. She stood on his mat with her coat buttoned to her throat and the bag left out in the car — she left the bag in the car, that's the whole chapter right there, she came not planning to stay — and she asked the question out loud in the one room where she's never once been able to. And I have been waiting for this since the pool, since "is he that good or did I make him that good." She finally took the doubt that lived inside her chest and put it in the air between them where he'd have to answer it.

And what I love, what actually made me sit up, is that she didn't let him win on points. He tried the move — the only move he's got — "Do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have than this one?" and she *saw* it. "That's not what I asked." "I'm not asking about anyone else's. I'm asking about me." God. That's the clearest she's ever been. For forty-eight chapters this book has handed him every out and let him take it with a drawer or a dropped palm instead of a sentence, and here's Vee finally refusing the substitution. Refusing the drawer. She wants the word and she names exactly why the word matters — not for her, for *out there*, for Cassie and her father and her mother, for the self she has to carry around in daylight that currently has no name on it.

"Words are cheap," he says. And she throws it right back: "Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me." I actually said *yes* out loud. Because that's it, isn't it — that's the thing the whole book has been dressing up as depth. The not-saying has been sold to me as his profundity, his exactness, a man too honest for easy words. And she just called it what it is: a man who hoards the one cheap thing because spending it would cost him something he won't pay. If words are so cheap, give her one.

And then — the thing that chilled me — he *folds his arms.* He steps back and folds his arms and holds the line "at cost" and says nothing. And the book, for once, does not rush in to tell me his silence is love. It just lets him stand there withholding. And when she cries he reaches for her with the hands, "not the hand in place of the word," and she finally — FINALLY — refuses the hand. She gets the door open. He says her name. "Just her name, and her name was not the word she'd come for." I could have wept. She walked out. She walked out into the cold that's worse, and she did not go back in.

Here's what this did to me that no chapter has done yet: the counter-voice isn't in the next room or in Cassie's dry mouth anymore. It's *Vee's.* She is her own Cassie now. The doubt I've been carrying for her, she's carrying herself, and she acted on it. That's the opposite of the vanishing I've been grieving. This is the first chapter where she's bigger walking out than she was walking in.

What I'm suspicious of — and I can't not be, this book has trained me — is that this is the Fifty Shades beat. She walks out at the end of book two the way I walked out at the end of book one and bought the second before I slept. The jacket told me: "she has moved the two who arranged her spring as surely as they have opened her." Is this her moving him? Or is this the engineered rupture, the thing Randi and Pace counted on, the door that closes so she comes back through it harder? Randi's still out there with her PPP fraud and her ragged "cheating bastards," and I still don't have the third game cashed. But I don't care right this second. Right this second a woman I've been scared for asked for the one thing she's owed and refused every substitute when he wouldn't give it, and left. That's my girl. That's the exit I wanted for her, even if the book means it as a cliffhanger.

My exit line — the day it asks me to be happy for her vanishing with no counter-voice anywhere — this is the furthest thing from it. This chapter IS the counter-voice, and it's hers.
