REACTION

Oh, this one got me, and I'm a little annoyed about how easily.

First sustained time I've been let all the way inside him, and the book knew exactly what it was doing withholding it until now. Him hungover on his back, tongue stuck to the roof of his mouth, the mop and bucket in the hall with the crust of foam on it — he got so drunk he was sick and then cleaned it up before he passed out, because of course he did, because even wrecked he puts things back to true. *Whiskey River was a liar.* He drank to blur her and it left every memory of her intact. That's the cruelest little detail and it's aimed at himself, which is new.

And the bowl. The vacuum-sealed chicken and broccoli, "everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted." The man who cooks her those symphonies eats prison food alone. That undid something in me. He builds meals as love and feeds himself fuel. The woman years ago who laughed and called it prison food and then drifted off "with no cruelty on either side and no part of the world altered by it" — that line is the whole thesis of his terror. Nothing altered. He's spent his life making sure nothing alters him again.

Daphne. So there it is. The tutor, older, showed him where to stand when everyone else was born knowing — the Ohio-shaped hole, the boy who could do the math but not the world. First everything, then gone, and *there had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone, because Daphne was the person he would have told.* God. And the lesson he drew from it — that the words were the difference, that saying "I love you" was what turned a loss into a thing that eats your life — so he simply never said it again and stayed survivable. That's not a strategy, that's a scar walking around in a man's body. He's not withholding the word to control her. He's withholding it because he thinks the word is a live wire and he already got electrocuted once.

Here's what I keep turning over, though, at 1 a.m.: this is the most sympathetic he's ever been AND the most exposed the machinery has ever been. Because I still can't forget the jacket, and the year-long play, and Randi. Is Daphne real, or is Daphne the thing that made him buildable? He didn't choose this either — he's "the perfect version of a thing someone built him into," and now I see the load-bearing wall they built on. That doesn't make me trust the game. It makes me sad for two people instead of one.

And the truck. The engine on the highway, his whole body gathering toward the gravel and the turn, the one second the house hands him Vee's face becoming the smile she gives only him — and it goes past. And he bends over the table and sobs. I believed it. That's the thing. I've been waiting all book to know if the two-who-started-it would get as real as the jacket promised, and this is the book cashing that check for him. He is not performing this. There's no one here to perform for.

What I notice about my own exit line: I said the day it touches her sweetly with the dread scrubbed out, I'm gone. This isn't her — it's him — but I clocked something. The dread in this chapter isn't laid in by the narrator against a warm moment. The dread IS the warm moment. His self-knowledge is the counter-voice, and it's *his own*. "He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him. He had believed that not saying it would make this hurt less." He said the quiet part. He knows he was weak. A man who can name his own cowardice this exactly is not a man dressing cruelty as dominance — he's a coward about the one thing, and honest about the cowardice, and that combination is doing something to me I didn't expect.

The ending. "Pace picked up his fork. He was not hungry. He ate anyway." Same as her, chewing the dead nachos two chapters back. They're both eating things that mean nothing, apart, on purpose, out of some grim discipline. The book rhymed them and I felt it in my teeth.

I'm in. I want to know if he says it. I want to know whether Daphne was hers to leave or whether someone handed her to him too.
