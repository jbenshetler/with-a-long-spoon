REACTION

Oh. *Oh.* They finally let me inside him, and it wasn't an excuse — it was a confession.

I walked into this chapter with my arms folded the exact way his were on that mat, ready to not be bought. And the book didn't try to buy me. It did the braver thing: it showed me the man alone, hungover to the bone, mopping up his own sick with bleach the night after she left, eating chicken and broccoli out of a vacuum bag because his body "had used what it had and needed more" — and it never once asked me to find that romantic. It just let it be what it was. A grown man wrecked and feeding himself anyway because that's what you do. *He ate anyway.* God.

And the truck. The truck went past and his whole body gathered toward the cut in the hill, toward gravel, toward her car finding the drive and her face becoming "the smile she gave only him" — and it *wasn't her,* and he bent over the table and sobbed. That's the moment I've been waiting for since chapter 49 without knowing I was waiting for it. Because here's the thing: I named the withholding as hoarding, as cowardice, as keeping the one cheap thing because spending it would cost him. And the book just — agreed with me. Out loud. In his own head. *"He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him."* He said it plainer than I could have. He indicted himself with the exact charge Vee leveled on the mat. The two breaths on the porch, the "I love how good we are together" — he tells me he felt the true answer rise, whole and ready, and *chose the smaller truth,* knew it would fail her while he was saying it, and let it stand because he was weak at the one moment she asked him to be brave. That's not a man the book is protecting. That's a man the book is grieving.

Daphne. Finally. So that's the scar — not a wound he's precious about, a lesson he drew the wrong conclusion from. He said it once, meant it, she left, it "consumed his life," and he taught himself the *words* were the difference — that if he never spends them again, loss stays survivable. "I don't go back." It all rhymes now. The parents, the scar, the folded arms, the not-saying: one engine, and the engine is terror wearing the costume of discipline. I spent fifty chapters wondering if the control was depth or defense and the book just handed me the answer with no wink: it's defense. He built a superstition out of one grief and has been starving her on it.

What I love — what actually moved me — is that knowing all this does NOT make me forgive him *for Vee.* The diagnosis isn't an absolution. He still did it to her. She still drove home in the cold and got nothing. But I'm no longer being asked to call his silence mysterious or masterful; I'm being shown it's a flinch, and the man himself hates it. That's the book grieving beside me again instead of winking, and it's the thing keeping me in these pages.

Two things I'm still holding at a distance. One: this whole chapter is Pace's pain, beautifully rendered, and it's the *second* chapter running where Vee and Pace have not been in a room — three now, counting her twin bed on New Year's. I feel that gap. The ache is real but it's two solitary aches pointed at each other across a county, and I want them in a *room.* Two — and this is the splinter the carry-forward won't let me pull out — I keep clocking Randi's cashmere blanket sliding off the foot of *his* bed, the one "for the chill that took her after." He's not just Vee's. He and Randi have their own after-sex domesticity, her blanket living on his bed. And he's crying over Vee while Randi's cashmere warms his feet. The third game Vee doesn't know about is sitting right there in the folds of a blanket and the book knows it even if he's too hungover to think it.

But right now, this hour, I believe his grief and I want him to drive to her. Say the word, you coward. You already said it to me.
