REACTION

Okay, first: the dance. God, the dance. This is the thing I've been hunting the whole genre for, and Rivers just handed it to me on a cold concrete pad under field lights. "Give me something to push back against. Otherwise you cannot feel my lead." He's teaching her that the frame isn't a cage, it's *information* — that she has a job, that her pushing back is what makes them move together, that "being jelly doesn't work." A man whose control literally requires her to be present, to have weight, to *answer*. And then the payoff on the floor: "the frame she'd fought on the pad as too close, too fixed, too much rule, was the thing doing it now: it held her in the one place the next step could come from, so there was nothing to do but take it." That is dominance making room for her instead of erasing her, rendered as a literal body-mechanic. I felt it in my own spine. This is the cleanest statement of my whole longing I've gotten in this book and maybe in years.

And the little knife she slips me right in the middle of the bliss — "how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd been building out of wanting him, wish laid over man so neatly she couldn't find the join." The book let her *see* it. For one second she looked straight at the machine — is he real or am I building him — and then "the floor took it." That's the nerve holding. It won't let her keep the sight, but it showed me she's capable of it.

Then the spiral. And here's where my stomach went cold in a way I need to sit with. The blonde comes off the rail, plants the poison — "gets a girl to do just what he wants," "gets a girl home safe" — with something under the smile "that wasn't." I don't for one second believe that was spontaneous. That was staged, or if not staged then it's exactly the kind of live ammunition Randi's whole operation is built to exploit. And it works instantly: Vee, who's carried shame in her body since girlhood, converts it straight to venom and does the one thing she does when she's scared — she attacks the good thing. "Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe from here, too?" I actually winced. Not because it's out of character but because it's *so* in character, and because someone has been studying that character for a year.

But — and this is the part I keep turning over — the book did NOT wink at me here. It didn't make her cruelty romantic. It let her be genuinely ugly to a genuinely good man, and it let the bartender vindicate him flatly: he leaves them at the door, doesn't take advantage, "wasn't him they were jealous of, honey." So the emotional verdict is real: she was wrong, he's good, she wounded him. That's honest.

What scares me is the *last beat*. She won't call Cassie — the one person who'd come with no agenda, because Cassie would ask questions and Vee "would have to put words to what she'd done." So she calls a cab into the smelly dark, spends money meant for other things, and by morning she knows what she's going to do: go to *Randi's*. Run to the manipulator to be coached back into the arms of the other manipulator. That's the noose. The chapter that most purely gave me the thing I want ends by tightening the trap, and it did it by *isolating her from the one clean witness*. Cassie's slow even breathing across the dark room — the canary, right there, and Vee eases the door shut and won't wake her.

So I'm not being asked to root for the trap yet. I'm being made to grieve it — watching her cut the last honest line and reach for the poisoned one, believing it's repair. That's still the deal I signed. But I felt the floor of my trust move tonight. If the next chapter is Randi "fixing" this and I'm asked to feel relief instead of dread — that's my exit. Right now I feel dread, and the dread is the book keeping faith with me.

The Peter/Daphne stuff from last chapter is sitting under all of this too — a boy handed to a nineteen-year-old at fifteen and taught to call it luck, naming the thing that was done to him as the best two years of his life. He *is* the machine because the machine was run on him first. That's the only frame in which any of this stays bearable, and this chapter's dance — the tenderness of it — is that boy's whole ruined gift turned toward her. I'm gutted and I'm hooked.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *the country dancing scene is the most turned-on I've been by consent in my LIFE and then she torched it and is running to the WRONG WOMAN to fix it and I want to reach into the book and shake her.*
