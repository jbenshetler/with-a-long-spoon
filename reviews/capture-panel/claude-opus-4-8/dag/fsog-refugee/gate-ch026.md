REACTION

Oh, this one had me. The dance lesson is exactly the thing — I've hunted for this for years and here it is rendered as literal choreography. "If you want to, I'll teach you." Close the space, don't hold yourself out, give me something to push back against so you can feel the lead. That's the whole fantasy made physical: his control isn't erasing her, it's the frame that holds her in the one place the next step can come from, so all she has to do is stop guessing and let it arrive. "There it is," he said, quiet. I felt that in my chest. A man teaching a woman to stop bracing so she can be moved — and it works *because* he's exact, not because he's forceful. God.

And then the book did the thing I did not expect. It handed the cruelty to *her*. Every jealousy-misunderstanding chapter I've ever read, the man is the one who's careless or the hero has to grovel — here Vee is the one who reaches in and stabs a good man with the truest, kindest things about him. "Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe?" I heard the glass go past catching, same as she did. And he answered straight, and set the untouched beer on the bar, and walked out into the cold. He didn't fight. He didn't punish her. He just went still and left. I hated it and I believed every second of it.

The blonde, though. That's the tell I'm chewing on at 1 a.m. The makeup "doing a lot of work," the "knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants," "he's so good about getting a girl home safe." That poison was *aimed*. Somebody wanted Vee to spiral, and Vee obligingly did. And the bartender — older, economical, done being asked for things — dismantles it clean: he doesn't bring dates, he brought one on a Thursday, he leaves them at the door, *"wasn't him they were jealous of, honey."* That reframe gutted me a little. She spent the night being handled by her own shame and a stranger's jealousy, and the truth was that she was the envied one the whole time.

But here's the thing sitting cold in my stomach. She lashes out, she's wrong, she's ashamed — and her resolution isn't to call Pace, or even to wake Cassie, who is three feet away and would sit up for this. It's *go to Randi's.* "The how was a separate problem." She wounded him, and her instinct is to bring the wound to the one person who is running a play on her. That's the horror doing its quiet work again — she doesn't go to the man or to her canary, she goes to the handler. And the book knows it; it made a point of Cassie three feet off and unwoken, twice.

Also — the shame flared ugly under stress. "Spread her legs like a slut." The whole burgundy-dress liberation of the last chapters, the standing up straight, the shame that "ran the whole length and never closed her" — one bad night and the girlhood voice is right back in her mouth, calling herself a floozy. That felt real and it scared me, how thin the new floor is.

What keeps me here: the counter-voice held. This wasn't sweetness handed clean — it was Vee sabotaging the best thing she has and the book letting me watch her do it with my eyes open. Pace stayed a good man on the page, genuinely, and that's still the trap's whole engine: the better he is, the worse the thing being built around him. And the pull toward the next chapter is pure dread — because I know what "going to Randi's to figure out how to fix it" means. Randi is going to receive this, and she is going to hand Vee her own remorse back polished into something that pulls her a step further in. I want to watch it and I don't want it to happen.
