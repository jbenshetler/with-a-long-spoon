REACTION

Okay. I did not expect this. I opened this thinking I was meeting Vee — the jacket's whole pitch is the girl waking up to her own appetite — and instead I got dropped into a warm overheated little house with Steely Dan going and a man slicing a honeycrisp thin because he's *noticed* she eats the thin slices faster. And I thought, fine, this is Vee and Pace. And then the last page turned the lights on. *There you are, Randi.* It's not Vee. It's the two of them — the architects, the ones running the game on the girl who doesn't know there's a game. And one of them just got caught in her own machine.

That's a hell of a move and I sat with it for a second after I finished. The recruiter — the glamorous friend who's supposed to be *drawing Vee open* — is the one who ends up facedown and begging in a voice she hasn't used since she was a child, crying onto the leather without knowing she's doing it, and then locking herself in the bathroom to decide she is never, ever going to let that happen again. She's going to go back behind the face. The con artist is the one who got conned by her own body, and she's terrified of it. "The size of a key turning in a lock." God.

And here's the thing that's got its hooks in me: the consent in this is *exactly* what I left the whole genre looking for. He asks. He establishes her word and then tells her to use it anytime, no apology owed, and *means* it. He checks her back, her shoulders, her hands. And — this is the part that actually got me — when she locks the bathroom door, he doesn't knock. He sits on the bed with his elbows on his knees and lets the door be hers, because he learned the hard way with someone named Daphne that a door you can hear through is still a door. A man whose dominance makes room instead of erasing. The spanking scene is genuinely hot — the edging, the taking-his-time, the "I'm not stopping, I'm taking my time," the way he builds a need in her that can't be bargained down. Rendered from inside, the *please that came from below* versus the please she'd use on a waiter. That distinction is so precise it almost hurt.

But I'm not comfortable, and I don't think I'm supposed to be. Because all this exquisite care, this man who's so good at finding the real person under the performance — he and this woman together *chose* a 21-year-old and are running this on her. The tenderness is real AND it's the instrument. "Psychologically merciless in its tenderness" — yeah. I believe them now. What's keeping me here instead of chilling me out of it is that the book clearly *knows* it's a knife. It's not winking that the plan is cute. Randi getting hollowed out on that bench is the book promising me this thing is going to turn on the people who started it. That I want to watch.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *the villain is the one who fell and she's going to pretend she didn't and I am not okay.*
