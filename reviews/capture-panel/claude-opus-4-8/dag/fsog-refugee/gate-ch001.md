REACTION

Okay, so I have to text someone about this, because the book just pulled a thing on me I did not expect. I opened this thinking I'd be meeting Vee — the girl on the jacket, the one being walked into Pace's path. And instead I'm in a warm overheated little house with cheap laminate counters and handmade walnut furniture, watching *Randi* lock the deadbolt behind herself and climb onto a man's lap and eat apple slices off his mouth. The architect. One of the two people with the plan. I'm in the seducers' bedroom before I've even met the mark. That's a hell of a way to open a book, and I sat up.

And here's the part that's got me unsettled in the good way and the bad way at once: the *man is exactly what I came back to this genre hoping to find.* He asks. He waits for the answer. The safeword gets taken, twice, in a soft voice — "tell me anytime," "I won't be upset." He turns the chair when she circles her finger. He takes her hands out of his hair not to control her but so her hands are *empty for her own choosing.* He slices the apple thin because she won't eat it thick and cut-side-up because she eats faster that way — he's reading her starved little relationship with food and feeding her anyway, gently, around the edges of it. The whole bench scene is the slowest, most watched, most *present* thing I've read in ages, and the slowness is the point: he builds a need in her that can't be bargained down and then he will not let her perform her way out of it. God help me, it worked on me. The three-pleases, the *please* that finally "came from below," the full-body shudder that "was not anything she could have produced" — I felt that land. HEAT all the way up.

But. BUT. The reason she breaks isn't pleasure. It's being *seen* — he looks past the famous blue eyes and the practiced surprise and finds the actual girl hiding behind the face she's been running since she was thirteen, and she comes out, and she *hates* it. And then — the gut-punch — she locks the bathroom door (the first time she's ever done that here), looks in the mirror, and quietly, calmly, decides: *he can have the face. Not ever. The one he found is going back behind it and she is not coming out again.* And then she walks out and gives him the lying smile and says yes, she'll stay. The bottle cap pressed under her thumbnail into the nail bed where it hurts — I caught that, I wasn't supposed to miss it, and I didn't.

So the thing the jacket promised — "as true for the two who started it" — is already here, and it's not romantic, it's *tragic.* He fell in and doesn't know she's fleeing. He's being genuinely tender to a woman who's decided tenderness is the one thing she cannot survive. And underneath all of it is the fact that this exquisite careful seeing is also the exact apparatus they're going to point at some other girl named Vee who doesn't know there's a plan. The care is real AND it's a weapon. That's the dread. That's "every yes was freely given, that was the problem." The book is not winking at me — it's showing me Randi's damage with a cold, clear, merciless eye, and that's the only reason I'm not out. If it had played her retreat as coy, or played the plan as sexy mischief, I'd have closed it. It didn't. It played it as a wound.

What I want now is Vee. I want to see the machine from the outside before I forget it has teeth.
