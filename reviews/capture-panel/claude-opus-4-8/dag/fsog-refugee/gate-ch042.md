Oh. Oh, this is the one. This is the exact thing I said I was waiting for and the exact thing I was afraid of, delivered in the same breath, which is what this book always does to me.

Because look at what she did. She built a sentence. For weeks. She couldn't say *I love you* — not out of shyness, but because there's a *rule*, and she found the rule by "bumping against it from the inside," which is the most quietly devastating line about him in the whole book. He said it first is the law and he never said it, so she engineered a whole poem to carry the meaning past the rule without breaking it. "I had been eating crackers my whole life, and you fed me a meal." That's beautiful. That's *hers*, made the way he makes things, from nothing, checked carefully, load-bearing. She handed it to him in the cold pre-dawn with the moon coming up. That is a woman giving a man her whole chest.

And he — took two breaths. The book let me hear the two breaths, the hand that started two responses and put both down. And then he said "I love how good we are together." And I felt the floor go. Because that is not the sentence back. That's a sentence about the *machine* of them, the performance, how well the parts fit. She gave him a meal and he complimented the table setting. "What you do — what your body does" — the body, again, always the body with a slight distance on it, "I'm glad you feel it too." Glad *you* feel it. Not: I feel it about *you*.

And here's what wrecks me and keeps me honest: I can't even fully swear he did it on purpose. The book won't let me. "Maybe he understood and chose to say it like that. Maybe it had gone past him entirely." She can't test it because testing it means saying the words and the words go through him first — she's caught in the exact machine they built her into, and the machine has no exit that doesn't require the thing she's forbidden. That's merciless. That's the "psychologically merciless in its tenderness" the jacket bragged about, and I hate that it's true.

The laundry room. God. Two days later, folding her clothes, and her body does "a small thing it did not have a name for" before her mind arrives — same move as always, but this time it's not desire arriving early, it's *grief* arriving early. And she does the thing I've watched her learn to do to other people and now she does it to herself: she takes the sentence, puts it in "the place where she put things she did not know what to do with," and — "The place was not empty." That line. The pile of small repairs she's had to do all fall to keep him functioning as the man she loves. She doesn't look at the pile. She retires her best sentence back into her body and vows never to say it again. She *shrinks the room herself.* The crackers-and-meal "would not return to her speech." She just taught herself a smaller vocabulary to keep loving him possible.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *she wrote him a love poem in code and he graded the code. and then she apologized to herself for asking.*

Now — my exit line. "Both." — the two of them are rhyming: the girl who can't name what he is, and the girl who can't say what she feels. The alarm's still not ringing but the whole house smells like smoke now.
