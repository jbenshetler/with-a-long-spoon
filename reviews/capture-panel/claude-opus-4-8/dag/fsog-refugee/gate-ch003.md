REACTION

Oh. *Oh.* I see what this book is doing now, and I'm cold and I can't look away.

Two chapters ago I thought I'd found him — the man I left the genre to find. Pace asks, Pace waits, Pace says *tell me your word* and *tell me anytime* and means it, Pace sits outside a locked bathroom door and refuses to open it because a closed door is hers. I was so ready to love him. And this chapter takes that exact care — the carefulness I came here for, the *he knew her body's answer, he did not take it for her answer* — and shows me what it's *for*. He's not asking so she can choose. He's asking so he can harvest a want out of her body while she's still shaking and undefended, watch her try to reroute it into something survivable, and then march her across a sunlit quad to go *shopping for a woman*. The pointing game. God. He points, she picks. And the whole thing is dressed as the best afternoon of her life, the burning maple and the frisbee and the wind in her ponytail, and underneath it two people are hunting.

And then: *Vee.* The name off the jacket. The girl I was told about on the back cover, the one who "walks toward him because everything in her has been waiting to" — she's the laughing red-haired girl who threw her head back and forgot, for the length of a laugh, that she could be seen. And I watched Randi body-check her on a brick path with three seconds of dance-trained precision so clean that *Vee* apologized. Vee said sorry for being hunted. I wanted to put my hand through the page.

Here's what's keeping me, and it's the thing that unsettles me most: the book is not winking. This is the part of the genre that usually makes me leave — coercion played as cute, cruelty with a smirk — and this book refuses the smirk. It knows exactly how predatory the pointing game is. It knows *Randi* is also prey, that something was taken out from under her on that bench and she's spent two chapters sealing it back behind the face. The line that gutted me wasn't even about Vee. It was Randi driving a bottle cap under her own thumbnail during aftercare while he strokes her hair and thinks the evening went beautifully. He doesn't see it. *He doesn't see it.* The man who sees everything.

I'm suspicious of Pace in a way I wasn't allowed to be before, and I'm grateful to the book for earning it honestly instead of asking me to call it romance. The warmth between him and Randi is real on the page and that's precisely what makes it sick — it's a real bond being used as a tool. I would text a friend at 1 a.m.: *he's perfect and that's the whole horror, start the series.*
