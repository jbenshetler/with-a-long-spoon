Okay. So I was braced after chapter two — that bathroom scene, the deadbolt going over in her, the decision to go back behind the face and never come out — I thought I understood the shape of this book. Pace is the kind, patient one, Randi's the one quietly shattering and hiding it, and I was leaning in for *her* story. And now chapter three flips the whole board over and I had to sit up.

Because here's the thing I'm turning over at 1 a.m.: the *jacket* told me there's a plan, a game between two people about a girl who doesn't know. And I've been reading two chapters thinking Randi was the mark. She's not. She's the hunter. That "Do you think she'll like the bench?" at the end made the hair on my arms stand up. Vee — the name from the back cover, the girl this whole thing is *about* — just got spotted across a quad and collided into on purpose with a decade of dance training weaponized into looking like an accident. And Randi's *glowing*. The girl who couldn't let herself be seen in chapter one is now the one doing the seeing, the acquiring. That's a genuinely great structural turn and I did not see it coming this fast.

But — and this is the but that's sitting heavy — I need to talk about Pace, because he's the reason I'm still here and also the reason I'm suddenly uneasy in a way I actually respect. The man asked the question *into her hair*, waited for the heat to leave the room, wanted to give her room to answer true. That's the consent-watching I came to this genre starving for. He keeps saying he doesn't take her body's answer for her answer. Lovely. Textbook of the thing I want.

Except. He's also *running* her. He clocks that she moved the wanting onto him — "a different owner" — and instead of that being a moment of *oh, she's scared, let me go gently,* it's a move in a game he's privately scoring. "The best idea he'd had in a month." He's reading her like the bench he built, every mechanism oiled and silent. And now the two of them are pointing at strangers on a quad and the book is *delighted* about it, and some reader somewhere is being asked to find a girl being hunted toward a dungeon bench charming. That's the wink. That's the thing that made me leave the mafia books. And the jacket *told me* it's coming — "fall for it exactly as she does."

Here's why I didn't stop, though, and it's narrow: the book isn't hiding it from me. It's not dressing coercion as romance and hoping I won't notice — it's showing me Randi's performance seam by seam, showing me she moved the appetite because wanting a woman herself was "unsurvivable," and letting me see that Pace *sees* it too. Nobody's being lied to except Vee, and I'm being told, flat out, to watch it happen. That's not the genre winking. That's the genre looking me dead in the eye. I'm suspicious as hell and I want to know if the book is going to make me complicit or make me grieve. Those are different books and I can't tell yet which one I'm in.

The pointing game itself — I'll be honest — was hot and clean as a scene. Her body arriving at "yes" before she could dress it, the bare word with nothing around it, the kiss with her own taste still on his mouth so she catches it mid-sentence. That's desire rendered from inside, which is exactly my drug. The problem is the "yes" is about a third person who hasn't consented to anything, and I can feel the book setting a table I may not want to eat at.

What's keeping me: I still don't know who Vee is, and I desperately want to, because the whole emotional weight of this is going to land on whether she's a real person to me or just prey. And the Randi-Pace dynamic has genuine two-people-who-don't-perform intimacy in it that I believe. I'm reading on, fast, but with my guard fully up.
