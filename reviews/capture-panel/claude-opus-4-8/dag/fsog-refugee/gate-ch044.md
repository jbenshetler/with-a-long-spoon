REACTION

Oh, this one's quiet and it gutted me anyway. Three chapters now of a woman lying awake next to — or across town from — the best thing in her life and not being able to name it, and this is the one where the shirt stops working.

That's the whole chapter, really. The shirt. Her one reliable trick — his smell, sawdust and salt and the clean warm of him, breathe it off her own shoulder and have all of him — and she finally clocks the thing I've been waiting for her to clock: *it was warm because she was warm. He wasn't in it.* She's been holding her own body heat given back and calling it him. God. That's the truest, saddest sentence in forty-four chapters, and it's about a laundry-worn flannel. She built a whole man out of a smell and a folder of things he does with his hands, and there's a hole in the middle of it where the word should be and the shirt can't fill it because the shirt was never him, it was her, wanting.

And then she takes it off and lies there bare in the cold and *stays in it.* That's new. That's the first thing she's done in ages that wasn't editing reality to keep herself comfortable — she chose the cold over the comforting lie. She usually reaches for the version that lets her rest inside a clean shape (she literally "forgave him" across the room in 43 because forgiveness was a shape she could wear). Here she won't. She drops his shirt in a heap, not folded, not back on the pillow — down where a dropped thing lands — and I felt that like a small mutiny. The girl who gave him the flannel to re-scent because she'd worn it to nothing is done pretending the cloth is a person.

Here's what's building across 42, 43, 44 and it's the thing keeping me in the book: the machinery finally has a cost *he* has to pay, and it's showing. "I love how good we are together." "I love how good we are together." She built her best sentence — crackers and a meal, weeks of assembling it — and pushed it into the air right up to the line of the word, and he answered with a sentence about *the thing between them*, not about *her*, and her own body did the small *wait* behind her breastbone before her mind caught up. The rule that he says it first — she "did not know the rule was a rule until she had bumped against it from the inside." That's the cage described from the inside better than any chapter yet. He's withholding the one word and she's rearranged her whole shape around not saying it ahead of him.

And I notice the book is NOT winking at me here. It's not selling his restraint as swoony. It's letting me sit in the laundry room with her going *the two things were not the same shape* and *I do not know what just happened* and refusing to resolve it, because the one person who could resolve it is the one she can't ask. That's exactly the wire I read for — the book showing me the trap, naming the silence at the center, not asking me to find it romantic.

What I'm suspicious of, still, always: is the withheld "I love you" real emotional truth (two wounded people who literally cannot say the word) or is it *technique* — the deliberate withholding that keeps her starving and pliable, the same architecture as building the hunger before he sets the plate down? I can't tell, and I don't think she can either, and the book knows I can't, and that's the whole horror. She even names it: whether his attention is showing her the truth or keeping her from it.

Cassie asleep below, window cracked its two inches, the canary breathing easy while the girl above her comes apart in the cold and bare. I keep waiting for Cassie to wake up at the wrong moment. Not yet.

I'm cold with her. I'm staying.
