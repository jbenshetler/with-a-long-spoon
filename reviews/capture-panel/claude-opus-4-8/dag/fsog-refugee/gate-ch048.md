Okay. Okay. This one got under my skin, and not in the good way, and I need to sort out which.

Here's what it did right, because it did do things right: she finally *said something*. Not the word, but close enough — "is this what we are now," the whole thing coming out at the wrong size for the room, a daytime voice in a room built for murmuring. I felt that. I've thrown that sentence. And him not answering, going into that same stillness he takes over a knot in the wood, and then handing her back a plain question — "where do you want to go tonight" — that's the thing I came to this genre for and almost never get. He didn't go cold. He didn't punish. He took the ugly loud thing she threw and set it down carefully. And then at the end, in bed, the control finally breaking, him taking her like he'd run clean out of the strength to wait — I wanted that too, she wanted that, she'd been *begging* for it in her own head, and the book gave it to her. The flannel-shirt energy of a man who listens. "I'm listening now." That's real and it's warm and if this were a different book I'd be swooning.

But here's what I actually noticed, and it's why my stomach is cold. She lied to win the fight. She said "you went cold on me after the Rusty Nail" and *the book tells me she knows it isn't true* — "some back room of her knew which of them had gone cold that night, knew who'd carried a pie up whose porch steps." She's rewriting her own history to keep the line of her grievance holding. And then — God — "she told herself the thing that let her go: that she had forgiven him." Forgiven him *for what.* He didn't do anything. She knows he didn't do anything. She's manufacturing a crime so she can perform absolution and have the night close into a clean shape she can rest inside. That's not a woman in love. That's a woman managing a wound she can't locate. The book KNOWS this. It's showing me her doing it with full clarity. That's the craft and that's the horror.

And the restaurant. The nachos lying dead on the plate. "He had ruined it for her, was the thing. Not just this." He's narrowed her world down to the one table she can taste at — his. Everything else is a billboard now. She used to have *fine*, and fine was plenty, and now fine is ash in her mouth and the only food that's food is the food he makes. That's not romance, that's a cage made of good cooking, and I've been watching it get built for forty-seven chapters and this is the one where she notices the walls and calls them her own choice.

The thing that actually scares me most, though — the end. "She belonged to him. She knew that the whole way through... But did he belong to her?" And the answer, laid out plain: he gave her his control, his body, every last thing but the one. She has nothing of him she could hold up to anyone. It lives only in the dark, in that bed, and stops at its edges. She can't name him to her mother. Can't lay him out for her father. She has no name for the best thing in her life. That's the whole book saying the quiet part: this man has given her *nothing she can carry out of that house.* A drawer. A shirt that smells like him. No word. And she's smaller every chapter.

What I keep waiting for isn't crossing. I notice: the counter-voice is STILL in the room. Her own body did the small *wait* after his non-answer in 46, and here the doubt grinds all night and won't quiet. So the book isn't asking me to just be happy for her vanishing — the dread is right there inside her, named, refusing to settle. That's the only reason I'm not gone. The day she lies to win a fight *and the book lets her get away with it clean, with no ache left over* — that's my exit. Tonight she got away with it but the ache stayed. Barely. That's the thread.

That's still sitting live in me and this chapter didn't touch it, and I want it so bad I can taste it more than those nachos.
