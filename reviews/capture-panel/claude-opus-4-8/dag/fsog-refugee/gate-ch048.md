REACTION

Oh, this is the one I've been waiting for and dreading — his side of it. And I have to hand it to the book: giving me Pace at the diner right after those three brutal chapters of Vee unraveling in her childhood bed is a smart, cruel move. Because I came in braced to catch him out. I wanted Sheri to be his other Vee. I wanted the chicken-week silence Vee couldn't see into to be him doing to somebody else exactly what he does to her. And it isn't that. Sheri's gay — Paige, the bird prints, the seven-at-the-table house she couldn't stay near. She rode with him for two years, she wants nothing off him, she steals his fries and calls him on his shit. She's the one person in this whole book who isn't inside the machine. I liked her instantly and I trust her, which is exactly why what she says lands.

Because here's what the chapter actually did to me: it let Sheri say the thing I've been screaming at the page for three chapters, and it let her say it clean. "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her." "From this seat, that feels pretty shitty." Nobody softens it. She doesn't buy the brick-laying — I felt him doing it, "every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something," and she just knocks the wall over: she didn't ask you for reasons. That's the truest sentence in the chapter and a woman who owes him nothing said it.

But — and this is the thing gnawing at me — WHY can't he say it? The book keeps him from answering. Every time Sheri pushes to the center, "he didn't answer that. He answered the one next to it." He has answers to everything adjacent and nothing to the actual question. And I know from the jacket, I know from Randi, that there's a *reason* he can't — the rule that he says it first, the year-long play, the whole architecture. So his "I can't" isn't a wound I'm being invited to nurse. It's a move. Or it's a man so far into a game he built that he's stopped being able to find the exit even when a friend holds the door. I genuinely can't tell which and I don't think the book wants me to yet. What I noticed: he "made the decision before the door had swung shut" not to bring her down today. He *decides* things can keep. He has "long practice at deciding a thing could keep." That's the same muscle as everything he does to Vee in bed — the ordering, the withholding, the giving-in-the-order-he-means-to-give. It's all the same hand.

And the tenderness that usually disarms me? It's here — "the air's different when she's in the room, it crackles," "she gets what it's for, the philosophy of it" — and Sheri deflates it perfectly. "You mean the sex is good." "The philosophy of your food. Lord almighty, get over yourself." I *loved* that. It's the first time the book has let anyone puncture the reverence around Pace, and it made him human and a little ridiculous and I liked him more for laughing. That's the closest this chapter came to touching me sweetly with the counter-voice actually *out* of the room — him laughing across two wrecked cheeseburgers.

But it didn't quite do it, because the counter-voice isn't scrubbed, it's just relocated into Sheri, and it's still ringing: he wants Vee, he misses her, and he still won't spend the word. The dread's not laid in with a heavy hand this time — it's just sitting there in plain daylight, being true. He's the one who left it where it is.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: "okay the gay best friend just told him to his face that not saying I love you means he cares more about the withholding than about the girl AND HE DIDN'T DENY IT. also he calls it the philosophy of his food and she said get over yourself and I screamed." And then, quieter: "I still can't tell if he's a coward or if he's running a play. the book won't let me see and I think that's the whole trick."

Two small things I'm holding. One: Sheri says Vee "find out about the brunette" — "No. Not like that." So Pace knows there's a brunette to find out about, knows the arrangement has a shape Vee could discover. He's inside the play with Randi and Sheri clocks it as a real thing that could blow up. Two: the fries. She reaches over and eats his fries, twice, and he lets her. Nobody eats off Pace's plate in this book — Vee gets fed in an order, on his terms. Sheri just takes. That's the one relationship in the book that runs on taking-without-asking and it's the only clean one. That's doing something and I don't have the shape of it yet.

I'm still in. Not because he charmed me — he didn't, this chapter, the charm got laughed at — but because someone finally said the quiet part at full volume and the book let her, and I want to see whether he can hear it.
