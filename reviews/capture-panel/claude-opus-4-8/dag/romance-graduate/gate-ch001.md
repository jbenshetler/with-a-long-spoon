REACTION

Okay. Okay okay okay. I need to text somebody about this and it's 1 a.m. and there's nobody awake.

First: I came for heat and this fed me heat, but that's not even the thing. The thing is who this chapter turned out to be about. I opened the jacket knowing the deal — Vee is the mark, Randi and Pace are the two who cooked up the game, Vee's the one who "never knew there was one." So I sat down braced for a seduction scene starring the innocent. And instead the very first chapter puts me in the room with the *architects*, and then — slowly, expertly — pulls the floor out and shows me that Randi, the glamorous predator, the one running the play on Vee, is herself completely undone on a bench in this man's boring little house with cheap laminate counters. That's the move. That is a *move*. Whoever Helen Rivers is, she knows exactly what she's doing, because I spent the whole chapter thinking "this is the new girlfriend, this is some sub he's training" and the name landed — *"There you are, Randi"* — and I actually said "oh my GOD" out loud.

Because now the whole blurb re-reads. "As true for the two who started it as for the girl who never knew there was one." Randi's the one who's going to get caught in her own trap. And chapter one already showed me the crack. She built a face at thirteen and hands the face to everyone and keeps nothing behind it reachable, and this man reached behind it and she *hated* that she loved it and she's already decided — quiet as a deadbolt going over — never again. The deadbolt. She locks the door first thing every time she comes in, and she locks the bathroom door tonight for the first time ever, and the last image is a key turning in a lock inside her own ribs. That's not decoration. That's the entire book's thesis dropped into chapter one and I felt it as dread, the good kind.

Now the sex. THANK you. This is what I've been trying to explain to people who hand me "literary" books where the door closes. It's fully explicit — the apple slices, the bench, the edging, the "not the right *please* yet," the three real pleases from below — and every single physical beat is doing psychological work at the same time. The edging isn't there to pad the word count, it's there to strip her of her apparatus. "She could not pout her way back. She could not turn the heat up; the heat was already at maximum, and the maximum was below her, where she was." I have read a thousand spanking scenes and I have never read one that was *about* something the way this one is. The apple thing wrecked me before we even got to the bedroom — thin slices, cut sides up because she eats faster that way, a man who has clocked her eating disorder and works around it with this awful tenderness. That's the interiority I've been starving for, and it's IN the seduction, not fenced off from it.

Pace. I don't trust him and I can't stop watching him. He's the fantasy dom done right — the patience, the "tell me your word," the aftercare, leaving the water bottle because he knows she drinks more than she expects. But the chapter is quietly telling me he's a little bit dangerous precisely *because* he's this good. Daphne built a door once and he went in uninvited and it cost him and he "learned." So now he respects the door. But he's also running a *game* on a 21-year-old with this same toolkit. And the last beat — she's smiling the lying smile, and he's reading it as "a beautiful night with a beautiful woman," deciding whether to offer dinner — he MISSED it. The man who sees everything just got out-faced by the one person whose whole life is the face. That's delicious and it's ominous.

What I'm suspicious of / a little scared of: the water-bottle-cap-under-the-thumbnail thing. She presses the sharp edge into her nail bed, hard, and doesn't know she's doing it. That's not spicy-romance shorthand. That's a person who hurts herself quietly, and the book slid it in without underlining it. Same with the crying she doesn't know she's doing. This book is going to hurt and it knows it and it's being honest with me about that on page one, which paradoxically makes me trust it.

Is it too much interiority, not enough forward motion for a chapter one? A tiny worry. It's one long scene, essentially — arrival, undressing, bench, aftercare, mirror. But it never stalled for me because the reveal is the plot engine and the mirror monologue is the whole premise loading. I don't feel like nothing happened; I feel like everything happened.

What I'd text a friend: "STOP what you're reading. Book one opens on the villains and then makes you realize the villain is the one about to fall and she just decided in a locked bathroom to never let anyone see her again and I am OBSESSED."
