REACTION

Oh, this one hurt in a different place than the others. Chapter 45 is the first time Vee actually swung at the thing — out loud, at the wrong volume, "Is this what we are now?" — and I sat up, because for forty-four chapters she's been doing her careful filing-down, and here she finally let the loud ugly true thing out of her mouth. And he fielded it like a man catching a thrown plate without flinching. "Okay. Where do you want to go tonight?" That move. God, that move. He takes the charge out of everything she brings him by refusing to be charged at. It's maddening and it's *expert* and the book knows it's expert.

But here's what got under my skin — the Chili's. Because this is the chapter where I can finally *see the cost of what he's done to her*, and it's not sexy and it's not tender, it's a dead nacho going soft under a pile of orange cheese. She wanted it. She drove there wanting it, the gloppy skillet, the fine ordinary night out — and he has ruined fine for her. "He had ruined it for her, was the thing. Not just this." That line landed like a stone. That's the whole horror of the book compressed into a chip: he rebuilt her palate so she can't eat at the table her own life came from. She's thin, her mother sat on the end of her bed, and now she can't even enjoy a bad plate of nachos. He's narrowed her. He's made her unfit for anything but him and calls it feeding her.

And then — this is the part I'm suspicious of, deeply — the whole restaurant misery gets *re-explained* into something gentle. The irritation on his face, the eyes slipping to the screen, which she reads (correctly, I think, for once) as him not wanting to be seen in public with her — and then in the truck he thaws, and she builds the accusation, and he sets down "You know what happened after the dance," and then the beautiful listening speech. "In a room like that I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you. Somewhere quieter, I can. And I do. I'm listening now." It's *gorgeous*. It's also exactly the kind of thing that turns a man's refusal to take her out in public into a sensory-devotion poem. He converts every limit he imposes into evidence of how precious she is. That's the engine. I've watched it run all book and here it is running on a restaurant.

What I can't stop turning over: she lies to herself in real time and the book lets me watch her choose the lie. "some back room of her knew which of them had gone cold that night, knew who'd carried a pie up whose porch steps after — and she said it anyway." She rewrote the Rusty Nail. She gave herself *forgiven* to wear — "warm and large and easy to wear" — because forgiving him gives the night a clean shape she can rest inside. She needs the shape more than the truth. That's the most honest thing about her and the saddest.

The sex was the best-written sex in a while because it was *about* something — her begging, silently, for his control to break, wanting him rough, wanting the want in him to get the better of him. Because his control is the wall. His patience is the thing she can't get past, the thing that keeps the name locked up. So when he finally does break, finally gets over her "like a man who'd run clean out of the strength to wait" — she reads it as having gotten something of him, taken the careful apart, *had* that. "She'd done that." And then the gut-punch: even with nothing left behind any wall, "he hadn't said it." He spent every last thing but the one. And the name drowned again at the top — "She came with his name in her mouth and not the other thing." Peter. She said Peter.

And the close: "did he belong to her?" The whole book flipping the ledger over. She's his, no dispute, a body trained to open and give itself away — but what does she *have* of him? Only this, only the dark, only what stops at the edges of the bed and can't be shown to anyone. That's it. That's the trap stated plainly and she almost — almost — sees the walls of it.

What I'm sick about: Randi's not in this chapter and I felt her absence like weather. After chapter 41, where Vee *saw the MIRA point* and chose to protect Randi, to not say the name, to hand her the busywork — I keep waiting for that to come back. Vee is now protecting the person who harvested her. She did the kindest thing a friend could do for the architect of her own ruin. And nobody in this bedroom knows what anybody knows. The dramatic irony is stacked three deep and I'm a step ahead of all of them, exactly like the jacket promised, and it's unbearable in the good way.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: "he RUINED NACHOS for her. that's the scariest thing in this book and it's a plate of nachos."
