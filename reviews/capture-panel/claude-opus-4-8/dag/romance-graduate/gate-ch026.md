REACTION

Okay, so here's the thing that's holding me hostage in a chapter that, on paper, is the exact move I've DNF'd forty books over: the jealous-outburst-at-the-bar, the Big Misunderstanding, girl storms off, spends a lonely night hating herself, decides she was Wrong. I have read this scene so many times I could've written the blonde with the too-much makeup and the poisoned little "he's so good about getting a girl home safe." I saw her coming from the bar rail. And normally the venom-and-flee routine is where I'd close the app, because it's conflict pumped up out of one bad read of one moment.

But this book has me by a different string and it knows it. Because *I* know what Vee doesn't, and it turns the whole tired scene inside out. Vee lashes out at Pace for running a game on women — "Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe" — and she's WRONG about the specifics and dead right about the shape of her life. There IS a game. There IS a plan. She's just aimed her one true instinct at the one man who, in the narrow local sense, is clean. The bartender lays it out flat — leaves them at the door, doesn't take advantage, brought a date on a Thursday for the first time — and it's supposed to land as "oh no, he's actually good, I wronged him." And it does land that way for Vee. But I'm sitting here going: girl, your radar isn't broken, it's just pointed three degrees off. The one person in your life actually working you is the one you're about to drive to at dawn to apologize. "Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey" — that line's meant to melt her and it iced ME instead.

So the dance lesson. I have to admit the pad scene got me a little, physically, in the way this book keeps getting me: "the space you're keeping. Close it — come all the way in — and my feet can't land anywhere but past yours." That's the whole con in a sentence about a frame. Give me something to push back against. Being jelly doesn't work. He's teaching her to surrender by teaching her to resist correctly, and it's genuinely the hottest thing in a chapter with zero sex in it. And she clocks it herself — "how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd been building out of wanting him" — which is the closest she's come to seeing the seam, and then the floor takes the question. Of course it does. That's the machine: every time she gets near the edge of the thought, something warm arrives to carry it off.

What I'm actually chewing on at 1 a.m.: this is the first chapter where Vee gets to be a whole ugly person on her own — petty, hot-faced, "spread her legs like a slut," calling the strangers floozies while she watches them, nursing the drink, turning down men just to punish herself. No Randi lockpicking, no Pace courtliness. Just Vee alone with her worst inheritance, the mother's voice — *a man like that has girls, and you'd be one of them* — arriving flat and on cue. And I love her more for it and I'm scared for her more, because the takeaway she builds out of a genuinely rotten night is "I punished a good man for being good, I'll go fix it." She's learning, from her own shame spiral, to trust him MORE and doubt herself. That's the most efficient thing this trap has done yet and it did it while he was offscreen, sulking home untouched-beer.

What it cost me: no heat, and the tropiest bones I've seen from Rivers. If the writing weren't real and if I didn't have the dread of Randi's doorstep waiting at the end, this is a stop. The "decides to go to Randi's" ending is the only thing that made the miscommunication feel load-bearing instead of filler — she's not resetting to zero, she's walking one more room deeper, to the architect, to debrief the fight. Fifth debrief incoming and this time she's bringing a wound Randi gets to dress.
