REACTION

Oh, this is the one. This is the chapter I didn't know I was owed.

Sixty chapters I've kept Sheri in my pocket — "the one who called it in four seconds," the four-second girl, the friend nobody listens to. And then somewhere back in Randi's room Vee said it herself, *I built her on the nights I felt stupid,* and I filed it: Sheri's not a person, she's Vee's own dread wearing gold hair and tan legs. So when Pace says "There's somebody I'd like you to meet" and then just — *"Sheri"* — over the sink, to the window, the way he hands over everything, dried-spoon flat, I got the cold train Vee got. "A coat of paint." Knowing the face was hers didn't stop the cold arriving on schedule. That's exactly right, that's how it works, you can know a fear is invented and still dress for it.

And then. The blonde "didn't die so much as fail to find anywhere to stand." Five foot nothing, boots included, two-color perm, makeup a little off worn like a dare. And the heat in Vee's chest *stayed exactly where it was.* "It had never needed the blonde." I actually put the wine down — the wine I'm not drinking, I'm the reader — because I've read the rival chapter four hundred times and it is ALWAYS the blonde, it is always the woman who is everything the heroine isn't, and this book took the blonde I'd been handed since Christmas and just quietly declined to make her exist. Vee armored herself sole to collar against a woman she invented, and the woman she got looked at her legs and went *oh.* The look "enjoying her." The laugh coming out before she voted on it, a month leaving her ribs in one breath. I laughed with her. God help me, four explicit chapters deep and counting gears and this bridge chapter — because that's what it is, a bridge, nobody's clothes come off — cracked me wide open.

Because here's the thing the book did that I did not see: it gave me a person who is *outside the machine.* Everyone in this book is being run or running someone. Randi steers, Pace withholds, Vee crops per audience, Cassie got charmed. And here's Sheri, who orders your cider without asking the chalkboard, who says "you don't praise a man at his own table," who shuts the folder with no seam and no glance to see how it landed — she's *calibrated,* every word chosen, so for a second I braced, oh no, another architect, another one who knows exactly what she's doing. And then she told the coming-out story. The tablecloth off the table, nobody caught a dish. "The ones who owed me words weren't spending any." And I understood her calibration isn't a con — it's a woman who lost everything in a month for saying one true thing and has learned precisely what words cost and who deserves them.

And she handed me PACE. The real one. Not Wednesday-host Pace working a room, not the doorstep Pace on his knees — the man who drove her home asking nothing, showed up with donuts and fixed the lock the landlord wouldn't. "He never handed me one false word — no *they'll come around*, no *their loss*." THAT'S the withholding, seen from the outside for the first time in the whole book and read as *mercy* instead of disease. He doesn't spend the cheap words. On anyone. And then Sheri's throwaway: "Went bad with his daddy. He hasn't been home since he was sixteen." Peter. Daphne. The father. The whole architecture of the silent generous man, and here's a witness who's known him two years and got there clean, without the machine.

The blush. Pace *blushing* — "motorboat them things?" and the color coming up past anything deniable, the ears, the neck, and Vee has seen him easy in the dark where nobody stays dignified and never once seen him embarrassed. "The single most disarming thing she'd watched him do." I felt that land in my chest. It's the first thing in sixty chapters he hasn't controlled. Randi's flinch is his blush — the one tell the cover can't swallow. The book gave them both a body that outruns them, and I clocked it.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: SHERI IS REAL AND SHE'S GAY AND SHE'S FIVE FEET TALL AND SHE ORDERED VEE A CIDER AND I LOVE HER AND I'M SCARED. Because — and here's my suspicion, sitting cold under all this warmth — this is the most genuinely happy chapter in a hundred pages, the one un-engineered table in the book, and the book knows I know that. Sheri is outside the con. Which means Sheri is the one person who could see the con. She called it in four seconds once. She reads a dance hall's whole economy — "he's making a down payment" — in one breath. If anyone at any table looks up and sees what's being built around Vee, it's this woman. So the book handed me joy AND handed me my one hope of a witness in the same small loud package, and I don't trust it not to use her. But right now, tonight, in the truck with Vee's head heavy on his shoulder and his hands ten-and-two "like something full he was carrying" — I let it be good. It earned good.

The heels. She reached past the boots for the induction heels Randi buckled on the boutique floor, wore them to a January taproom where you can't even two-step in them, and Sheri's first instruction is "leave them shoes home." The one clean person in the room, correcting the machine's costume without knowing it's a costume. That's the needle the whole chapter's threaded on and it's so quiet I almost missed it.
