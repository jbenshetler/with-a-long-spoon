Okay so here's what scares me: this chapter is where the book blinked, and I need to decide if I blinked with it.

Because chapter 39 — the outlier, MIRH, Miranda Holdings, Randi's own father's loans sitting on the worst point in the county, the "cheating bastards" said ragged and too near to be about strangers, Vee choosing not to say the name, choosing to hand Randi something to hold — that was the book at full power. And then chapter 40 just... goes back to the warm house. Salmon, then pizza from water buffalo cheese, the flannel shirt she washed and can't stand that it stopped smelling like him. And part of me went, wait, are we not going to sit in what just happened?

But then I clocked what it's doing, and it got me anyway, and I hate a little that it got me.

Because this is the "new ordinary" and that's the whole horror of the title. She called the five nights herself — she pulled BACK, she chose school, she did the healthy thing — and lasted five, and the book knows that's the tell. "Some unreasonable corner of her had wanted him to fight." That line is the machine still running under the sweetness. He never fights. He opens the door and waits on the far side, because a man who fights is a man you can push against, and he taught her at the dance you can't be jelly, you have to give someone something to push against — and now HE'S the one who won't be pushed against, and it costs her more than a fight would, and she doesn't know why. I know why. The narration still knows more than her gladness does. That's the only reason I'm still in this book and chapter 40 kept the contract.

The drawer. The flannel shirt. God, the shirt. She wore his shirt every night she wasn't there and it went stale and then it went to NOTHING and one day it didn't smell like anything at all, so she washed it and needs him to wear it all weekend and give it back Sunday so it smells like him again. She's rationing him. She's built a system for metabolizing his absence, a shirt she fills and empties and refills, and she's crying about it in his kitchen and blinking it down. That's not a woman in a nice relationship. That's a woman in withdrawal who has engineered a maintenance dose. And the book lets me see it as exactly that AND as unbearably tender at the same time, which is the merciless part — it's not cruel to her, it's cruel to ME, because I feel the sweetness land while I can read the dependency underneath it.

The food-as-taste-waking-up thing is getting a little repetitive, I'll be honest — the cotton candy vs. cut strawberries, the "walk down each flavor one at a time," we've done this beat several times now and I could feel the shape of it coming before it came. Same move as the wet finger, the body telling on her, and it still charges me because it's shame-as-heat done with actual interiority, not just wet-and-ready boilerplate. Twice now — "unusually proportioned table," singled out, italicized-almost, in the BEDROOM. That's not furniture. That's a Chekhov's table. A furniture-maker built a table of unusual proportions and put it in the room where he takes her, and the book has now pointed at it twice without letting her look. The scar with a time on the door, and now a table with a purpose on it. I am not going to be able to leave before I find out what that table is for.

What I'm sitting with: the reckoning I got in 39 is going to get buried under exactly this — the new ordinary, the warmth, the shirt — and that's the point. Vee saw Miranda Holdings and chose not to say the name, and now she's back in the trees being fed, and Randi's crack got sealed over by Vee's own kindness. Everyone keeps re-sealing everyone. Nobody says the name. The whole book is people declining to say the name.

This was a quieter, softer chapter than 39 and I felt the dip, but it's a rest beat with two live wires in it (the table, the rationing), and I turn the page.
