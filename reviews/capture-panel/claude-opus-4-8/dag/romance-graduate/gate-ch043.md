REACTION

Oh, this one hurt me in the good way and then the bad way and then it just kept its hand on my throat.

Chapter 42 set the fuse — the cracker-and-meal sentence, her one attempt, built for weeks, carried whole in her mouth like a memorized poem, and he answers with "I love how good we are together" and she can't even tell if it went past him or if he clocked exactly what she was doing and stepped around it on purpose. That's the thing this book keeps doing to me: it will not let me know whether he's dense or surgical, and I've decided by now he's never dense. So when 43 opens with her holding the word again in the dark, I already knew we were paying for the drawer and the scrunchies and every treasured thing. The bill came.

And what a smart, ugly little escalation. She finally throws the loud thing — "Is this what we are now?" — a daytime voice in a room built for murmuring, and I actually flinched with her, because I know that feeling, the one where you hear your own volume land and can't un-say it. And his move is the whole horror of him in three words: "Okay. Where do you want to go tonight?" He takes the ugly thing she threw and sets it down gently and hands her a plain question, and there's nothing to do with a plain question but answer it. That's not kindness. That's him refusing to have the fight she needs to have, and dressing the refusal as generosity. Chili's. She says Chili's. God, the Chili's of it all — she reaches back for the girl who thought a skillet of nachos was a fine night, and it's gone, he took it, "he had ruined it for her, was the thing. Not just this." That line is the whole trap in one sentence. He's remade her palate so thoroughly that the plain pleasures of her old life taste like a billboard next to a painting, and she can't go back down a size, same as the bras.

But here's where I got genuinely scared, and where I want to text a friend at 1 a.m.: the restaurant scene, the flicker of irritation, the fork squaring to the table, his eyes slipping to the screen. She reads it as "he's ashamed to be seen with me, this is just sex, proven in fluorescent light." And then he explains — the loud room, "I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you," "I'm listening now" — and the fight leaves her all at once and she goes to him and tells herself she's forgiven HIM. And I sat there going: no. No, honey. YOU know who went cold after the dance. Some back room of her knows it too — "knew who'd carried a pie up whose porch steps after" — and she says the false thing anyway because she needs the line of it to hold. She rewrote the fight so she could be the one who forgives. That's the Randi move. That's the fraud-daughter move, the making-things-truer-with-the-right-image move, and she's running it on herself now, in her own head, to keep the shape clean enough to rest inside. Ch39 she managed Randi's hands onto the busywork. Here she manages her own memory. She's not just installed anymore, she's staffed.

And the sex — I'll give it its due, because the book always earns the heat and this was real. The wanting-him-to-lose-control, and then getting it, and it catching her up before she knew she had it left in her. The name coming up right at the top and her body breaking over the edge and taking it back down, "drowned it clean, as it always did, as she let it" — she came with his name and not the other thing. That's devastating and hot at once, which is the only register this book has. But the after is the knife: marked all over, his spend low in her, hers, and the arithmetic starts. "She belonged to him... But did he belong to her?" She had him helpless tonight, took the careful apart, and even with nothing left behind any wall he didn't say it. "He'd given her all of it and not the name."

And the closing turn is the cruelest and the most honest thing in the chapter — she has no name for the best thing in her life, not his big one and not her small one. Can't say "boyfriend" to her mother, can't lay him out for her father. A nameless thing that lives only in the dark and stops at its edges. That's the whole book's thesis arriving through her own ribs: every yes freely given, and no word for what she's said yes to.

Where's Randi in all this? Nowhere on the page, and that's loud. Because the not-saying-it, the rule that he says it first, the fight she can't have — who taught her that the fear of saying it into a room that won't say it back is the worst thing? Randi's Redford-boy story, "willing to take care of myself." The scripts are all in her now and she thinks they're her own instincts.

What's cooling me, a little, honestly: three chapters in a row of the same interior loop — the word she can't say, the not-knowing, the shape she pushes into it. It's beautifully done and I feel it, but I've now watched her fail to say it, half-say it, and get told-around-it, and I can feel the book circling the same drain. I want the wire to touch something outside her skull. Cassie's holding the fraud she doesn't know about; the nude's out in Randi's hand; Sheri and Thanksgiving are loaded. Give me the room, not just the ribs. But that "he had ruined it for her" and the self-forgiveness rewrite bought back every bit of my patience. I'm still all the way in.
