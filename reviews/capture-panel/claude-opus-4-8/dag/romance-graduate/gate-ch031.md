REACTION

Oh, this one's a swerve, and a smart one. For once nobody's in the room with her. Pace is in Berkeley, Randi's only in the shoes and the ankle strap, and the whole architecture of the book — the two people running her — just steps offstage and leaves Vee alone in the good light with a scroll and a slit. And I sat up, because this is the chapter where I find out what's actually hers.

The first step is the careful one. She could mince it modest to the podium and no one would ever know there was anything to know. And then — "She took a breath, and made the second step long." God. That's the whole book in one line. She chooses it. She gives the room her leg on purpose, uncropped, and doesn't make herself small. And here's what has me chewing my thumbnail: is that her, finally, sovereign — or is that the machine so deep in her now that it runs with no operator present? The book won't tell me, and I love it and hate it for that. "Whether that was pride or only the same heat still running, or whether, by now, the two came to the same thing." That last clause is the knife. That's the dread I've been carrying paid out in a single sentence — the possibility that the distinction has stopped existing inside her. They didn't have to be there. They installed it. It walks on its own now.

But — and this is why I'm still not out — the narration is STILL honest about it. It doesn't sell me the long step as pure triumph. It hands me the ambiguity straight, lets me feel both the real thrill (she has good legs, she stalked, the dull room woke up — that IS a rush and the book lets it be one) and the horror underneath. That's the tightrope I've been trusting for thirty chapters and it hasn't slipped.

And the photo. Oh, the photo undid me a little. She sends Pace the uncropped frame — the dress doing exactly what he built it to do — and "He would have no idea what else was in the frame. That part was hers." That's the first time I've seen Vee keep something. A private interior he doesn't get to read, doesn't get to name for her. She's been living inside their vocabulary the whole book, feeling her own life through Randi's words, and here she has a fact about herself that stays behind her own teeth. It's small and it's the most hopeful thing that's happened to her, and it's also — of course — built entirely out of what they made her. She's keeping a secret that only exists because of the dress they made her come bare in. Sovereignty and capture in the same gesture. This book will not let me have a clean feeling.

Cassie. CASSIE. She held the phone. She said "You look—" and didn't finish it. "Go make your mother cry again." "Come on, streetlight." She's the only person in this whole book who wants nothing from Vee, who documents it because someone had to, who buys the terrible sandwich. And Vee's first and only thought, the whole walk, is "How did I look?" — not "did I do it," not "was I brave," but how did I LOOK. That's the tell that broke my heart. The instrument is right there, offering the actual thing — a witness who loves her — and Vee's whole interior is angled at the mirror the other two built. She sends the frame to Pace. She stands with Cassie. The wrong one gets her attention even here. And the phrase "she could be famous at me" — Cassie knows exactly what she's holding and stays anyway.

The thing I noticed, the thing I'll carry: the black dress still on its hanger at home, "the one that had always been good enough." Fine. Fine was never enough — that's the jacket, that's the whole premise. And she left Fine on the hanger and came bare. That's the trade the book keeps asking me to weigh, and this chapter is the cleanest statement of the cost yet, because there's no seducer in the room to blame. Just Vee, and her breath, and the second step.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: "she made the second step long AND NOBODY WAS EVEN THERE MAKING HER. that's the scariest chapter yet and nothing happened."
