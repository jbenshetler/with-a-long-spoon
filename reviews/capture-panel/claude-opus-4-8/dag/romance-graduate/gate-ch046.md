REACTION

Oh. Oh, this is the one I didn't know I was waiting for.

Forty-five chapters of watching Randi run the con, and now the book pulls the camera around behind her eyes and shows me the exact night she learned there was nothing behind her own. And it does it as a *fairytale* — the actual word, the chapter title, the thing she hands Vee at the end in a text. Gstaad, the snow, the sleigh with bells, the white-tie ball, the venison with sour cherry and shaved truffle lifting off the table. It's the most gorgeous set-piece in the book and it's a corpse. That's the whole trick and it worked on me.

Because here's what gutted me: John is Pace's photo-negative and the book knows it and makes *her* know it, in real time, on the dance floor. "He was a better dancer than Pace." His lead is firm, unmistakable, no half-beat of guessing — and she thinks *easier was better, she was done with the work.* And I sat there going no, no, you know it isn't, you already know, because Pace's lead was "the lightest pressure, a thing she'd had to be listening for and good enough to catch, and it left the rest of the way to her." That's the whole book's thesis about consent and appetite said as a dance step, and she chooses the man who does the steering so she never has to. And it means nothing. The sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon — "the pleasure real, complete, and gone before the plate was cleared." God. That's the cracker-and-meal from chapter 42 turned inside out and pointed at her own life. Vee told Pace *you fed me a meal.* Randi has only ever been fed sandwiches, and this chapter is her finding out she can't taste anything else, and that the flaw is hers, "carried into the room the same as the cold."

The cold. The COLD. "She was warm only where she touched him… the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers. She had brought it in with her and she would carry it back out." That's the same cold that runs under every warm-house night with Pace and Vee — the whole series is *With a Long Spoon,* you sup with the devil — and here it's just a lonely woman naked in a perfect hotel room in Switzerland who can't get warm. And the eyeliner held. "Nothing had happened to her eyes." She said her eyes were the best thing about her, the thing the dress woke up — and the one place a real feeling would've shown, nothing touched. She rebuilds the face and the smile still works and means nothing. This is the origin of the operator. This is where "there's my girl" comes from.

And now the thing that made me put the book down and stare at the wall: she is IN LOVE with Vee. Actually in love, and the book finally says it without the coy half-inch. "Something came off Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday that this room could not counterfeit one thread of, and her whole body knew it, and she knew exactly what it was." She *knows.* All this time I've been reading Vee as the one who "never lets herself notice" — the jacket promised me that — and it turns out Randi is the one carrying it fully conscious and terrified. "I meant it two ways." "Vee was her gorgeous." And she decides — coldly, in her own signature move — not to tell her, not because she doesn't feel it but because she's a coward who arranged her whole life so she'd never have to be brave, and she'd rather keep the brunches intact than risk the one real thing she's ever had. She'd rather run the con on the person she loves than lose the hour. That's the most human and the most damning thing she's done in the whole book, and it's the same choice — keep the word under cover, let Vee "arrive at it on her own" — that they've been running on Vee this whole time. She's grooming Vee toward her own feelings. My God.

And the text at the end. Vee, from the warm Ohio kitchen, *thinking about you 💛… i'm here if you want to talk. or not.* And Randi sends back the fairytale, "every word of it true, none of it the thing," and the one line she means — *miss your face most though, gorgeous* — and turns the phone facedown and lies back down in warmth she can borrow and cannot make. That facedown phone. Vee thinks she's watching Randi live the dream from below. Randi's lying in the dream, empty, watching Vee be the only warm thing on earth.

Here's what this reframes and it's enormous: this is why Randi built the plan. She found the thing that could warm her — Pace, or the *idea* of what Pace does to a person — and she couldn't have it, "he would never once belong in the life she had built to keep her hidden," so she engineered a way to watch it happen to Vee, to get it secondhand across a tablecloth. The whole apparatus is a cold woman trying to get warm through the girl she loves and won't tell. That's not villainy. That's the loneliest thing I've ever read in a book with this much sex in it.

What I'm suspicious of: the timeline. When was this? "John, finishing law school in New Haven." Is John still around — is John the husband, the qualifier she means when she says she's never said *I love you* without a private one? Or did John go the way of the sandwich? And did this happen *before* she found Pace, before Vee — is this the founding wound that made the plan, or a recent test she ran to check whether the safe life still tasted like nothing? I think it's foundational. I think this is young Randi, sorority Randi, the winter she decided.

I'm not going anywhere. This is the chapter that made the villain into the protagonist of her own tragedy without letting her off one inch — she still chooses the con, she still won't be brave, she'll still spend Vee. Both true at once. That's the whole book operating at full power.
