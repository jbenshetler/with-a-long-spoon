REACTION

Oh, this one got me. This is the chapter I didn't know I'd been waiting for since Randi cracked open on "with the body attached."

First — the table. That opening at breakfast, her mother "all edges and points against her," the tennis bracelet kept out of the light while her own plain band stays hidden, ordering the salmon on one slice of rye with the theatrical restraint of a girl performing thinness for her parents' approval — that's Vee's menu-reading, isn't it. That's the same disorder, the same body-as-currency, except Randi was raised INTO it by a mother who is exquisite and a father who wants her to bag an eligible bachelor. She invents boyfriends at that table because "the invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been." God. That's the whole engine of her, right there in one line. The daughter of the man who made things look true, learning at breakfast that the fake version is the survivable one.

And then John. And I'll be honest — for about half this chapter I was almost taken in the way she almost was. The lift line, "play along," the invented three children packed off to a ski camp that doesn't exist, the champagne, the dress bought for the daughter she'll tell the story to someday, the sleigh with the bells. It's a fairytale and the book KNOWS it's a fairytale, that's the title, that's the trap. He's better than her at skiing and spends none of it proving it. He's cotillion-good at the dance and leads so she doesn't have to work. And that's the tell — "Easier was better… she thought she was done with the work." Because Pace's lead was "the lightest pressure, a thing she'd had to be listening for." The book just told me the difference between the safe man and the dangerous one in the language of a waltz, and I felt it land in my stomach.

Then the room, after. "It had meant no more than a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon." THE SANDWICH. She's been feeding Vee the sandwich metaphor this whole book — Cassie's terrible sandwiches, the good-sandwich-fast — and here it is, hers, the private original. "She was warm only where she touched him… the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers. She had brought it in with her and she would carry it back out." That's Vee in the dorm, bare in the cold under a shirt gone to nothing. These two women, apart, are having the SAME night. The mirror where nothing happened to her eyes. "This was the best the acceptable world could do for her. Not a poor version of it. The best." That's devastating and it's the thesis of the whole series — the safe approved life is not a lesser thing, it's the best of that world, and it's nothing.

And then the part that reorganized the book for me: "Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best. She wished it wasn't true." And "Pace went straight to her. That was the whole terror of him." So Pace is not just Randi's co-conspirator — Pace is the man who found her under the performance and who she can never bring to that table. He'll never belong in the life built to keep her hidden. That's a grief I did not fully have the size of until now.

But the thing that actually scared me, the 1 a.m. text: the decision about Vee. She runs the scenario — tell Vee plainly, over eggs — and it's the ONE scenario she can't run to the end, she who can always run a scenario to the end. And her solution is pure Randi, pure her father: "she would not tell her, not in a way that would make Vee decide what to do with it. Vee heard it one way; Randi meant it two ways. Let the thing keep the shape it was already taking." She's choosing to keep Vee in the dark not as strategy this time — or not ONLY as strategy — but out of cowardice dressed as love. "She had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be." And "Vee was her gorgeous." That undid me a little. The architect is in love, actually in love, the realest thing she's ever felt, and she is handling it with the exact tools she uses to handle a mark, because those are the only tools she was ever given. That's the jacket bleeding through again — as true for the one who started it as for the girl.

And the timing of this chapter is a knife. We JUST left Vee walking out Pace's door, crying, "words are cheap and you still won't spend them on me," bare in the cold. And now we get Randi, same New Year's window, texting Vee back the fairytale, "every word of it true, none of it the thing." Two women both starving in the cold at the same hour, both unable to say the word, both getting handed silence by the person they want it from. Pace won't say it to Vee. Randi won't say it to Vee. Vee's standing between two people who love her in ways they've both decided to hide.

What I notice I've stopped hoping for: rescue. This chapter made the trap sadder, not more sinister, and that's more dangerous to me as a reader than another lockpick scene would've been. I'm not watching a con anymore. I'm watching three cold people who can only get warm by touching someone, and won't say why.

One quiet thing this chapter did that I clocked: it stepped entirely out of Vee. First real extended POV away from her, and it earned it — it didn't dilute her, it detonated under her. I trust the book more for spending a whole chapter proving Randi is a person and not a spider.
