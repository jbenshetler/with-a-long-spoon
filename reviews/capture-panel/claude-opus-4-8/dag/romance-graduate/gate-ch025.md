REACTION

Okay, so this is the breather chapter, and I know a breather on sight — after the dress and the whole ridiculous joined-at-the-hip relay of 24, I didn't need another hot one and I didn't resent getting the shoes instead. What I resented, for exactly one sentence, was the baseball.

Simpson's paradox. *"Same numbers. Nobody touched one of them."* Come ON. I have now been handed error-correcting codes, the MRI signal-in-the-noise thing, and now a stats lecture, each one a tidy little placard the narration tapes to the wall so I don't miss that the whole is a different animal than the sum of its innocent parts — that every yes was fine and the aggregate is the trap. I GOT it. I got it in chapter three. The book keeps flossing its own subtext in front of me and it's the one habit that makes me feel handled instead of seduced. If it does it a fourth time I'm going to start marking them.

But here's the thing that actually reached in and grabbed me, and it's not the toe moment (though — the toe moment: Randi on one knee, thumb across the frosted plum, the floor tipping, Vee's hand shooting out to a shoulder to stay upright. That's the daylight wanting the jacket promised, moving in the open now, and it's good). It's the credit card. Vee standing at the counter with the debit card between her fingers — the card that only ever spends what's already behind it, the girl who reads the right side of the menu, who feels every dollar on Thursday — and putting it *back*. Taking out the emergency card with nothing under it, knowing the bill will be sitting there next month, and signing anyway and not letting it reach her face because the one thing she won't do in that room is flinch. THAT'S the chapter. That's the first time the surrender has cost her something concrete and irreversible in the daylight, something she can't ladle out of a story to Cassie. She's been handing over her body and her shame gladly; now she's handing over her actual solvency to belong to Randi's world, and Randi is right beside her buying loafers at a multiple of it *without looking at the number.* The whole seduction economics of that — the lure being ease with money, Vee going quietly into debt to stand next to it — landed harder than any bedroom scene could have. That's the receipt she doesn't know she's signing, and it rhymes with the green panics she traded away without noticing it was evidence.

And Cassie, God. "The friend was the only part of any of it Cassie had ever been able to see." She never once looked at the professor. She doesn't care about the dress, never cared about a dress, cares about what the dress did to Vee's face. That's the only clear-eyed love in the whole book and it's the one Vee's slowly starving out. It made me sad in the good way.

The comedy earned its keep too — the yeti canoe shoe, the pink rhinestone platforms, "one brisk walk from becoming a sandal." I laughed, and I liked that Randi's real laugh "costs her something to give," because it means even the fun is being spent down. Same-size-seven, "practically a marriage" — the sharing, the merger, all of it pointing one direction.

I want the induction now. The dress is done, the ruinous shoes are bought on a card she can't cover, the whole thing is a loaded gun on the mantel. Show me her walk into that room.
