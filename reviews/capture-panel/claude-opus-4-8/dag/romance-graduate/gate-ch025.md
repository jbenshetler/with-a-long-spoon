REACTION

Okay so I got no heat and I didn't even miss it, which almost never happens to me, and I want to sit with why.

This is the Daphne chapter. Twenty-four chapters of that scar sitting up on the inside of his thigh unexplained, and I called it in my notes — Daphne, the door he opened once — and here it is, except it's not the scar he tells her about, it's the wound the scar is standing in front of. He was fourteen taking the SAT, fifteen in a dormitory full of grown men, and they handed him a nineteen-year-old "tutor," and it went on two years. And Helen Rivers does the thing I've been begging this book to keep doing: she narrates it as the machine, not the swoon. Pace lays it out in perfect order — "nothing happened, nobody did anything wrong, it would have been stranger if she'd stayed" — and the narration tells me flat out that the good order is the tell, that this is the same underselling voice from the hallway gone one degree flatter, "held the careful way a person carries a full glass so it won't go over the rim." He handed her the thing that broke him and named it luck. That's the exact grammar Randi used on Vee — "you're not caught, sweetheart, you're lucky." And now I know where he learned it. He learned it face-down. This is the origin of the whole con. The man who "asks before he takes, leaves her the room to choose" was made by someone who took from a child and taught him to file it under best two years I'd had.

That reframes everything and I actually gasped a little. Because if Pace is a survivor of exactly this, then he's not running Randi's game cynically — he's running the only tenderness he was ever shown, the tender version of the thing that hurt him, and he cannot see the seam because he's standing inside it. Vee held the ugly word under her tongue — "the clean ugly grown-up word" — and wouldn't say it because it wasn't hers to say. I said it for her. Grooming. And the horror of the book just doubled, because the groomer is also the groomed.

The hallway story — teaching himself trig in seventh grade, asking questions the teacher couldn't answer, so she moved his desk into the hall and gave him long-division worksheets for a year. "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment." That's Vee, actually seeing him, and it's the first time in the book I've watched HER be the instrument that finds the true signal under someone's noise. She did to him what he does to her. And he responded like she'd put her hand on a place he hadn't offered.

And the infinite-paint horn, the coastline, Newton's three sentences — I should have found that tedious, the brilliant-man-explains-beauty setpiece, I've DNF'd books for less. It didn't read as showing off because it was grief. "Most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it, and I got to read some." A boy who got pulled out of childhood and handed the universe as compensation. It's the same transaction as Daphne. Everything he got, he got by being taken early.

What I'm suspicious of — and this is the thing keeping me from fully melting — is how good he is. The book keeps him sympathetic to the exact millimeter. I still don't have Pace and Randi's origin on the record; chapter 24 gave me them in bed, finally in a room together, but it was still the machine humming, Randi tightening when he described Vee lighting up a room. The cost is starting to land on the two who started it — Randi "got past me before I could help it" — but I want the ledger. I want to know if Daphne is why Randi does the shopping, if Randi found a broken boy and built a life around aiming his brokenness at girls. The scar's still closed — "not tonight," a door with a time on it. I'll wait for that door.

No heat this chapter and it earned the skip, which is the highest thing I can say. But it's a loan against next chapter. If the book goes three more like this I'll feel the closed door.
