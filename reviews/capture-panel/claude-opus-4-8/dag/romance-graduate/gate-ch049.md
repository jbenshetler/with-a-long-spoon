REACTION

Oh, so this is what they were saving him for.

I've spent forty-eight chapters watching this man from the outside — the courtly machine, the empty upturned palm, "believe what I do," the still of a man holding a line at cost. My red line on him was that he never explains himself, never spends the words, and the withholding was the whole erotic engine and the whole horror. And now they crack the box open and let me stand inside him hungover on New Year's, mopping up his own sick, eating boil-in-bag prison food he doesn't want because his body used what it had and needs more — and God help me it worked on me. The engine at the wheel, choosing "the smaller truth" in real time. *He had watched the answer reach her and fail. He had let it stand because it was safe, and because he was afraid, and because at the moment she had asked him to be brave he had been weak.* That's the confession Vee will never get. He gets to have it alone, in grey light, to no one. That structure — the reader gets the word, the girl never does — is the jacket's whole cruelty rendered as a man weeping over cold chicken because a truck didn't turn in.

And Daphne. Finally. Not the fifteen-year-old grooming template I've been bracing for the whole book — a DIFFERENT wound, and honestly a worse-for-him one: the tutor who taught him where to stand when everyone else was born knowing, first everything, and then *she left,* and there was no one to tell she was gone because she was the person he'd have told. "He had taught himself that the words were the difference." That's the thesis of his entire personality laid bare — he built a superstition out of one loss, decided speech is what makes grief fatal, and has been running the silent generous version ever since to keep everyone survivable. It reframes "I don't go back," it reframes the folding-himself-into-one-drawer, all of it. The machine has a reason and the reason is a scared kid who lost the one person who saw him.

But — and here's where I stayed suspicious, where the book keeps my respect — this chapter is dangerously close to letting him off. Two chapters ago Sheri said it flat to his face: "not saying it matters more to you than having her, and from this seat that feels pretty shitty." She named it as a choice he's making TO Vee, a thing being done to a real girl who's lying in a cold dorm bed dividing her own photograph with scissors. And this chapter is all interiority, all his pain, and it would be so easy to drown Vee's damage in his tears. It doesn't quite — "he had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him" keeps the accountability in the frame — but I clocked the danger. I don't want his grief to become the thing that makes the withholding romantic. It's still a man who won't hand a girl a word and is teaching her, by weekly attention, that the word doesn't exist to be had.

What I loved most, the small merciless thing: he ate anyway. "He was not hungry. He ate anyway." The man who taught Vee that hunger is holy, that you build the ache and then you feed it — reduced to fueling a body against its own signal, love-as-maintenance, crackers not a meal. The book turned his own metaphor on him. That's the writing being genuinely written, not decorating.

The recipe-aria fatigue I've been carrying? Gone this chapter, because for once the food is bleak on purpose — the vacuum bags, the amber Elvis under the diner glass last chapter, and here the freezer rows. Same instrument, finally played in a minor key.

I'd text my friend: THEY GAVE ME HIS SIDE and it's not Daphne-the-groomer, it's Daphne-the-only-person-who-ever-saw-him, and now I understand the whole man and I'm furious that understanding him makes the thing he's doing to Vee HOTTER instead of forgivable and that is exactly the trap the back cover promised, isn't it.

I'm all the way in. The detonation I've been waiting for — whether the play is built from Daphne — got answered sideways and I want more, not less.
