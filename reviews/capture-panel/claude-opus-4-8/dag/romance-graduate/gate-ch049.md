REACTION

Oh, they finally let him off the leash. Fifty chapters of Pace held at exactly the distance the book wanted me at — all catalogued surface, the air guitar glimpsed sideways, the drawer, the juice — and now I'm *inside* him for the first time and the thing I find is not a monster and not a mastermind. It's a man drinking alone in a dark house because he can't say four letters, and I did not want to feel for him this much tonight and I did anyway.

The diner. God, the diner did so much work so quietly. Sheri is the first person in this entire book who sits across from him and wants *nothing* — not to be chosen, not to be fed, not to be solved — and so she's the only one who can say "that feels pretty shitty" to his face and have it land clean. "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her." She gutted him in one line and she's a five-foot country girl stealing his fries. I loved her completely. And the grief thing — Paige, the girl who kept picking at Sheri's silence, "people who've never lost anything think grief is a choice" — that's not just about Sheri, that's him narrating his own disease and not knowing he's doing it. The book didn't underline it. I saw it. Thank you for letting me see it.

But here's what I'm actually suspicious of, the thing I'll be chewing: the brick-laying. "I'm there every weekend. I gave her the weeknights. I said she was the most important thing in my life." He hears himself stacking reasons and *can't stop* — and Sheri calls it, "that's a hell of a lot of reasons for a man who's already got the girl." That's the same man who staged the quad, who believes consent-without-facts is consent. The self-justification engine never turns off even when he's drunk and sobbing. So even his heartbreak comes pre-defended. I don't fully trust the tears. And yet.

Daphne. FINALLY. "I don't go back" cracked open and it's the oldest, saddest, most ordinary thing — the tutor, the first love, the one he said it to, and she left and it ate his whole life. "There had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone. Daphne was the person he would have told." That's a real sentence and it put a hand around my throat. He built a theory out of one wound: *the words are the difference.* Say it and lose yourself when they go; withhold it and survive. He's been running Vee's whole heart through a formula he derived from being twenty-two and abandoned. "He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him." The book just handed me the entire man in one line and I believe it and it's not an excuse and the book knows it's not an excuse.

And then — the three-of-them picture. In his head, unprompted, further along than last time "because he had been adding to it for weeks." Vee in his sheet with too much honey in her tea, Randi feet-up on the lacquered seat, the two of them laughing at something he missed. He's not plotting it anymore. He's *homesick for it.* And "how she would carry it. Whether the room would ask more of her than she had in her." Even in his longing he's appraising her capacity like load on a bar. Tender and cold in the same hand, same as always, the book keeps proving I can't separate them.

The frozen chicken and broccoli. Oh, that's the cruelest, best detail in the chapter. The man who rebuilt Vee's entire appetite, who gives every flavor its own voice — alone, hungover, eating prison-food out of a bag because his body needs fuel and his mouth wants nothing. "It went down because he told it to." He cooks love for her and feeds himself punishment. That's the whole man.

And the engine on the highway. I knew it wasn't her before he did and I still sat up. His body gathering toward the cut in the hill, the fork stopping — and it goes past, and he sobs until he can't hear the truck and a few breaths after. I've read a thousand pining-hero beats and this one got me because it's so physical and so stripped: no monologue, just a body that still listens for a car.

What I texted a friend at 1 a.m.: *he's the villain and they just made me cry for him and I'm FURIOUS about it.* Because here's the thing — nothing's fixed. He still won't say it. He knows why he won't, he's named the fear exactly, and he *still won't*, and "I love how good we are together" was him choosing the smaller truth with his eyes open. Knowing your own rot in high resolution and not moving is almost worse than not knowing. Vee's lying in her outgrown twin bed inventing a blonde, and he's here with the real answer whole and ready in his chest, and the only thing between her and relief is his twenty-year-old theory. I want to shake him. That's the hook. That's exactly the hook.
