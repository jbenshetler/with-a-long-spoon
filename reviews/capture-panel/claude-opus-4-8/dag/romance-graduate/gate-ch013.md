REACTION

Okay. Okay. So he made the shirt. He *made the shirt* — the one she wore into the ground, the one with no tag she turned inside out hunting for a brand. I had that detail from way back and I did not clock it, same as she didn't, and the book let me not clock it, and then it paid it off right here in front of me. That's the thing I keep not being ready for with this book: it plants and it waits and it doesn't nudge me in the ribs when it collects. I would've forgiven a lesser book for underlining it. This one just let me feel stupid for the right amount of time.

And the dress. God. The russet from chapter 11 — she said it over cherries, half-ashamed, already putting it back on the shelf, *it'll be good enough for every dinner,* the mother-voice doing the portioning. And he said "that sounds like a beautiful dress," took another cherry, and I thought — I actually thought — *he's doing the apple thing again, he's the man who removes the portioning.* And he is. But the detail that got me, the detail that is genuinely good and not just sweet, is that he didn't buy the shade she named. He matched *her.* The magazine brick-red would've fought her coloring; he held the burgundy up to her hair in the mirror and it was mixed for her. That's the whole man in one gesture — he doesn't give you the thing you asked for, he gives you the thing you'd have asked for if you knew yourself as well as he's decided to. Which is unbearably romantic and also exactly the thing that's been scaring me since chapter one. He sees past what you say to what you are, and then he *provisions* for it, weeks out, before you walk through the door.

Because here's where my stomach is. This is the bench. "Do you think she'll like the bench?" has been sitting in me for five chapters and this is what it looks like when it arrives — not a bench, a gown, cut on her body, in good light, down to what she wears underneath. He's already told her the terms: he measures her first, in the satin, so he can see how it falls. And she wore the satin out there *on purpose,* for the shut bedroom door that didn't open. He didn't open it. He said "my bedroom" flat as "the kitchen" and left it shut and something in her dropped — and that drop is the hook tightening. He stopped the kiss on the stoop. He's the one who stops now. No boy ever stopped. And every single withholding makes her want it more and makes it *her* reach, her yes, her idea. That's the machine. Every yes freely given. That was the problem. The jacket told me and I'm watching it run and I still want her to have the dress.

What I can't get past: this is designed. The too-warm house she walked past asking about, "the way you walk past a wrapped box on the counter without asking who it's for" — the book literally told me there's a wrapped box and she's choosing not to ask. And I know from chapter 13 that he's feeding Randi the trail secondhand, *her stomach is soft,* keeping Vee's truck surrender as currency he gives Randi in bed. So this gorgeous dress scene — the single most romantic thing anyone's done for Vee — is happening inside an operation. The measuring is the point. He engineered a reason to put his hands on her in good light, in the satin, and made it so she'd *beg* for it, warm and immediate, no hedge. The craft of this book is that both those things are true at the same temperature and it won't resolve them for me.

The MRI patent — lighting up like a six-year-old about dinosaurs, the kid gets out of the tube faster — fine, it's the one place he's unguarded, I'll take it, it humanizes him and I note that it's also exactly the detail that makes a woman trust a man. Everything he does works twice.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: HE MADE THE SHIRT. And then: I think the dress is the trap and I'd put it on anyway and that's the scariest sentence I've written about myself all year.
