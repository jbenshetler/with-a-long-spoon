T1 — Vee, Pace, Randi, where it leaves me

Vee. Protective, and grieving, and — this is the part I didn't expect a year of alphas to leave me capable of feeling — a little complicit. I've stopped hoping for her rescue, and I want you to understand that's not the same as not caring. It's worse. Somewhere around the shirt chapter, the one where she's alone in the dorm and realizes the warmth in the flannel was only ever her own body heat given back — "he wasn't in it" — and she takes it off and lies bare in the cold *on purpose*, I stopped rooting for someone to pull her out and started just wanting to be in the room with her. Because by the end she's not the mouse anymore. She's staffed. She crops her own parents out of the true picture and calls it sparing them, she rewrites her own fights so she gets to be the forgiver, she builds the man ways to never have to say the word and calls it her nerve. She drove back to that door and handed him her whole naked self so he wouldn't have to spend a single syllable. She won the argument in chapter 45 — she *won* — and it changed nothing, and she went back. Right now I feel about Vee the way you feel about a friend who's radiantly happy inside something you can see the shape of and she can't. Tender, and sick, and unable to say the thing that would only cost me her.

Pace. Furious, and it's the specific fury of a man who gave me no clean exit. My one red line the whole book was: if this collapses into "the dom was simply right, she just needed to submit emotionally too," I'm gone and I'm burning the book. It never crossed it. He never once explains her body to her — he makes her say it, the hand goes empty the instant she says don't, and that restraint is the whole erotics of him. And then they cracked him open — the hungover New Year's chapter, mopping his own sick, sobbing when her truck didn't turn in, Daphne finally spelled out, the tutor who taught him where to stand and then *left* and there was no one to tell because she was the one he'd have told, so he decided the words are the thing that makes loss fatal — and understanding him made the withholding *hotter instead of forgivable*, and I resent it exactly as much as the jacket wanted me to. So where I land on Pace is not warm. He keeps a receipt. He lays love up brick on brick — the drawer, the dress, the pancakes made-and-not-poured at midnight — and cannot see that the one withheld word is the entire wall, and I don't fully believe he can't; I think part of him won't. The pancakes gutted me worse than any of the sex, because it's the substitution laid bare: she says *you fed me a meal when I'd been eating crackers*, and he answers the meal with an actual meal rather than the four syllables. I understand him completely and I forgive him nothing. That's a genuinely uncomfortable place to be left and I'd rather be there than bored.

Randi. This is the one that surprised me. I can't hate her, and by the end I understand her fully, and *that* is the worst of the three, not the best. The Gstaad chapter did it — the perfect safe man, the fairytale she texts Vee that's every word true and none of it the thing, and underneath it she's lying there cold everywhere she isn't touching him, thinking *Pace on his worst night reaches further than this man at his best.* She's the daughter of the fraud and the exquisite cruel mother, raised where the invented boyfriends were always safer than the real ones, and she's in real love with Vee — the realest thing she's ever felt — and she's handling that love with the exact tools she uses on a mark because they're the only tools anyone ever gave her. She runs Pace's own method on Vee, the leave-the-sentence-hanging, make-her-finish-it, walk-the-shame-into-wanting. She's the same machine pointed at the same girl from the other side. And she's *bleeding* — the crack on "like he wants me, with the body attached, not the other way around." So I feel toward Randi something close to heartbreak, which is an absurd thing to feel about your villain, and I think it's the book's single biggest achievement that I do.

T2 — what it refused me / what it still owes

Yes. Several things, and I want to separate the ones I think are deliberate withholding from the ones I think are the book getting away with something.

The deliberate withholding I'll allow, even admire: nobody learns the gesture was built. The whole back half is engineered — Randi scripts the apology entrance, takes credit for the bartender's honest work, builds the coat-with-nothing-underneath grand finale — and it all runs flawlessly and closes over warm and glad, and not one of the three ever finds out. The Randi-thing in Vee's own body never gets a reckoning; it went from panic in the practice-room stall to just *weather* she lives in and doesn't flag. That's cruel and I think it's on purpose. That's the jacket's promise — "every yes was freely given, that was the problem" — kept to the letter. I'm not owed that resolution *yet*.

But here's what it actually refused me and I'm still owed: **a witness who lands a hit that sticks.** Sheri named it to Pace's face — "not saying it matters more to you than having her, and from this seat that feels pretty shitty" — and he looked down, and then a friend fed him and let him set the heavy thing further off, and nothing changed. Cassie — my clean compass, the one person with no angle — fired the scalpel *once* ("is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to?"), got a "both," and then was literally removed from the room, sent away for the weekend, asleep through the nights Vee couldn't sleep. The book kept making her absence *mean* something, which bought it a lot of patience from me, but it has now cashed that trick enough times that it owes me Cassie awake in the right minute, once, before I'll believe she's a character and not a structural device for loneliness.

And a real live wire it planted and left lying: Cassie's dad got laid off in the exact fraud Randi's father ran, and the three of them are sitting in one row of a graded group project, and Vee is holding that secret. That's a loaded gun on the mantel and Volume One ended without firing it. I want it.

The stuff I think the book is quietly getting away with — the recipe arias (there is only so much toasted-spice-and-reduced-sauce a person can take, and it only ever came alive when the food went *bleak*), the reveal-by-hanging-sentence run three or four times until I could see the gear turning, the same interior loop of the-word-she-can't-say — those aren't debts, exactly. But they're the seams starting to show, and a book this smart doesn't get to pretend I can't see them.

T3 — "Pace deceives her the whole volume and ends loved, trusted, and unexposed. Nothing lands on him."

Okay. I want to push on your premise a little, because it's not quite the deal I think the book made — and then I want to grant you the part that's completely fair, because it is.

First, the pushback. "Nothing lands on him" isn't true from the inside. Chapter 49 is a whole chapter of it landing — him hungover and alone, eating food he doesn't want against his own hunger, weeping because a truck didn't turn into his drive, understanding in grey light that at the exact moment she asked him to be brave he was weak. The problem — and this is the real knife, sharper than the one you're holding — is that *she'll never get that chapter.* The reader gets his confession; Vee never does. He gets the diner and the friend and the absolution; she gets the twin bed she outgrew in middle school. The asymmetry isn't the book failing to punish him. The asymmetry *is* the punishment the book is describing — his, and hers, and the gap between them is the whole thesis. So it's not that nothing lands on him. It's that what lands on him is private and survivable and what lands on her is public and shapeless, and he has arranged his entire life, since Daphne, to keep it exactly that way.

Now the part where you're dead right, and where I share your problem: he ends the volume *rewarded* for the deception, materially. Loved, trusted, back in her bed, the machine reset — "those stay on," the heels, the second she's soft he takes the reins back. The book let me understand him so thoroughly that it flirted, in 49, with letting his grief make the withholding romantic, and it held the accountability by a single thread ("he'd hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him") and no more. If I'm honest, the thing that keeps me from resolving your objection into a clean "yes, that's a flaw" is the same thing that keeps me up: I genuinely cannot decide whether what's being done to Vee is the most romantic or the most sinister thing I've read this year, and I've come to suspect that *never being allowed to decide is the actual drug the book is selling.* That's either a triumph or a cheat and I can't tell which, and I've read fifty-one chapters and I still can't tell, which is itself kind of the answer.

Does it change whether I buy Book Two? No. I'm buying it. But let me tell you *exactly* what I'm buying it on, because it's conditional and I want it on the record so you can hold me to it.

I'm not buying it because I trust Pace gets his. I'm buying it on the collateral the book has already put down: Randi is bleeding, Pace *cracked* on New Year's, and the jacket promised this becomes "as real for the two who started it as for the girl" — and Volume One is structurally the top of the arc, the trap fully closed, everyone warm and glad and lying. That's a spring wound all the way tight. Something in Book Two has to touch the outside world — the fraud, Cassie, the photo that's now out in Randi's hand, the collision Sheri named — because the whole first volume is hermetic, nothing external allowed to land except a cop siren that swerves past, and you cannot hold a book airless for two volumes.

Here's the line, though, and it's the same line it's been the whole way: if Book Two lets the deception cost him *nothing* — if he stays unexposed and unchanged and the book starts agreeing with him that the word doesn't need saying because look how good they are together — then it will have curdled into the exact thing your objection accuses it of, and I'll DNF it and feel stupid for the goodwill. And separately, if Book Two runs the same gears — the hanging sentence, the recipe aria, Vee circling a word for another three hundred pages — I'm out on boredom, not principle. So it's a yes with a knife on the table. The reason it's a yes at all is that this is the first book in years that gave me the heat *and* made me argue with it at one in the morning about consent and complicity and whether understanding a man is the same as excusing him. That's the thing I've been starving for. I'll pay to find out if it has the nerve to make him bleed where she can see it.
