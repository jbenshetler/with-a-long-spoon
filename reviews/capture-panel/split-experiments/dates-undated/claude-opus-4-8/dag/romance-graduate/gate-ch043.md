Oh, this one's a knife, and it waited until I thought I was safe. Chapter 42 was the warm bath — the dying shirt, the five nights, the pizza coming apart on her tongue into every separate flavor, him wearing two shirts in his own kitchen because she told him to. I let myself float in it. I knew the book was spending down its warmth on purpose and I floated anyway, because the writing earned it. And then 41 had already slid the knife in and I half let it go past me the first time, the way Vee did — MIRA, MIRANDA, the point off alone in the white, up and to the right, the biggest and ugliest one, the loans that went up while the payrolls went down. Randi's father. "Cheating bastards," two words with no clause to fold the plural into, aimed too near to be meant for strangers. And Vee *saw* her see it and then did the one tender, fatal thing — gave her somewhere to put her hands and never said the name. God. That's the pool chapter in reverse: a wall took the truth before Vee could keep it, except this time she chose to drop it, out of love. The kindest instinct she has is the exact mechanism that keeps the box sealed. The chapter title "The Outlier" — the point on the graph, and Randi, and Vee herself, all of them sitting off by themselves pretending they're on the line.

But 43 is the one I'd text about. Because here's the thing I didn't see coming: the title is "Cropped" and it's not about the photo at all, or it is, but it's about *her whole life now being a cropped image.* The version her parents have "stops at the waist." Her mother touched her own collarbone and then touched Vee's and said she'd gotten thin — oh, that's my note from way back, the weight she never asked to lose, and now her mother's nurse-face is clocking it from across a dinner table and asking if she's making herself sick, sitting on the end of the bed, and Vee hands her *dancing.* She lied to her mother. "How many times have you actually gone dancing?" "Once." That exchange gutted me. Cassie doesn't moralize it, just lays the fact on the table the way she lays everything — and Vee's defense is "How am I supposed to tell her it's the sex? The amount of sex?" which is funny and true and also the sound of a woman discovering she has no legible story for her own life.

And then the sewing. *He made the dress.* I did not have that — I thought the dress was bought, the expensive-gift read. He sewed it on a machine on his sun porch because she mentioned a magazine once. "My father would take it better if I told him Pace takes that dress off me than that he made it." That line reorganized the whole man for me. The air guitar, the drawer, the scrunchies, and now a sewing machine and a dress made by hand from a photo she couldn't afford — this is the most intimate, most devoted thing anyone has done in the book, and it's also the most *concealing*, because it's unrepeatable to anyone outside the house. Everything he does for her is both the realest love and the thing that cannot be said out loud to another living soul. Same organ. The love and the isolation are the identical act. She's walling herself in with gifts and calling the wall warmth.

What breaks me is the end. "So what is he. To you." And she has *nothing that fits in Cassie's frame.* Not the word, not the story, not a version of herself that stands up cleanly. "Is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to?" "Both. Probably both." That's the pool again, that's four underwater strokes of almost-seeing — except now she's being handed the question directly, by the one clean instrument in the book, the one person routed around the grid on purpose, and she still turns her face to the dark window. Cassie didn't push. Vee was grateful. "She was also, a little, not." THAT. That little clause is the whole tragedy — some buried part of her wants to be pushed, wants someone to pry the box open from outside, and the one person who could is too gentle to do it, and Vee registers the disappointment and files it. Gentleness is exactly what won't save her. I wrote that in my notes forty chapters ago and the book just proved it to my face.

And I'm sitting here holding what Vee can't: she's now carrying Randi's secret (the father, the fraud) *without knowing Randi carries a far bigger one about her.* Two women at that library table, each holding a thing about the other, Vee holding hers out of love, Randi holding hers out of a con that became love. The symmetry is almost unbearable. The bill the jacket promised — I can feel it accruing in every tender scene. This book keeps advancing the hurt while the shape stays warm, and I'm all the way in.
