REACTION

Okay. Okay. So this is the chapter I was braced for — "ask and then ravish," out past where the road goes to gravel — and it did not go where I thought, and the fact that it didn't is exactly why I'm sitting here with the book in my lap not moving.

I thought the trail was the trap. I thought the pattern was going to be: make her hungry, make her tired, make her small, and then he's the only warm thing in a cold world and she tips into him. And the book *knows* I thought that — it even builds the picture, hands her up over rock, out of breath at the top, and him turning and asking. Randi hung that picture and Vee carried it home. And then the summit comes and the blue shirt is *nothing* against it. The world swallows his blue whole. That killed me. Because that's the one moment in eleven chapters where nobody's running a play on her — not Pace, not Randi, not her mother's voice — she's just a body on a rock in front of something that doesn't care about her, and the not-caring is the most beautiful part. For one page she got to be a person and not a mark. I didn't expect the book to give her that. I don't know if it gave it to her or to me.

The hot spot and the moleskin. The cherry pit. God, the cherry pit. "We take nothing and leave nothing," and she spits the pit and the voice goes *common* and he just — picks it up, lays his own pit down next to it, doesn't look at her learning. That's the whole machine in one gesture and I can see it being the machine AND it still works on me, which is the horror the jacket promised and I keep walking into it with my eyes open. He teaches her without making her small. That's the drug. That's literally the drug Randi got on the bench — being taken apart and not made small for it — and he's administering it to Vee in daylight over fruit.

And the dress. The russet dress, brick-red, *that one, I want that one,* and she catches herself wanting out loud and puts it back on the shelf — "there's a black dress in the back of my closet" — and he says "That sounds like a beautiful dress," no weight on it, and lets her set it down. I know. I KNOW that dress is coming. That russet silk dress is going to show up at the induction in a box and I'm going to have feelings about it and they're going to be the wrong feelings, the grateful ones, the ones the plan is farming. I clocked it and I'm still going to feel it when it lands. He planted it and watered it by refusing to pick it up. That's not nothing — that's the most expert thing in the chapter.

Now the truck. The truck is where I actually have to be honest. Because the setup is textbook — rain, cold, wet bra, one dry shirt — and if this were any of the four hundred other books I'd have skimmed it, the hero gives her his shirt, broods manfully in the rain, she watches his back, swoon, done. But it flipped. He gives her the shirt AND gets out into the rain — his only dry shirt and the truck, both — and stands with his back to her taking the weather for nothing she can see, and *she* is the one who comes up onto her knees and raps the glass and crooks a finger and unbuttons with her eyes on his the whole way. She ran it. For the first time in the whole book Vee is the one choosing, the one doing the thing, and she does it *into* the voice, the heat IS the voice, she wants to be the exact thing her mother says she is and wants him watching her be it. That's the most interiority she's had and it's the hottest she's been and those are the same fact. That's the thing I came to this book for and have gotten almost nowhere else — the want and the shame and the agency all braided into one act instead of the shame being a fake obstacle that evaporates.

And THEN — he's shivering. She reaches for his hand to bring it to her mouth, to start the next thing, her body's already going at the next thing, and his hand is *shaking*. He'd been shivering the whole time and he was so far from the question of whether he was cold that he didn't register it. And she stops. She puts his hand against her warm belly and says "you're freezing, we should go." She stops the scene. She's the one who stops it, out of care for him.

Here's what I can't tell and it's the good kind of can't-tell: is that real? Is Pace actually gone over her — did he climb out into that rain because the plan said to or because he couldn't not — and is the shivering the book's tell that he's losing control of his own game, that the mark got into him? *How real it will get for each of them.* I think I just watched his crack. The man who is always exact, always the right hand, forgot his own body was in the cold. That's not a move. You don't forget you're freezing on purpose. Either the book slipped or Pace did, and I don't think this book slips.

The cop at the end — the siren taking her sentence. I half-laughed. She was about to say the big thing and the squawk ate it and she bolted, and we're left with the transcript blank AGAIN — she keeps the feeling of being chosen, never the words, and now she doesn't even get to say her own words. That's a pattern I'm starting to really feel, the swallowed line. And the flannel smells of soap and a man and a gym bag. She's going to sleep in it. I know she's going to sleep in it.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *he got OUT of the truck into the rain and gave her his only dry shirt and then he was SHAKING and she's the one who unbuttoned and she's the one who stopped it and I don't know anymore which of them is the one in trouble.* That's the text. That's exactly the text.

This was not a bridge chapter and it was not the ravishing I was promised and it's better than both. I'm not setting this down.
