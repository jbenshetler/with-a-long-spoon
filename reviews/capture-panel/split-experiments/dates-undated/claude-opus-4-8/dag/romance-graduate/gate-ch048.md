REACTION

Oh, this is the one. This is the chapter the whole book has been owed, and I didn't know I was owed it until it landed.

I've spent forty-seven chapters being a step ahead of Vee and watching Randi aim everything at her like a weapon, and I'd gotten to pity — the "say something nice to me," the "it frightens me." But I didn't have the *floor* of her until now. And the book did the smartest possible thing: it didn't give me Randi crying in a therapy office. It gave me a fairytale. The literal fairytale. The chalet, the snow, the stranger in the lift line doing the married-couple bit — and I was *charmed*, God help me, I was charmed the exact way she was, "play along," the invented children sent to ski camp, "you're already in it, you're whispering." The prose let me fall for John right alongside her, which is the trap, because then the back half guts it.

The skiing. "He had the run to spare and spent none of it proving it." That's him skiing and it's also Pace, and the book knows it, and it's letting Randi do the comparison in real time without underlining it too hard. And then the dance flips it — John *leads*, firm and unmistakable, and Pace's lead "was the lightest pressure, a thing she'd had to be listening for and good enough to catch." Easier was better, she tells herself, I'm done with the work. And every single one of us reading knows that's the lie she's going to live inside. The work was the thing. The work was the only thing that ever reached her.

And then — the sandwich. "It had meant no more than a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon." After this whole book of *food* as the metaphor for being truly fed, the meal versus the crackers, Vee tasting every separate thing on the pizza two chapters ago — Randi gets the perfect man and he's a sandwich. "She was warm only where she touched him." The cold is hers, carried in. "She had put nothing on the table and had won exactly that." That's the whole engine of her. That's the sorority smile, the invented boys safer than the real ones, "at this table." Her parents, the mother all edges and points, the father pricing suitors — I finally see the room she learned to disappear in.

"Pace went straight to her. That was the whole terror of him." And then — *and then* — "Something came off Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday that this room could not counterfeit one thread of." I knew it was both of them but I didn't know she knew it this cleanly. She can run every scenario to the end except Vee's face when she tells her. That broke me a little. The grifter who's never said "I love you" without it being currency, and with Vee "there was neither," and she's too much a coward to risk it, so she'll keep the con running *to keep the one real thing she has*. That's not evil anymore. That's a drowning person holding the thing that's drowning her.

And the texts at the end. Vee from the warm Ohio kitchen — "i'm here if you want to talk. or not." — and Randi giving her the fairytale back, every word true, none of it the thing, "miss your face most though, gorgeous." Facedown phone. Back into the borrowed warmth.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *the villain chapter turned out to be the saddest chapter and now I don't know who to be afraid for anymore.* This is exactly what I came to this book for — it gave me a whole interior, a whole childhood, a whole theory of why a person chooses safe over seen, and it did it through snow and a sleigh and a sandwich instead of telling me. And it reframes the con: Randi isn't running Vee to win, she's running her because telling the truth is the one risk she's structurally incapable of.

Only thing holding me at a slight arm's length: it's a flashback, so it's a held breath, not a forward step. Vee is still out in the cold at the end of 47 and I was braced to see where *that* goes, and instead I got pulled sideways into the past. Worth it — completely worth it — but my body is still reaching for the fallout of "words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me." I want back to the present now, hard.
