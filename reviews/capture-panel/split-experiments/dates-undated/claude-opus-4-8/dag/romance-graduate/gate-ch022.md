REACTION

Okay so chapter 21 was the dress, and I came out of it drunk — the relay of it, the "covered in a lot of me," him sucking her own slick off her fingers with the dress held up out of the way, the whole ridiculous tender farce of two people trying not to ruin a thing he'd built all night. I'd have texted a friend at 1 a.m. just the words *he made it TO her* except I didn't have those words yet. And then this chapter hands them to me. Randi hands them to me. "He didn't make this *for* you. He made it *to* you." And the book knows it, because Vee has already stood in that sun porch and cried over exactly that without a word for it, and now the word lands with Randi's fingers on the shoulder seam. That's the thing that still gets me about this book — it lets the feeling happen first and the naming come later, in someone else's mouth, and the naming is a second seduction.

Because that's what this whole chapter is. It's a seduction and it's wearing the costume of a girls' shopping day. And I SAW it coming and I still felt it work, which is the only thing I ever want from a book anymore.

The stats lecture. Simpson's paradox. Oh, come on — that's the cleverest the book has been with its own machinery since the error-correcting codes, and it didn't explain itself once, which is why I'll forgive it. Each year on its own, one man's the better hitter. Put the years together and it flips. Same numbers, nobody touched one of them. That IS the book. Every single lunch, every yes, every afternoon, looks one way inside its own frame — a kind man, a chosen thing, a dress, a friend who solves your shoe problem. Aggregate them and the answer reverses and nobody touched a number. And it's sitting up on the board behind her while both her friends have turned their backs on the professor to watch what the dress did to her. The professor's little red dot on the one number at the foot of the column — that's me, that's my dread, pointing at the thing none of them is looking at.

And Cassie. God, Cassie. Cassie didn't go down into the numbers, but Cassie didn't go down into the *dress* either — "Cassie did not care about any dress; she had worn the same fleece through two winters." Cassie only ever watched the friend. "Was it a good night?" And when Vee says *oh, yes*, something eases in Cassie's face and she picks the highlighter back up. That's the one clean love in this book and it's still clean here, and that's almost the scariest part, because Cassie reads the made bed and the real answer and she's satisfied, she's happy for her, she has no idea she's the only instrument in the room still pointed the right way and she's standing down. Nobody's watching the door. I wrote that after 20 and it's worse now.

The shoe day itself — I need to be honest, I was grinning through the cheap-store part. The yeti shoe, the pink bedazzled platforms, "do yetis shop here," the flat that's one brisk walk from becoming a sandal. That's genuinely funny, it's two women being silly, it's the warmest the book has been and I felt the warmth and I wanted it for Vee. And THAT is exactly the problem, because the whole bright silly stretch is the setup for the turn, and the turn is vicious in how gentle it is.

"What size?" "Seven." "That's me. That's me exactly." Same feet, same everything, *practically a marriage.* And I went cold. Because that's not a cute coincidence the book threw in, that's Randi closing a door on Vee that Vee thinks is opening. We can share. And then — "which is exactly why you're not buying that." The tan shoe. The sensible one. Goes with everything. "A dress a man sat up nights making doesn't want to go with everything." She's using *Pace* to move Vee off her own money. She's using the thing Vee loves most to walk her into a shop with no prices on the shelves.

And the money. Oh, the money is where I actually clenched. Vee doing the TJMaxx math, the most-I-could math, and the number on the sole starting past where her most ended and *kept going.* And she can't say it, can't stand in the hush and tell Randi she can't afford the door. That's the realest, most painful interiority in the chapter — the going-under of not being able to say I don't have this. And then the credit card. She puts the debit back. The card that only spends what's behind it goes back in the wallet and the emergency card comes out, and the book is SO clear-eyed about it: "There was nothing under it. She knew that even while she did it." She's going into debt she can't pay to be owed-to by a dress. "They were what the dress was owed." That sentence is the mother's voice turned all the way inside out — she used to portion herself down to apple slices, and now she's overspending herself up to match a gown, and both of them are the same disease, which is not trusting that she gets to just *want a thing and have it.*

Meanwhile Randi buys loafers that cost a multiple of Vee's white-knuckle shoes without looking at the number, mid-story about a coat, and walks out in them. The book doesn't editorialize. It just lets the two cards sit on the counter next to each other and lets me do the subtraction. That's the restraint I stayed for.

The foot. The *foot.* Randi down on one knee on the pale floor — "I've got her" — and instead of reaching for the box she holds Vee's foot in both hands and looks at the toes. The frosted plum. The polish Vee chose weeks ago to catch the light the way the silk does — the polish she painted *for Pace* — and Randi's thumb moves once across the nail of the big toe, light, and it goes all the way up the length of Vee and the floor of her tips and her hand shoots out to Randi's shoulder to stay upright. "Mm." That's all. And Randi looks up and smiles and says nothing and bends back to the box. That's Randi running the exact same play she runs with the lunches — open one door, draw her open, shut it soft at the peak. "You don't have to tell me." Except it's not words this time, it's a thumb on a toe, and it's the first time the con has touched Vee's actual skin with want in it, and Vee *felt it as want* and her body answered and she still filed it under friendship. "Vee never lets herself notice how much she wants Randi too" — the jacket told me, and here it is, literal, a swing of the room while she's sitting down.

And the thing that twists it is Vee's generosity. She clocks Randi gone quiet at the shoulder seam and reads it as jealousy — *of him*, of a man who sits up nights — and she wants it for Randi so hard it hurts. "Randi, with her beautiful boys in a hurry and nobody who ever sat up a night over anything of hers." Vee pitying Randi. Vee wanting love for the woman hunting her. I could scream. That's the cruelest the dramatic irony has been and it's built entirely out of Vee being kind.

What I'm suspicious of, fully, now: the shoe size. "We can share." That's not flavor. That dress and those shoes are going to be on Randi's body before this is over, or they're going to be the reason the three of them end up in a room, and the sharing is going to stop being about footwear. Randi said "practically a marriage" and I don't think the book put that word there by accident any more than it put Simpson's paradox on the board by accident.

Where I'm at with it: this was a quieter chapter than 21 by design — no sex, a comedown, a bridge. But it's the good kind of quiet, the kind where the plot is being walked three steps closer to the cliff under cover of laughing about yetis. I was never bored. I never reached for the wall. If anything I'm more wound than I was after the dress, because the dress was joy and this was the bill for the joy coming due in a currency Vee can't read yet — money, and want, and a best friend she's never had who is the one setting the trap. The line that'll keep me up: "same numbers, nobody touched one of them." I want the induction so badly now I can taste it. Three of them, one room, a dress made *to* her and shoes that fit the other woman exactly.
