**REACTION**

Okay. I'm sitting here a little wrecked, and a little annoyed at being wrecked, and I can't fully separate the two.

The opening worked on me. Her hangover is told well: the water on its folded paper towel so it won't mark the wood, the night coming back "out of order, the way a dropped deck comes back — not the game, just the cards," his mouth on her forehead "after he thought she was already gone." The bread. A man baked cinnamon raisin bread from scratch while she slept off three ciders. I laughed at "You made *bread.*" It's domestic in a way I'd normally roll my eyes at, but it's specific enough that I didn't.

"Sheri's my friend. My one friend. There isn't anyone else." That landed in my stomach two ways at once, and the second one is why I'm still up.

The first way is the one the book wants. The friendship-window speech made me sad. "Men make friends by competing… I was never once in the pack." "That door's on a timer." "Every one of those has a gap under it." He went to college at fifteen, had a patent at eighteen, and had nobody his own age in any room. Then there's "weren't safe or weren't welcoming," and no name, and Sheri's "not home since he was sixteen" falling into place. Vee crossing the kitchen without saying sorry and holding him the way Sheri did, whole weight committed, got me. Him setting his chin on her head and looking past her, "not happy and not sad," was the best image in the chapter.

The second way is that I read chapter 61. Two chapters ago this man was underneath Randi while she told him what she wanted to do with Vee's breast in her mouth. Now he tells Vee, flat and honest-faced, that he has one friend and "there isn't anyone else." Maybe Randi isn't a "friend." Maybe she's a lover, a co-conspirator, whatever the word for that is. That might technically be true, and it's still the most loaded sentence he's said in the book. Vee is planning to spend Tuesday giving Randi the "win with both hands," and Randi already knows everything. The dramatic irony is thick enough to stand a spoon in. I love it and it makes me queasy, and that's exactly where this book keeps putting me.

Now the complaints, because I have them. This is the second quiet chapter in a row, and I felt it. Sixty-two had real events: a new person, a reveal, a whole night. Sixty-three is mostly Vee doing math in her head about his life. The paragraph where she sets eighteen-year-old Pace into her own freshman year, with the dorm carpet and the trays, ran long. I could feel the book walking me through grief I'd already gotten from his three sentences. Then there's "He set it in good order and the good order was the tell." Don't TELL me it's the tell. I saw it. That's the explain-the-subtext thing I hate, and it showed up exactly where the chapter was strongest.

The Daphne paragraph unsettled me in a way that I think was intended. "The ugly grown-up word" she keeps pressing back down. The book is circling a grooming story about a fifteen-year-old and won't name it, through Vee who won't name it. That's character-consistent, since Vee is a professional not-namer now ("Vee, I l—"). But it means two big things are now sitting unspoken under this couple.

Heat-wise, nothing, and I didn't need any, because ch61 is still burning a hole in my head. But I'm now two chapters out from anything with a body in it, and I'm aware of the clock.
