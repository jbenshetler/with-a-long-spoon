**REACTION**

Okay. I'm lying here a little stunned, and not the way I expected.

For the first third I was settling into a nice chapter. Robe, quilt, fire, tea gone cold because they're too busy talking to drink it. His hand on her breast "as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm." That's the kind of casual intimacy I came for. The Ohio stuff made me laugh out loud. "Green beans improved by bacon until the vegetable had surrendered its own case." The saffron "old enough to vote." Vee is *funny*, and the book finally lets her be funny with him, not just with Randi. Then he says "And affectionate" about the casseroles, and she notices he goes first to the hand underneath the thing. I melted a little. That's a man with actual emotional intelligence, and the book showed it instead of telling me about it.

Then "My real name is Peter." Flat, mother gone from the answer, and she lets him have that. Good. Then the desk in the hall: "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment." She doesn't say sorry, she just presses her shoulder against his arm once. These are two adults handling each other with real care, and it's the thing I can never find.

I'll be honest, the math section almost lost me. The paint shape, the coastline, Newton's three sentences: it's lovely, and I get why he's lit up, but "Most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it" is the book reaching for a quotable. I felt the chapter turn into a TED talk for about a page. I skimmed the coastline. I came back for "It was not arrogance in his voice. It was almost grief," because *that* line feeds me.

Then Daphne.

Nineteen and fifteen. Two years. Assigned to him. "Nothing happened. Nobody did anything wrong." I went cold all over. The book had been sitting on Daphne since early on, and I assumed she'd be an ex. She's not an ex. She's the thing that happened to him, and he calls it the best two years of his life and puts himself between the memory and the word. I loved that Vee holds the word "under her tongue" and doesn't say it. It's not hers to say. That's exactly right, and it hurt. (The book got one beat close to explaining itself: "the good order was the tell." I'd already caught it, thanks. But it was a small slip.)

Here's what has me texting at 1 a.m.: **an older student, assigned to help the young one find his feet.** That is what Randi is doing to Vee. A more experienced person deciding what a newcomer is ready for, arranging it, curating it. Pace was *chosen* once, by someone older who probably thought she was being tender, and now he's on the other side of the same structure and can't see it. He doesn't even hear himself defending Daphne. Of course he doesn't hear himself defending the game. Suddenly the whole book is about this, and I'm a little scared of how smart it might be.

And the scar. Her hand on it, his hand pressing hers down. "Not tonight," a door with a time on it instead of a wall. Palms together over the scar. I'm giving that a 3 for the bond. That's a turn.

Then the ending wrecked me. "She had stumbled into her." "For no reason she had done anything to deserve." "She had never been so fortunate, or so happy." Vee, sweetheart, you didn't stumble. The bump outside the dining hall, Randi in the seat next to you the next morning, the man crossing the coffee shop ten days later. I read that last paragraph knowing every one of those was placed, and she's lying there calling it one piece, like the leaf and the stars. It's devastating irony, and the book trusts me to feel it without pointing.

Also, one quieter thing from 23 that I'm still holding: Randi cleaned her plate. After the apple bowl, that felt huge. And here Pace is the one being fed: understood, read exactly, given back warmer. Everybody in this book is starving for something different.

Heat-wise this was a simmer, and that's fine. Chapter 21 earned me a talking chapter. It's two quiet-ish chapters out of three now (22, 24), so I'm watching for that. But this one wasn't recap. Everything in it was new, and it changed how I see him.
