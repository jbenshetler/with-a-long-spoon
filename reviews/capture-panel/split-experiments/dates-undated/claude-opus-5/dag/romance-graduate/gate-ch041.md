REACTION

A statistics group project. I read the first paragraph and thought *oh no.* A man clicking a slide forward and back like he's checking the pressure in a tire, saying *exploratory data analysis* out loud, and me three hundred pages into a book about a girl being taken apart in a warm house in the woods. I nearly skimmed to the next white space. And then forty minutes later I'm sitting here with my hand over my mouth because that rote little man handed them the murder weapon in his own dull voice and nobody in the room heard it but me: *there's the main body of the thing, and then the points off by themselves, and you can set those aside if you can account for them.*

The chapter is called The Outlier and it is twenty-five percent of the grade.

But first — Cassie's in the class. Cassie's been in the class since September. I had her written out. I wrote a whole paragraph in my own notes about the girl cracking the window two inches beside an empty bed while the other two went off to Tuesdays and Thursdays in the dark, and here she is on Vee's right-hand side, four months of the same three seats, Vee in the middle without discussion, the texts that assume the other two. Randi on the left with a latte she isn't drinking, because of course she isn't. Cassie with nothing in her hands at all *because Cassie didn't carry things into rooms she meant to walk out of* — I'd have bought the book for that sentence alone. And then the two of them put their fathers on the table within a minute of each other and the floor opened.

*My dad got laid off in 2020.* Flat. No handle left out for anyone to pick it up by. God.

And Randi — this is what's wrecking me — Randi wasn't lying. She wasn't covering. She thought she was *right.* She said it with the ease of a girl who'd grown up inside the thing they were about to graph, she said *we'll see* and let it go, **certain,** and then she drove the last half hour herself because she was aiming the whole picture at home. She wanted to hand Cassie the bright counterexample. She went looking for her father to prove him good and found him up and to the right, alone in the white, the biggest point in the county. Four letters of her own shorthand. MIRA. And then MIRANDA.

*Cheating bastards.*

Two words with nothing let out after them. No *those*, no *these*, no clause to fold the plural down into something a person could hold. I read it three times. That's a woman watching herself get included in a sentence she was trying to say about strangers. Nothing worn smooth with use, aimed too near to be meant for anyone else in the room. And the only thing I know about Randi Thorne-adjacent — that she will not let anyone give her anything, that she won't let Vee near a check, that she takes one bite of the pastry and says *delicious* in a way that closes the subject — just got a floor under it that I didn't have before and did not want. She doesn't eat in front of people. Her father's money is the payroll of a county.

And then **Cassie says the cruelest true thing in the book without knowing she's holding a knife.** *That's not the exception. That's just the one that's easy to see.* To Cassie those two words were agreement. She couldn't see the screen. And Randi has to *nod at her.* There was nothing else to give it, so she gave it a nod and closed the record with one keystroke. I will be thinking about that nod for a week.

But the thing that actually turned the book over: **Vee saw.** For the first time in two volumes, Vee is holding something about Randi that Randi doesn't know she's holding. *Randi didn't look at her. Randi had no idea she'd been looked at.* That is the exact shape of the mirror on the couch wall — Pace watching Randi's face while she talked, not telling her, keeping asking. Now both of them have done it to her. Both of the people who love her have an unreported look at her face from the one angle she thought she'd hidden. And of the three of them, the only one who got that look and used it to be *kind* was the twenty-one-year-old from Ohio who slid the laptop a few inches toward herself and started in on the write-up to give her friend somewhere to put her hands. That's not a girl being managed. That's the best thing anybody's done for anyone in this book and nobody will ever know she did it.

And then, and *then*, she gets it wrong in the one way she always gets it wrong. *Not that she let herself think about it much.* Dealt a rotten thing by a man she had the luck to be the daughter of, no hand in it, none of it earned, and the kindest thing a friend can do is not make her carry it in front of anyone. Which is grace, and I'd want it extended to me, and it is also — again, again — Vee deciding at speed not to find out. She didn't say the name. She didn't look back at the point. Three chapters ago she licked a stranger's glaze off her own lip and asked herself nothing. She sat down, she did not find out, and now she's gotten *good* at it, good enough that it looks like compassion from the outside. It might even be compassion. It's also the skill that's going to keep her in that house until the walls come in.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: THE PROFESSOR TOLD THEM THEY COULD SET ASIDE THE OUTLIERS IF THEY COULD ACCOUNT FOR THEM. Vee is the one who builds the model. Cassie is the one who pulled the data and will know exactly how many points there are. Cassie's dad got laid off in 2020. Does Vee drop the biggest point in the county to spare the woman on her left, with the laid-off father's daughter sitting on her right? Twenty-five percent and the only honest graph she'll ever make. I have been complaining for ten chapters that nobody but me was doing the arithmetic on Vee's money, the emergency card with nothing under it, the Benedict she climbed the right-hand column to order — and the book just put the arithmetic on a projector in a cold lecture hall and it turns out the other woman's side of the ledger is worse than mine.

No heat in this one and I didn't want any. Randi's head tipped on her shoulder murmuring something unrepeatable about a boy two rows down was all it needed; that's the three of them before this afternoon, and I don't think I get it back.
