REACTION

Okay. First: the opening page almost lost me. "Finishing, Randi stood, exchanging affections with her parents before excusing herself to go skiing." Who wrote that sentence? That's a synopsis, not a scene — the book dropped into this flat little wire-service register for the whole breakfast and I actually thought *oh no, is this a different hand*. And then two things in that same stiff paragraph went into me like a nail and I forgave all of it. She hugged her father *eating her revulsion*. And: her mother's tennis bracelet playing with the window light, and Randi feeling the plain white gold on her own wrist, **locked**, and keeping it out of the light. That's it. That's the whole woman in one gesture. She wears his thing to her parents' table and she turns her arm so it won't catch the sun. I have been asking for eight chapters what that band is and the book just told me by having her hide it.

And then: *the invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table.* So the Radford boy wasn't a one-off flourish for Vee's benefit. Inventing men is her native tongue — it's what she learned to do at eleven, across a plate of egg whites, for a father who wants to know how many hearts she broke this fall. She hands fiction to the people she can't be seen by, and she's been handing Vee a *little* of it and the real stuff too, which is more than she's ever given anyone, and she still can't say the one sentence. I get her now in a way I didn't last chapter and I don't love what it costs me.

The John section is the cleanest piece of construction in this book and it didn't once feel like a construction while I was in it. He's not a straw man. He's *great*. He's funny on the lift line, he invents three children with her and doesn't miss a beat, he skis better than she does and — God, this is the detail — *he had the run to spare and spent none of it proving it.* He conjures a sleigh. He puts both hands over his heart on the stairs and can't finish the Byron. If this man walked into any of the four hundred books on my Kindle he'd be the hero, and I'd have been happy about it.

And the dance. The *dance.* He leads like he skis, everything in reserve, and she gives up the steering and glides, and she thinks: he's better than Pace. Pace's lead was the lightest pressure, a thing she had to be listening for and good enough to catch, and *it left the rest of the way to her.* And she decides, out loud in her own head, **easier was better, I'm done with the work.** That's a woman choosing the anesthetic in real time and the book just lets her, lets her be sure of it, lets her buy the dress and stand in front of the mirror an hour early inventing a daughter to tell the story to — and then it takes the whole thing away from her in a single paragraph in a bathroom. The pleasure was real, complete, and *gone before the plate was cleared.* A good sandwich eaten fast.

The ring of her lipstick on him. Felt her own mouth do it *from a long way off.* And at the mirror: her mouth gone, the face thinned and smeared, and **nothing had happened to her eyes.** The eyeliner held. Of course it did. I put the book down for a second on that one. The best thing about her, the thing the dress was bought to wake up and give to the room, and it's the one thing in the whole night nothing touched.

She was warm only along the seam where she touched him, and the cold wasn't the room's, *it was hers, she carried it in and she'd carry it out.* And then the last line — she goes back and lies down against him anyway, *in the warmth she could borrow and could not make.* That's the most frightening sentence anyone in this book has produced and she produced it about herself, with the lights on, alone, which is as near to daylight as she's ever going to get. I asked for someone to ask her what was wrong. Nobody did. She asked herself and answered honestly and then got back into bed with the wrong man to be warm. *She had put nothing on the table and had won exactly that.*

(One small gripe: a couple of those lines are a half-inch too tidy — *the flaw was hers, carried into the room the same as the cold*, *that was the whole terror of him.* She's a woman who runs scenarios, so thinking in theses is in character, and I'll allow it. But the book was better three paragraphs earlier when it just showed me the lipstick and said nothing.)

And the Vee of it. She takes the thought out and looks at it — *I could tell her* — and she can run every scenario to the end except Vee's face. She knows Vee wouldn't cut her loose, would be gentle, would find something soft to say, and that they'd never be *this* again, that the hot easy talk would learn to watch itself. She'd be trading the most important hour of her week for a terrified hope. *She was not brave enough to lose her. She had never had to be brave; she had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be.* And then the resolution, which is the most Randi thing imaginable and also a genuine decision made out of love: don't tell her in any way that makes her *decide what to do with it.* Let her arrive at it on her own. Keep the brunches exactly as they are. **Vee hears it one way; Randi means it two.** That's not a handler's plan dressed as tenderness and it's not tenderness dressed as a plan — it's one organ, same as it's been, and I have stopped expecting the book to separate them for me.

*Vee was her gorgeous.* And *she had never once said I love you to anyone without meaning it as currency, and with Vee there was no qualifier.* Hand to God, that line did more for me than any declaration either of these people have managed in forty-eight chapters.

And then the text. Vee, from a warm Ohio kitchen with minutes left in the year, with the fight at that door still bleeding in her — *i'm here if you want to talk. or not.* Nine hundred miles away, and still the only person who offers Randi a door with nothing behind it. And Randi sends her back the chalet, the mountains, *i'm never coming home*, every word of it true and none of it the thing, plus the one line she meant. Everyone in this book tells true things that aren't the thing. Vee did it to her father about the dancing. Pace does it with his hands. It's the house style and it's going to burn the house down.

What I'm mad about, and it's the good kind: chapter 47 ended with that girl crying in the dark and him saying her name once, and now I'm a month downstream in Switzerland and nobody will tell me what happened at that door. Did Pace spend Christmas alone in the warmest house in the county? Did he call? Did he spend a *word*? And — new question, and it has teeth — **what exactly is the arrangement?** Randi went to bed with another man wearing the band that's locked on her wrist, it caught on her hand during the waltz, and not one syllable in this chapter treats that as a problem. Either there's an agreement I've never been shown or there's a thing she's keeping, and I don't know which, and that's a hole the size of the Mercedes.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *the villain just took the perfect man to a white-tie ball in a dress she bought to be remembered in, and came out of the hotel bathroom with her lipstick on his chest and nothing at all behind her eyes, and then got back into bed with him because he was warm, and I have never felt sorrier for anyone in my life.*
