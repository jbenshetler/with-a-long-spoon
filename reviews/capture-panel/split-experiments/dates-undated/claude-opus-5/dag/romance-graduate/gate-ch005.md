REACTION

Oh, Randi's *hand*. That's the whole chapter for me. Twice the cup comes up and no sip happens, and Vee files it as *she's bored, I'm talking too long,* and I wanted to reach into the page and shake her. I've read a hundred books that would have had Randi narrate her own jealousy at me in italics. This one gave me a coffee cup held with both hands a second longer than a sip requires and trusted me to do the rest. I did the rest. I'm still doing it.

And the thing I did not expect: Randi doesn't do her bit. Vee *borrows* it — the name swerve, V.T., like the university, my parents find it funnier than I do — she borrowed it off Randi in the lecture hall and tries it on and it *works*, and then Randi, asked for her own name, says "Randi." One word. Flat. The same flat one-word name Cassie gave her in chapter four. She couldn't do the charm because she was sitting there watching her girlfriend's mouth come out of her new friend's face while her boyfriend looked at that face like the room had narrowed to it. That's the kind of detail that makes me text somebody at 1 a.m. I don't even have a friend reading this yet and I was composing the text.

Cassie, meanwhile, is the only person in this book who isn't playing. "He looked at her the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." Said to Randi's face. And Vee knows exactly whose look that is — the one that slides off Cassie every day — and Cassie named it anyway without a flicker. After "Goodbye, Vee" last chapter I've decided Cassie is the Cassandra and nobody's going to listen to her, and I'm already braced for how that goes.

The math. Two things that won't give on their own, put together at right angles, and they turn into one plain thing that's easy to name. He writes two crossed strokes in the margin and hands her back the pencil. The book did not tell me what that was about. It just let it sit there next to *latent variables* underlined once in her notebook going nowhere. I could kiss it for not explaining.

And Pace — God help me, he works. I came in cynical about him, I still think he's doing something to a twenty-one-year-old she hasn't consented to the shape of, and the *may I* went straight through me anyway. The asked-for-the-pencil-with-his-eyes-first. Taking her "it's pretty hard, it's calculus" seriously instead of correcting her. "You gave fair notice. I took it seriously." Clothes that won't resolve into a type, which is the most seductive thing a man in this genre has done to me in two years — I'm so tired of the label at the chest and the right jacket, and here's a heroine reaching for the shape under a man and not finding one. That's what I want. I want to not already know.

What I'm suspicious of: her own lovely paragraph about what he'd done — the tutor's path, not the mathematician's path, *he kept himself out of it so the solving stayed hers.* I felt the book lean slightly toward my ear there. It earns it back with "the thought did not finish," which is honest — she can't finish those thoughts yet, that's the point — but it used that move three times in ten pages and the third one I saw coming.

The part that actually scared me is how *good* being chosen feels from inside her. She let herself want the phone to light up. She doesn't know there's a plan. I do. And I'm sitting here wanting him to call her, which means they've got me on their side of it, which is precisely the problem.
