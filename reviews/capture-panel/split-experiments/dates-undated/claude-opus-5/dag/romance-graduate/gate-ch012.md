REACTION

Two pages. I have never read a two-page chapter and I didn't know what to do with my thumb at the end of it — it was over before I'd settled in, and then I sat there for a minute anyway, which I think is the answer.

Here's what it did. I have spent eleven chapters wanting to be *in* one of these rooms instead of being handed the residue of it, and this is the room. Vee comes in wrecked and glowing in a shirt that is three sizes too big and is not hers, and the book doesn't bother to tell me that the flannel is the whole chapter, it just puts Cassie's eyes on it in the first line and lets Cassie look. *She let Cassie look.* That's Vee doing something, not having something happen to her, and it's the same muscle she used knocking on the glass. She's learning to be seen on purpose and she is practicing it on her best friend, who is in love with her. God.

And Cassie. Cassie does the bit — blood type, windowless van, *you think* — and then Vee slows the way she slowed at the tree, and the book says *Cassie, who had been doing the dry thing, the file-revising thing, went still and stayed that way.* And then: **Cassie was rapt.** Three words and I had to put the phone down. She's not jealous-watching. She's not plotting. She is *listening to the girl she loves describe a man taking his dry shirt off for her*, and she is rapt, because Vee being happy is apparently stronger in Cassie than Cassie's own wanting, and that is the most generous thing anybody in this book has done so far including the man who stood in the rain.

Then *"Vee," Cassie said finally. Soft. Not "Oh, Vee." Just her name, like setting something down where it wouldn't break.* That's the whole confession and it never becomes a sentence. The book even shows me the version it declined to write — the *oh, Vee*, the pity version, the one with a warning in it — and tells me Cassie chose not to say that one. She had a door open and she closed it herself. And Vee says *I know*, and what Vee thinks she knows and what Cassie means are two entirely different things, and the book does not explain that to me. Thank you. Thank you for not explaining that to me.

*Borrow real boots next time. / I'm keeping the shirt. / Keep the shirt.* Cassie's last line in the chapter is permission. She gives it away. She gives Vee to him in four words and makes it sound like a joke about laundry.

What I'm sitting with at 1 a.m.: Vee told Cassie first. Not Randi. Randi got the dinner story six days late and third-hand-feeling; Cassie got this one the same afternoon, wet jeans still on. And Randi is going to find out that she's not first, and Randi is going to see that flannel, and the flannel is *evidence* — a dry shirt out of a gym bag, given away, not asked back for. Randi is a woman who edited her own vowels. She is going to be able to read that shirt like a lab result. And "he looked at me like I'd handed him something. And he didn't touch me" — that's the sentence that's going to do it, because that's not a technique Randi can file as the plan working. That's him being *glad*, and glad isn't in the brief.

Also: *Rye.* The chapter is named for the bread. Not the mountain, not the shirt. The thing she ate. This book keeps insisting that the real event was the hunger and the food.

The one place I flickered: "I'm reporting what I observed" is a beat too sitcom-quippy for Vee, a borrowed line from a different kind of book. It lasted a second. Everything on either side of it is the real thing.
