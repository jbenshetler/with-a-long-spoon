REACTION

Oh, the *bucket.*

That's what I'll keep out of this whole chapter. He wakes up, the room's grey, his tongue's glued down, and the first thing the house gives him is *the smell of bleach had reached the bedroom.* And then the mop and the bucket sitting in the hall outside the bathroom with a crust of white foam above the waterline. Nobody tells me he threw up. Nobody tells me that at some point in the middle of the night a twenty-two-year-old man who drank alone for the first time in his life got up off the bathroom floor and *mopped,* because a thing out of order is the one thing he can't live in. He steps around it and shuts the door. That's the book I came for. Not a word of explanation, and I had to sit back from the page.

And then it does it again with the freezer. He opens it and the cold rolls out and there are the *rows* — chicken in one column, broccoli in the next, every bag the same as the one behind it, *enough in there to go on like this a long time.* The man who built a tomato sauce down for half an hour and shaved truffle into another woman's life and taught that girl that her mouth had been asleep since birth — alone, he boils bags. *The bowl held everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted.* He woke *her* up and he eats with the sound off. And the old girlfriend who looked into a bowl like that and said *prison food* and they laughed and then quietly stopped, *no part of the world altered by it.* God. He has a whole filing system for people leaving without the world moving. That's the actual wound and the book put it in a freezer instead of a speech.

Sheri. **Sheri.** Five feet of her, two colors of hair, too much makeup worn like a dare, and she eats the double like she rides and she is the first person in fifty chapters to put a bill in front of that man. *"Then not saying it matters more to you than having her."* And then — *"And I'll tell you, from this seat, that feels pretty shitty."* And he looked down. He LOOKED DOWN. He didn't argue it, he didn't reframe it, he didn't take his voice *down* the way he does when he's closing an inch. He just took it. I have been standing in the aisle for twenty chapters screaming that nobody in this book ever asks anybody a question they don't want the answer to, and the one who finally does it is the cyclist who brings a pie she pretends she made.

And she's not alone the way I thought she was alone. Paige. *Junior in the design school, birds mostly, a laugh way too big for her, came from a house that's seven at the table, everybody hollering over everybody. I liked it there.* And it lasted two months because Paige kept picking at the fact that Sheri doesn't talk to her people — *call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas* — and then she kills the sing-song flat and says **"Ironic. Isn't it."** Because she's sitting across from the one man in the county who also doesn't go back, who *also* doesn't explain it, and the two of them don't touch the irony, they just both look at the grain of it. *People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice.* — *Don't waste the good stuff on me.* These two. I'd read a whole book of these two in that booth under the amber glass with the young Elvis and the car on the turntable. And Vee flickered at *her,* in November, over a pie. I said it in my notes and I was right and it still didn't feel good.

But here's the live grenade: **"She find out about the brunette, then?"** Sheri *knows.* Casual, mouth full of fries, like it's weather. And she met Randi — *y'all came in dancing, I hugged you hello, she looked at me like I was one more of your harem applicants. Never did learn my name. But I got to see her.* Randi walked past the only woman in this book who can't be charmed, didn't bother to learn her name, and that woman is going to be at the dance hall the second Saturday in February on **Valentine's weekend** and doesn't care what the redhead's got to say about it. That's not a plot point, that's a fuse with a date on it.

Now. The thing I've been waiting fifty chapters for: he *tells* me why. Daphne. He said it once, to the only person he'd have had to tell that she was gone, and losing her ate his life, and every woman since got everything but the word, and loss stayed survivable. *He had taught himself that the words were the difference.* And so: *he had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him.*

I have two feelings about that and they won't settle. One: it's a *superstition.* It's a twenty-two-year-old genius running a load-bearing bolt theory on a word, and it is exactly, humiliatingly, true to how smart people get stupid — he found a variable and he's been controlling for it for seven years. The porch moment from inside him is devastating: *the true answer had been there, whole and ready, he had felt the words rise* — and then he chose the smaller truth and **he knew while he was saying it that it wouldn't give her what she'd given him, and he watched it reach her and fail, and he let it stand because he was afraid, and because at the moment she asked him to be brave he had been weak.** That's the most honest sentence anyone in this book has produced.

Two: that section is the one place the book stopped trusting me. The Daphne run came out in flat little dossier sentences — *She had been his tutor, then his best friend, his first girlfriend, his first lover, his first love.* — and then the chapter underlined its own thesis twice. I've had fifty chapters of this book handing me the finger coated and gleaming and *raising his eyebrows and saying nothing at all,* and here it suddenly annotated itself. I didn't need the annotation. The bleach did it. The bags of chicken did it.

And the thing I can't forgive, right in the middle of him missing her: he's **still planning the threesome.** Three weeks of silence, her taillights going down through the cut, and the whiskey takes the lid off and what floats up is the kitchen tableau, *further along than last time, because he had been adding to it for weeks.* Vee in his sheet with too much honey in her tea, Randi's feet up on the lacquered seat, the two of them laughing at something he missed. *What he wondered was how she would carry it. Whether the room would ask more of her than she had in her.* Sir. She just stood on your mat with her coat buttoned to the throat and asked you for one word and you gave her *believe what I do* and let her drive off crying, and you're worried whether the *room* will ask too much of her. That's not cruelty. That's worse — it's the same organ that keeps the heat up for hours before she arrives, pointed somewhere she can't see. And Randi's cashmere blanket slid off the bed in the night and he folded it and put it back where it lives, hungover, before his feet were steady.

The truck, though. The truck got me and I knew it was coming and it got me anyway. *His head lifted before he knew he had moved.* The wrong sound — *he knew that at once and whole, as he'd have known a voice that was not hers, and he did not let go of it.* He held onto a tire noise on a county road for one more second on purpose because it was shaped like her. And then the engine didn't slow and the breath caught high and the next one broke and he bent over the table and sobbed *until he could no longer hear the truck, and for a few breaths after.* And then: the coffee had cooled, the chicken had cooled, he wasn't hungry, **he ate anyway.** That's the man entire. He will do the correct maintenance on a body he isn't living in. He'll do everything except pick up the phone.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *the one who finally told him the truth was the woman with the bleached roots who steals his fries, and he CRIED AT A TRUCK, and he's still boiling bags of chicken in a house with a pizza stone in the oven, and I am going to lose my mind if someone doesn't dial a phone in the next ten pages.*
