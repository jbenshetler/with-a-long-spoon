REACTION

She asked. She finally, actually asked, and she did it with her coat buttoned to the throat and her bag still in the car, and I have been waiting a hundred pages for a girl who sits down and does not find out to stand on a mat and refuse to cross a floor.

The bag. The bag is the whole chapter. This is a woman who has a drawer in that house — the good drawer, the top one, the one your hand goes to first — and the drawer is the single most eloquent thing anybody in this book has ever said to anybody, and she drove out there and left her toothbrush in the Corolla. She came as a visitor on purpose. And he came through with the towel over his shoulder and two glasses already poured and his face did the thing it does, and she stood there and *let him arrive at the fact of it.* I had to put the book down on my chest for a second. Cruel and right. She's learned his language — the heat brought up hours early, the wine, the warm — and she used it against him by simply not accepting delivery.

And then he did the thing I didn't think he was capable of. *Do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have than this one?* No, sir. That is not an answer, that's a comparables sheet. That is the first time in two books that man has been *less* than the room required, and the book knew it, because she clocked it in one line — he made the true thing pull against the point. He's done the opposite of this forty times: turned the tap off, found the true thing under the question, handed it over without a label. This time he reached for the label-dodge and it came out as *could be worse.* From Pace. The man who names things for a living. The man whose shop has the outline of every tool painted on the wall so you can see at a glance which one is missing.

*Words are cheap.* From a mathematician. From a man of exactness who sewed her a dress because she mentioned a magazine once. Words are not cheap to him, words are the one currency he's got a surplus of and has decided to keep in the safe, and she said it back to him — *you still won't spend them on me* — and that's the best sentence she's had in the whole book, better than the crackers-and-a-meal one, because it got through. It got through and what came back was the arms folding. *The still of a man holding a line at cost.* He's not withholding out of coolness and the book made sure I knew that; it costs him. And I know exactly what it costs, because I know what's in the other room, and the word *boyfriend* is a load-bearing wall he cannot move without the whole house coming down on a woman in a green Mercedes. He can't give her the word because the word would be a lie *in words,* and he has built his entire ethic on never lying in words — only in architecture. *She consents every time or I'm done.* Sure. Consents to *what,* Peter.

And then: *Not the hand in place of the word.* And: *"Vee," he said behind her, once. Just her name, and her name was not the word she'd come for.* I made a noise. That's the whole of him in nine words — he has one instrument, and the instrument is magnificent, and it does not play this note, and she has finally refused the only thing he knows how to give. The hand that settles everything, lifting, and her out the door past it.

What I'm actually sick with: where does she go. It's Friday. There is a brunch table booked in someone else's name with two mimosas already on the cloth. Vee Thorne is going to walk into that sun porch tomorrow morning cracked wide open with *I asked him what we are and he wouldn't say,* and the one person on earth best positioned to catch her, soothe her, name her, and keep her exactly where she is is going to be sitting across from her ordering without opening the menu. *You're not greedy, you're just finally hungry out loud.* Imagine what she does with this. Imagine Randi — who knows precisely why there's no word, who is the reason there's no word, who is in love with her — getting to be the one who comforts her about it. That's the most dangerous Vee has ever been and I don't think she's in any danger at all from Pace. I think the danger has bitter orange on its breath and a white-gold band at the wrist.

Also, and this is the part I'd text at 1 a.m.: *she's right.* That's what wrecks me. Every grievance in that speech is accurate and generous and she even stops to say *I'm not complaining, I need you to hear that.* She itemized his goodness to his face before she asked for the one thing, and he still couldn't. Four chapters now she's been circling this — the sentence on the porch that got answered one size too small, the Chili's fight where she accused him of the wrong crime because she couldn't say the right one, the night she took his shirt off and lay bare in the cold rather than keep breathing her own heat back out of it — and this is the one where she stops performing the question and just says it. The escalation is why I forgave the repetition. If chapter 48 is her lying in the dark again I will have words.

One more thing and then I'll stop: she went out into the cold *and did not go back in.* The book let her leave. Two books of this girl choosing the warm room over the hard question and she chose the cold.
