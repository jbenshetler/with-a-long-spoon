REACTION

Oh, he lined it.

That's the thing I'm going to be carrying out of this chapter, more than the sun coming through the silk, more than the mirror with the screw heads still bright. He lined the bodice and the hips and *nothing else* — "a soft wall set into the only parts of her the dress had decided not to give away." He sat down with a bolt of cloth and thought about exactly which parts of her the dress was going to hand over to a room and which parts were hers to keep, and then he built the keeping *in*. That's not a seduction. That's a man doing the arithmetic of her dignity in the dark at two in the morning and then not mentioning it. And he never mentions it. She finds it with her hands.

And the line that did me in before she even got the thing off the hanger: he took all that trouble and then took the further trouble to hide the trouble, so the only thing left for her to see was the part that looked like no trouble at all. That is the whole man in one sentence and the book earned the right to say it this plainly because it's spent twenty chapters *showing* it — the moleskin, the wax paper, the riser, the small bowl. I'd normally flinch at a book telling me its own thesis. This one I let have, because she's the one thinking it, and she's thinking it crying with her hand over her mouth.

But here's the beat that actually reorganized something for me: she takes her underwear off **at the mirror**. Not for him. He's in the other room. She sees the pale edge cutting the line, and the first thought is the small careful steps, and the second thought comes up hot behind it and doesn't care — and she strips herself bare for her own reflection and then *stands there and looks.* "The shame of having stripped herself bare on purpose to be looked at, and it crossed into wanting almost before it was shame at all, and she let it come." Twenty chapters of heat-and-shame running on the same wire with no seam, and here she stops trying to find the seam and just *takes the current.* And then — then — she doesn't tiptoe down the hall. She **strides**. She goes to him. Every other time in this book the choosing has been her saying yes to something he set up. This time she authored the picture and then went and delivered it.

Also: it was *funny*. Genuinely, physically funny, mid-sex, and nobody lost any heat for it. "There's a lot of you covered in a lot of me" and him twitching, and her saying it again slower *because* it made him twitch, and laughing so hard her leg slid off his hip. Two people with nowhere safe to put anything, elbows out, fending a wine-colored silk off a disaster, and refusing to let go of one single thing they're holding. I have read hundreds of these and I can count on one hand the sex scenes where somebody was allowed to be delighted. And then the hands — she dries them on his flanks because his arms are up and he can't stop her, and then second-guesses it, and holds her fingers up, and he says *closer*, and takes them into his mouth and cleans her off her own fingers with his eyes on her. I put the book face-down for a second. That's the whole grammar of him: she offers something small and mischievous and he answers it with the full weight of his attention, every time, and it keeps landing like a surprise.

And the feet. Her heels flat on his chest holding him off at the length of her own legs, frosted plum against all that skin, him stopping exactly where she put him and *looking*, and her letting him — he takes one ankle and moves it out to the side, slow, and asks nothing because she's already answered. "Well?" "I'm looking." "I noticed." That's the most relaxed she has ever been in a room with another person's eyes on her. The girl from the fitting who braced for her whole life's worth of being the joke is now opening her own knees and running commentary.

Now the part that scares me, and it's the last page.

The shoes she doesn't own. She rose onto her toes and watched the line finish itself and grinned at the woman that made and *came back down* — because the shoes this called for weren't anything she owned, and that woman could wait. God. That is a loaded gun on the mantel and somebody is going to buy those shoes, and I know who, and I don't want her to. If Randi hands Vee a box with heels in it, this whole thing tips. If Vee buys them herself with her own money, the book is building something I will follow to book eight.

And the daydream. The high room, the good light, the other girls in safe department-store navy, and her coming in **late, on purpose**, and letting the room turn. "It just felt like what she was for." I'm thrilled and I'm cold at the same time. Because her mother will be in that room. Because the word came up again in the mirror and she stayed anyway — but she stayed in a locked house with a man who loves her, which is the easiest place in the world to be brave. And because that dress didn't come out of nowhere. It came out of a plan started in a bed by two people, and one of them is going to see Vee walk into that light in it, and I don't know yet whether Randi will weep or ignite. The fact that Pace stayed up all night on it and hid the effort doesn't make it *less* of an instrument. It makes it a better one. That's what's going to gut me when it goes.

Last thing: "The only one awake in the warm house." Three times now this book has left somebody alone and awake in a room while another person sleeps or walks away — him in the doorway, him covering her bowl, now Vee lying there happy, "getting away with something." Same shape, different chair. That's not an accident and I've stopped thinking any of these three get out of it.
