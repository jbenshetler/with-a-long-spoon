REACTION

Ten chapters I've been yelling her name at this book and she walked in at five foot nothing with two colors of hair and a bottle already sweating on the table and she was *better* than what I'd been waiting for.

I want to be precise about what happened to me, because it happened in a specific order. First the cold — "a train she'd been told was cancelled," which is exactly right, that's exactly how it works, you can *know* the whole thing is a thing you built and knowing is a coat of paint and the cold comes anyway. Then the dressing. She reached past the boots. She reached past the boots and took down **Randi's induction heels** and buckled four inches of thin black strap onto her feet to go drink cider in a brick storefront in January, and the book didn't say one word about why those shoes and not the boots, and I said something out loud in my kitchen. The hau. The hau wants to go home, and she wore it to the wrong war.

And then the look.

That's the thing I'm going to be carrying. Vee stands there in a borrowed keyhole shirt braced for the pricing look — she's fluent, she says so, every woman reading this is fluent — and what arrives instead comes down her body with *its time fully taken, no math arriving anywhere, the mouth opening as it went*, and sticks at her feet, and turns into a soundless appreciative **oh**, cocked, held, *like a word being kept for later.* And the laugh came out of Vee before she'd voted on it. Before she'd voted on it! That's the fourth or fifth time this book has used that construction on her — the body getting there first — and every single other time it was dread or hunger or a tongue going out toward something she wasn't allowed to want. This time it was *relief*, and it came out as a laugh from the middle of her chest at a joke nobody told.

I don't think I've read a book that understood that before. A woman looked at her with naked appetite, publicly, in front of her boyfriend, and because there was nothing at stake in it, it *unclenched* her. That's the most generous thing anyone has done for Vee in three books and it took no words and cost Sheri nothing. And it is also, I'm aware, the cleanest possible rehearsal for the thing she couldn't do in a locked room in chapter 60. A woman's desire, aimed at her, in daylight, with no trapdoor under it. Try it on. See how it feels. It felt like laughing.

(And the blonde "didn't die so much as fail to find anywhere to stand. There was no room for her. There was barely room for the coat pegs." I laughed. Then: *and the heat in Vee's chest stayed exactly where it was. It had never needed the blonde.* Which is the one place I felt the author lean in and underline — I had it, I promise I had it — but it's one sentence and it's true, so, fine.)

Then she got the man.

I've been complaining in my own notes that I'd gone ten chapters with no interiority from Pace and had started reading him the way Vee reads him. The book's answer was to hand him to me through a stranger's mouth at a high table while he stood thirty feet away waiting on a porter that has been "coming in June since March." *Dancing at you is a down payment.* Two years of watching him turn a woman out on that floor like she was the only thing happening in the state, walk her back, thank her, and go get a water. **Hands stay where the dance puts them. Every time. The whole hall's given up on him.** And then — *you don't praise a man at his own table,* and the folder shut with no seam, and I understood that this loud crooked-makeup woman is as controlled about *when* as Randi is, and she's using it for nothing but kindness, and I nearly cried about the difference.

And the porch. She came out last March, the whole tablecloth off the table, nobody caught a dish, strangers handing out *it'll be all right* for free and the people who owed her words spending none — and she started crying in the middle of a two-step at the one place left with her name on it, and he drove her home and **asked no questions,** and was on the porch the next morning with coffee and donuts, and then fixed the lock, and then the faucet, and then noticed the bike and said he rode Sundays, did she want to come. *He never handed me one false word. No they'll come around, no their loss.* **"He was there. That's all a body can be for you at a point like that. There. And nobody else was."**

I had him filed as a man who does language in gestures because the words are broken. That's not quite it. He does the only thing that was ever any use to *him*, which — and here is where the floor dropped — brings us to: **"Went bad with his daddy. He hasn't been home since he was sixteen."** A silence she didn't decorate.

*Sixteen.* Daphne was nineteen to his fifteen. Whatever that was, it was the year before he left and never went back, and there is a scar high inside his thigh with a *not tonight* on it, and I have been sitting here for twenty minutes assembling and un-assembling a sentence I do not want to finish. I'm not going to finish it. But I notice the book put that fact in a taproom, in eleven words, in the mouth of the only other person in the novel who lost her whole family in a month, and then immediately sent the pretzels over.

And Vee got it secondhand. She learned the central fact of this man's life from a woman she met an hour ago while he had his back to her at a bar. And then she said, out loud, in the truck, drunk and honest: **"She told me things. Good ones. Not telling you which."** And he said *No,* and agreed, and drove. That's the first thing Vee has ever kept while *announcing* that she's keeping it, and it's the first vault in this entire book with nothing rotten in it. She can do it clean. It's possible. She has now done it once, by accident, drunk, about him instead of to him.

Other things that went into me sideways: his voice going up across a room, glad, on a woman's name that wasn't hers, and the hot fast thing that went through Vee at it — because that's the real jealousy, it was never the blonde's legs, it was *gladness aimed elsewhere.* Pace **blushing**, up the neck and into the ears, and taking it the way he takes everything, without fighting it, and Vee realizing she'd never once seen it. The beer at his elbow all night doing "more witnessing than work." The arm around her waist that read as affection and worked as ballast — *with him, those had never been two different things* — which is the single most load-bearing sentence anybody has written about this man. And the headlights held on a door until the porch light came on, and then backing down the run, and never mentioning it, the exact same easy unannounced way he does everything for everyone he keeps.

**"Bacon cheeseburgers," she said, to the window, to nobody.** — *"What?" — "Nothing."* Oh, that's good. That's him in December in a diner booth getting billed by this woman, that's the burger after the first Sunday ride, that's Vee putting a whole man together in the dark at fifty miles an hour, and it comes out of her as two words about sandwich meat.

Now. What I'm sitting on, and what has my stomach up:

**Valentine's Saturday at the hall is now fully loaded and three of the four people who'll be in that room don't know it.** Sheri settled it in December, she's told Vee to come, she's told her to *leave the shoes home* — Randi's shoes, off her feet, by name, from the one woman in this book with no agenda. **And Randi has been to that hall. Randi hugged Sheri hello on that floor.** Nobody at this table knows that but me. Pace taught half that bar to dance and says nothing. And "the redhead," which has been sitting in my notes for ten chapters as a question mark, is *Vee* — Sheri told him in December to bring his girl and her opinions to the hall, and he's taken six weeks to do it, and he's doing it now, two nights after a party he built out of names Vee dropped at his back.

He is assembling a room again. *I want to meet your people. There's somebody I'd like you to meet.* He builds the table, he pays for it, he leans back out of the lamplight. The kitchen tableau with three people in it mid-morning has not gone anywhere, and now there's a dance hall with a band and a floor and every single person in this book who's ever been in love with somebody in it.

The one thing I'd text at 1 a.m.: *the rival was a lesbian who thinks Vee's legs are a gift and told her so and Vee laughed so hard a month of fear left her body, and the author used it to teach her she likes being looked at by women, and Vee has no idea that's what just happened to her, and NEITHER DOES SHERI, which is why it worked.*

Also, after I spent a whole page of notes warning this book about its own formats — this was a brand new register in a brand new room with a brand new voice in it, and the voice is the best new character since Marsh. "You've got him happy. That's the whole book, far as I'm concerned." Honey, it is.
