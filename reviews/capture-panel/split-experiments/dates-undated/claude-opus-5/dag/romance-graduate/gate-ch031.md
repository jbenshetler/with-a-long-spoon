REACTION

Oh, she did it *herself.*

I had this chapter pre-built in my head — the daydream version she handed me at the end of the dress chapter, walking in late on purpose and letting the room turn — and the book took that away from me on purpose. The room is warm and dull and kind. Nobody's parents are looking at anybody. "No one was going to turn on their own." That's one of the most quietly brutal lines in the book, because it's the correction to everything she's been learning: Pace gives her the room, Randi gives her the permission, and then she walks into an actual afternoon with folding chairs and discovers that the world does not *come* to meet you, it just sits there drinking bad coffee. And so she had to make it happen with one step. Her first step was the careful one — she could have minced it all the way to the podium and nobody would have known there was anything to know — and then she took a breath and made the second step long.

I put the book down on my chest for a second after that.

Because that's the dress sequence completed in public. In his mirror she took her underwear off for her own reflection with nobody watching. Here she's got the same nothing-under-the-silk and a hundred strangers and a microphone, and she *chooses* it, mid-walk, in front of the whole room, and keeps her face exactly where it is. "She had good legs. She stalked across the stage giving them away a step at a time, and did not once make herself small." The woman who was buried in that marching-band mother-voice three hundred pages ago, the one who hid at sinks. And the murmur going through the crowd like a dull room waking up. God.

And then — the thing that made me actually sit up — she sends Pace the photo *uncropped* and he will see the dress do the thing he built it to do and have no idea what else is in the frame. "That part was hers." That's the second thing in two chapters she has kept. First the unnamed one under his hand at the sink, now this. She's started building a private room of her own, and she learned how to build one from the two people who each have one about her. That's not a girl being managed anymore. That's a girl who has figured out, without words for it, that having something of your own is how you survive being chosen.

And Randi isn't in this chapter and is all over it. The heels she knelt and buckled. The frosted plum on the fingernails that "woke and threw the light when it moved." That woman is literally on Vee's hands and feet in the one scene she's absent from and I noticed it like a draft under a door. Also noting with my whole chest that Pace was in Berkeley and the book didn't put Randi in that seat — I had braced for Randi in the audience, and the restraint of not doing it is worse, in the good way. Someone is going to be told about this walk and I already know who, and how, and that the telling will be the real scene.

Cassie. Cassie walked her to the doors and held her phone and said "You look—" and didn't finish it, and from Cassie that's an ovation, and her eyes went bright at the corners like the day the letter came, which she'd deny to the grave, and she took the *photograph*. Cassie documented it. Cassie bought her a terrible sandwich. "Come on, streetlight." I'm going to be destroyed when this lands, and the fact that the book gave me one gorgeous Cassie chapter right before whatever's coming is not lost on me — that's how you load a gun with kindness.

The mother thing got me sideways, too: the flight they couldn't afford, the bright voice she keeps for the things she can't give her, and then Vee three rows from a microphone with nothing on under the silk and *nobody in this room who had ever taught her what to be ashamed of.* That's the whole book in one sentence and it arrives as relief and as a loss at the same time. She wanted her there. She's also only free because she isn't.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: SHE TOOK THE LONG STEP. no one made her. no one was watching. she did it anyway and sent him the picture and kept the good half.

Two small gripes, since you asked what I noticed. The paragraph explaining the mechanics of why she couldn't wear anything under the dress — slit, edge, long line, therefore — walked me through arithmetic I'd already done in the mirror scene thirty pages back. And that last clause, *whether that was pride or only the same heat still running, or whether, by now, the two came to the same thing* — I'd gotten there. I'd gotten there on the murmur. The book doesn't need to hand me the key to a door I already walked through, and it's done this twice now in the chapters I love most, which is maddening precisely because the rest of it trusts me completely.
