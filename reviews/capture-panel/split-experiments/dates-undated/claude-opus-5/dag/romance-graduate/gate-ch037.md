REACTION

Okay, so she's a swimmer. Since she was twelve. And the book waited thirty-seven chapters to tell me, and told me in the one way that makes me sit up: *the green-and-white suit had nowhere left to go on her and hadn't for years. She looked at the water instead of at that.* That's the whole girl in two sentences. She's been in that building twice a week the entire time I've known her and I never heard about it because she doesn't think about it, because it isn't a story about her body — it's the one place her body isn't a story at all. *Her body, which had opinions all day long every day of her life, had none at all down here. It only worked.* I actually put the book on my chest for a second. Every single thing that's happened to that girl in this volume has been her body having an opinion she didn't vote on — the wet, the nipples, the soundless *oh*, the thing in the bathroom stall over a tablecloth — and here's seventy-five minutes where it just hauls her up and down a corridor and asks her for nothing. She's been quietly good at something this whole time and never once brought it to the brunch table.

And the attrition thing, where the questions get worn off her one piece at a time — *by the sixth she had him, and the ache, and the count. Then the count* — that's the first time the book has shown me her thinking without narrating her thinking, and it's better than most of the lectures. Cassie's *do you love him* getting in off the third wall, uninvited, in Cassie's flat voice. The answer sitting right there, easy to reach, and *maybe* having come out of her mouth anyway. She doesn't solve it. The chapter says out loud that swimming never answers anything and that's its entire use, which is the one bit of self-explanation I'll allow, because it's true and because it's about the only consolation anyone in this book has been handed for free.

But here's the thing I'm chewing on. She got four strokes — *four* — of *was he that good or did I make him that good, out of wanting, out of two months of being looked at like that*, and then the wall came and ate it. That's the second time this book has let her stand at the edge of the only real question and then moved her along. Once was devastating. Twice is starting to feel like the author's protective reflex and not hers.

And Randi did not get in the lane. Not once. Vee's head is wide open for seventy-five minutes, it lets in Cassie and the hairbrush on the counter and *did he mind*, and the woman who said *sticky fingers* to her face four days ago does not surface for a single stroke. I believe that, actually — that's exactly how you don't think about the thing — but it means I spent the whole chapter waiting for a shadow that never came, and I could *taste* that pastry glaze the entire time. She licked her lip on a sidewalk and never once asked herself where the sugar came from, and now she's done a mile and a half and still hasn't. That's the loudest silence in the book and it's getting louder by being left alone.

What I'd text at 1 a.m.: *she's a competitive swimmer and we're only finding out now, and the reason we're finding out is it's the only place nobody's looking at her.* And then, lower: *and she thought about a sandwich on the walk home. Girl. The glaze.*
