REACTION

Oh, Randi. Oh, this explains so much and excuses absolutely nothing.

I felt sick at that breakfast. The single slice of rye, no butter, the mother made entirely of labels and edges, the father asking for amusing stories about eligible men while Randi manufactures boys safe enough to serve at the table. And that bracelet—plain, locked on her wrist, deliberately kept out of the light. Whatever it means, it belongs to the hidden life. She walks into the acceptable one already wearing the secret.

John is the exact man she was trained to want, and for half the chapter I understood why she wanted the fairytale to take. He’s enormous, capable, connected, direct. He keeps up without needing to beat her. The skiing was genuinely sexy because he had power to spare and didn’t turn it into a demonstration. Then the dress, his hands over his heart, the orchestra, the sleigh—I could feel Randi trying to choose the whole life at once. Not him, really. The daughter she’d someday tell about him. Her mother’s approval. The good end of the table. A story so perfect she would never have to expose anything real.

And then Pace flashes through her mind while John kisses her, and I knew the fairytale was already dead.

Her comparison of their dancing gutted me. John’s lead is unmistakable, easy, restful. Pace leaves room for her to choose the rest of the step. Of course part of Randi longs to stop doing the work. This woman engineers every room she enters. She’s exhausted. But the kind of leadership that lets her go beautifully blank is also the thing that cannot find her. Pace makes her participate, answer, reveal herself. Vee does too, without even knowing she’s asking.

I did flinch at “Afterward.” You cannot take me all the way to the hotel room and step over the sex without my noticing, especially in this book. But then the aftermath was so emotionally naked that I stayed completely inside it. The lipstick left on his body while she feels herself performing from far away; the face repaired perfectly in the mirror; the eyeliner untouched because nothing reached her eyes—that hurt. This wasn’t bad sex she could dismiss. It was good sex with the best approved man, and it still left her cold. “Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best” is a brutal piece of self-knowledge.

And then Vee. There it is. Randi knows exactly what comes off Vee across an ordinary table. She knows she loves her in both meanings. She knows she is refusing to tell her because telling would require Vee to choose. That is the entire moral rot, stated plainly inside her own head: “Let Vee arrive at it on her own.” No. She is calling it cowardice, but it is also control. She wants the answer without risking the question. She will preserve the friendship by quietly changing what the friendship is, then treat Vee’s eventual desire as spontaneous consent.

“Vee was her gorgeous” nearly finished me, though. Because it is real. Not currency, not performance, not one of the invented boys. Vee is apparently the first person Randi has ever loved without keeping a private qualification—and instead of making her honest, that love has made her terrified enough to become exquisitely dishonest.

The New Year’s text was almost unbearable after Vee just walked out of Pace’s house crying. Vee offers exactly what Randi cannot offer her: I’m thinking about you, and you may speak or not speak; either choice is yours. Randi answers with a true lie, then slips the one naked thing into it: miss your face most. They are both reaching toward each other through men who cannot give them what they need, and neither knows the other is doing it tonight.

I want Randi badly. I understand her much better. I am also more certain that she knows what she is doing to Vee. She has built her entire life around never having to be brave, and Vee is going to pay the bill for that unless somebody finally tells the truth.
