REACTION — Okay. I bought this for "literary erotica, explicit and psychologically exact" and chapter one actually delivers on the blurb's contract, which almost never happens. The apple slices cut thin because she'll only eat thin ones, laid cut-side-up because she eats them faster that way — that's the kind of detail I mean. That one line told me more about Pace than three chapters of brooding would have.

And the scene itself — the kitchen lap, the bite-kiss-bite, his hands almost arriving and turning back — that's the slow-burn technique done by someone who understands *why* it works, not just that it works. The edging, the "that's not the right please yet," the please that finally comes from below and sounds "almost ugly compared to the bright sorority-house voice" — hot, genuinely hot, and it's hot *because* it means something. The psychological machinery is the erotica here.

But the moment that got me was the bathroom. "He said beautiful and she flinched." And then the mirror — the face that had begged, the smile she tries on sitting on top of the face that begged. That's the realest thing I've read in this genre in years, and it scared me a little, in the good way. I've read a hundred books where the submissive heroine cries after a scene and the dom says "there you are" and it's healing and they cuddle and it's fine. This book knows that some people can't be seen and stay seen. The bottle cap under her thumbnail. The quiet click of a lock she's never used in his house. Those landed hard.

And here's what I'm chewing on at 1 a.m.: the blurb says Vee is the one they choose, Randi is the one who's never let herself want a woman. So this whole chapter is the *before* picture — Pace and Randi already deep in whatever game this is, and Randi's response to being truly seen is to shut the door and decide "not ever." That reframes everything. Pace is tender and precise and maybe a little too pleased with his own perceptiveness ("he had helped her there" — sit with that, sir), and Randi is already planning how to keep the found girl locked away. The trap for Vee is already being built and none of them fully knows it. "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." Yeah. I see it starting.

Small reservations: the prose occasionally flexes a little too long in the middle of the scene — a few paragraphs where I noticed the writing when I wanted to stay in her body. And the narrator is deep in Pace's head and I suspect I'm being managed a bit, shown only what he notices. But he's a perceptive camera and the things he doesn't notice (the cap, the flinch, the lock) leak through anyway, which is either the character's blind spot or the author's plan, and I want to know which.

That ending image — the bench becoming a table again, waiting — is a whole thesis on this relationship in one paragraph. I'm in.
