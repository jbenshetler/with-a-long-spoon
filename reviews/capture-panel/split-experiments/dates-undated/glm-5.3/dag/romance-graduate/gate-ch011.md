REACTION — Okay. I put this down and had to just sit there for a minute.

The moleskin. That's the scene I'll keep. He takes her boot off and her whole body clenches beforehand — because taking the sock off in front of a man means something, her mother's whole tribunal fires before his hand is even under her ankle — and then he just... fixes it. Cuts the square, warms it with his thumb, laces it different, pats the toe, done. And the clench lets go without her catching it go. That's the sexiest thing in the chapter and there's not one erotic thing in it. I've read a hundred heroes "caring for" heroines as foreplay and this is the first one in years where the care was actually load-bearing — where it rewired something in her instead of decorating him.

The pit on the wax paper nearly got me. She spits the cherry pit, the voice calls her *common*, and he picks it up and sets his own pit next to hers and doesn't look at her doing it. "He didn't make her small to give it to her" — that's the whole book's thesis in one line and the book didn't underline it, it just let me watch her learn it.

And the dress. God, the dress. She puts the russet silk back on the shelf, does the whole self-dismissal out loud, and he says "That sounds like a beautiful dress" with no weight on it and takes another cherry. I gasped at how well he played that. And then I got scared, because I know this book: that dress is coming back. He filed it. Randi filed it too, probably, if Vee reports the lunch. Three people with money and a plan and a girl who's never once been given a thing without owing for it. When that dress shows up I'm going to feel exactly what Vee feels and I'm going to hate myself for it, which is clearly the point.

Then the truck. The truck is the hottest thing I've read in months and the man never touches her. He gives her his only dry shirt and stands OUT IN THE RAIN with his back turned so she can change — and she gets it, "his shirt, and then the truck, both," and she's already going. And the voice comes up, *floozie, three dates, your mother would* — and this is the thing that makes me want to grab this author's hand: the voice and the heat are the SAME thing. Her shame is the engine of her want, "the heat that knew the voice was right, that wanted to be the very thing the voice said." That is so exactly how it works and almost no one writes it, because it's uncomfortable to write and uncomfortable to read and most authors chicken out and pick one. Here she unbuttons her own shirt in a cold truck with her eyes held on his and I was holding my breath like it was my body being seen.

And then — his hand is shaking. HE'S the one freezing, he's been standing in the rain being chivalrous and going hypothermic for it, and she catches it mid-reach, and instead of the next thing she puts his hand against her belly and says *please, get warm.* She takes care of him. First time in this whole book anyone has taken care of Pace. The tenderness between two people who are both usually the object of someone else's design — that hit me harder than the strip would have on its own.

And then the cop. The cop! The cheap campus siren eating her sentence, her body already gone before her mind consents — that's not a cockblock, that's the book doing its work: even her exits aren't chosen, something with a siren decides when things end. She never got to say the big thing. She's standing in the rain in his flannel and I'm sitting here in my kitchen genuinely upset about it.

The hike-as-appetite-machine — hunger made first, then fed — yes, it's the motif again, Pace's whole theology, and it's borderline a tic, but the summit saved it: the world not caring that she was standing there, and that being the most beautiful part. She gets one place besides the pool where nothing wants anything from her. That she gets it *with him* and he swallows his blue shirt and lets her have it alone — okay, fine, keep your motif.
