REACTION —

Okay. I had to put the phone down after the mimosa call. I'm going to try to say this in order.

First: this chapter is the POV flip I've been waiting for since ch61, and it delivered the same vertigo — we're inside Pace's head while Randi runs him, and he *knows* he's being run, and he lets her anyway. "He knew he'd been managed. He turned it over once and set it down." That's the whole book in one line. But this time it didn't feel like elegant patience to me. It felt like a man being handled, and I felt the handle.

Because let's be honest about what Randi did here. She didn't seduce him. She *lied him into bed* — "Vee's been delayed. You have an hour" — manufactured urgency, manufactured panic, the whole clock a fiction, and then the reveal: "She's not coming." He made decisions all afternoon inside a false premise, including decisions about Vee's friendship, about protecting Randi from consequences. And then "we have all the time in the world." I read that and felt genuinely cold. Not the good kind of cold. This is the waxing omission with a budget. How do you get four mimosas into your best friend and then use her unconscious body as your alibi? I don't care how wrecked-and-crying Randi was afterward — that sobbing was real, "tell me she's better than me" was real, and it *still* doesn't buy back the lie she told to get in the door. The crying almost makes it worse, because now I know the wanting is real AND the machinery is real, running side by side, and I can't tell which one is driving.

And the line. THE line. "She's perfect. For your first woman." He said it TO RANDI. Pace knows — has known, maybe all along — that Vee is where Randi's appetite points, and he handed it to her in the exact moment she was breaking apart on him, as the thing that finishes her. That's not comfort. That's a confession traded for a confession, and he collected it like it cost him nothing. What does this man KNOW? How long has he known? Was the question in ch68 not discovery but *management*? I'm starting to wonder if the scheme has a scheme.

The heat itself — fine, it's hot, the clamps and the chain-as-rein and "she'd built the whole afternoon to earn it" is exactly the dynamic the book does better than anything else I'm reading. But what's going to live in me is the morning. Randi under the green, wiping her own night off him with her mouth, "making him over into something clean and ready for Vee's use — putting herself under the other girl." And Vee on the phone *thanking her for it.* "I don't know what I'd do without her." I had to sit with that. Vee's gratitude being spent on the woman who built her unconsciousness. And Pace — "he lay in the middle of the two of them and let it happen." Nobody in this book tells the truth standing up. They tell it lying down, sideways, through a third person's mouth.

And the last paragraph broke my heart a little — him Speed-Washing the green, taking the night off the sheets "until there was nothing left in the house for Vee at all." That's Pace's whole moral architecture in one dial turn. He doesn't lie with words. He launders with wash cycles. It's tender and it's horrifying and I can't decide which it is more, which is apparently the only question this book is interested in asking me.

Also: "I have secrets of my own" — Randi said that GRINNING, right after hearing that Vee pinked to the ears telling her everything. Vee doesn't know she told her everything TO one of the people the everything is about. The triangle has closed its loop and only Vee is outside it, still calling it friendship.
