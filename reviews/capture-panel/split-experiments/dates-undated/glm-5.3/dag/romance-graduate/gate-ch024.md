Daphne. *Daphne.* My load-bearing ghost finally got her body and she's — nineteen and he was fifteen. A "tutor." Two years. And he laid it out like an audit, every reason in its slot, and Vee watched him do it and saw the tell — "the good order was the tell" — and did NOT say the word. She held the clean ugly grown-up word under her tongue because it wasn't hers to say. That's the most grown-up thing anyone's done in this book. I would have fumbled that. And the scar. Her fingers found it, longer than her hand — more of it than she'd known — and he put his hand OVER hers and pressed it down onto it and said "not tonight." Not pulling her away. Holding her hand on the thing he can't tell yet. I gasped. That's the door with a time on it, and I'll wait for whatever's behind it, and I'm a little scared of it now because a scar that long, high on the inner thigh, on a boy who was "not a good fit where he was" — the book just quietly loaded a gun and left it on the mantel.

The math. I can't believe a monologue about coastlines and Newton's three sentences made my chest hurt. "Most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it, and I got to read some." That's not a mathematician talking, that's a kid who taught himself trigonometry for a graphics program and got his desk moved into the hall for it. *They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment* — she found that in him like she found the right rib at the fitting. And then the seventh-grade-hall story recontextualized everything: the patience, the asking before taking, the man who never makes a girl carry her desk into the hall. It was never just courtliness. "Peter." Saying it once into the dark, not asking for anything, just keeping it. And him not answering and not needing to. I had to put the book down for a second after that.
