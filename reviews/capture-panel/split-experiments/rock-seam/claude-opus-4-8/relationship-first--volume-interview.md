T1 — Vee first, because she's the one I can't put down cleanly.

Where the book leaves me with her is under that quilt, tea gone cold, holding an ugly grown-up word under her tongue because it isn't hers to say. And that's the thing that guts me: she's more honest, more careful, more decent inside that scene than either of the people running her. She reached into the room Pace didn't open — "they took the thing you loved and made it the punishment" — that was her moving the scene, and it's the most alive she's been. What I feel for her is not pity, which surprised me. It's something closer to fear on her behalf, and a kind of ache at how the loneliest thing in the whole book is the middle she keeps ladling out of every telling. She's got a person right there — Cassie — who would hold the whole story, and she hands her a version with the center lifted out. I feel closest to Vee and I trust her judgment least, and I've made my peace with holding both.

Pace — I'm colder than I want to be, and colder than the book is. He terrifies me precisely and steadily, and the last chapter didn't soften that, it sharpened it. Nineteen and fifteen, delivered in that too-orderly voice, called the best two years — and his hand over the scar, "not tonight," a door with a time on it. The book wants me tender there and I felt the pull of the tenderness and refused it, or mostly refused it. Here's the exact texture of how I feel: I am not sure the book is as frightened of him as I am, and that uncertainty is the one place I don't fully trust the writer. The bowl covered with a plate and put in the fridge broke my heart a little and I resent that it did. That's where he leaves me — moved against my will, alarmed, and watching the author to see if she blinks.

Randi is the one I feel the most complicated warmth toward, and the most dread. She started this as the instrument and she's become, to me, the tragedy in waiting — "she got past me before I could help it," in his undershirt eating everything off the plate, wanting Vee out loud in a kitchen where wanting Vee was supposed to be a job. I no longer trust my own read of her, and I mean that as praise. I can't sort her appetite for Vee from her appetite for the game, and I've stopped trying, because I don't think she can either. Where she leaves me: owing Vee a debt Vee doesn't know exists, and having quietly run up a second debt to herself that the plan never priced. I feel for her the way you feel for someone about to be handed a bill they can't read yet.

T2 — What I expect Book Two to do, and the one thing that puts it down.

I expect the crack to become the floor. Book One's whole motion was the plan starting to eat the planners — the poured glass she poured herself, "it's selfish, Pace," Randi leaving the bed and it costing him. I expect Book Two to be the two conspirators unable to keep running the machine and unable to say so straight, and I expect Cassie to come further into the frame, because the book has kept her deliberately just outside it and I've been dreading and wanting that in equal measure. I expect a reckoning with the withheld middle — Vee's secret is now load-bearing, and a book this deliberate doesn't plant that and leave it. And I half-expect, half-dread, that the Daphne door with a time on it opens, and that the book will ask me to stay tender toward Pace exactly when I least want to.

The one thing that would make me put it down: if the retelling machine goes back to being only a structure. Chapter eighteen was the warning shot — I skimmed my first paragraph there — and chapter nineteen redeemed it by turning the debrief into a weapon. If Book Two gives me two retellings in a row that are recap-plus-jokes, with the planners staying in control and the plan just running clean, then it's become plot moving people, and that's the one thing I've never finished. It's not the heat that would lose me and it's not the darkness. It's the machine running smoothly. I need it to keep cracking the people who built it.

T3 — Would I buy Book Two, and what for.

Yes — I already did, before I'd finished being annoyed at it. What I'm buying it for is not the plot and not to find out whether Vee learns the truth. I'm buying it for the register of chapter twenty-three and chapter twenty-seven: two people who know each other perfectly, across a table or under a quilt, one thing cracking, nobody naming it straight, and a single gesture — a self-poured glass, a hand over a scar — carrying more than the sex around it. I'm buying it because a writer who can do the tape measure in seventeen and the giggling in twenty-four and the poured glass in twenty-three has range, and I want to be in the room for what she does with it. The heat is why the door's open; the two people talking is why I stay.

Now the hypothetical — Book Two ends and Vee still doesn't know.

I don't refuse it, and my answer is yes, with a condition I can actually name. I'm not a romance reader; nobody owes me the reveal, and I've loved books that end on the question and distrusted the ones that answer everything they ask. So Vee not-knowing wouldn't cost me a thing on its own — in fact a book too eager to discharge that secret would worry me more. What I'd be buying Book Three for is the same thing: not the discovery, but the pressure of the withholding, the planners still cracking, Cassie still circling the frame. The condition is the one from T2 — I'd buy Book Three if Vee still doesn't know but the people are still moving each other. If Book Two ends with Vee not knowing because the machine simply kept humming and nobody changed, then it isn't that the question stayed open, it's that nothing happened, and that's the book I put down and don't quite pick back up. Keep the question open by all means. Just don't keep it open by standing still.
