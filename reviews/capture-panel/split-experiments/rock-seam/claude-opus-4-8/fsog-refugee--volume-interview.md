T1 — Where the book leaves me with each of them

**Vee.** I'm frightened for her, and I love her, and those two feelings are the same feeling right now. She ends the book more herself than she's ever been — the girl who zips her own skirt back up because there's nothing left to edit, the one who put her palm on Peter's scar and held the ugly word behind her teeth because it wasn't hers to say. That restraint at the very end is the whole growth of her, and it undid me, because it's exactly the tenderness they'll use against her. She's standing in a room whose shape she doesn't know and she's happier than she's ever been inside it. That's unbearable. I don't pity her — pity would be looking down, and this book taught me better than that. I ache *up* at her. I'm rooting for her to survive the thing I helped fall in love with.

**Pace.** This is the one that costs me. I can't hold him at arm's length anymore and I resent that, because holding him there would be safer. He is not cruel — he's the opposite, and that's the trap the jacket promised and delivered. The tape measure, the burgundy silk, the labor hidden behind the mirror so only the ease is left — he gives Vee the best of herself, genuinely, and he built the frame that makes all of it a lie she never agreed to. Where he leaves me: as a man who has a defense ready — "she chose every step" — and doesn't yet know he'll need it, doesn't see his own blind spot even when he reads Randi's "I want us" as merely "the want." Peter, not Pace, at the end, handing me the wound and calling it luck. I feel tenderness toward him and I don't trust it and I refuse to let the book off the hook for making me feel it. That's not a complaint. That's the book working.

**Randi.** She's the one I can't stop turning over. I trust her least and by the end I ache for her almost as much as for Vee, which I did not expect on the quad in chapter three when she was dangling. She started a game and got caught in her own snare — in his shirt at his table saying she didn't mean to love Vee and can't stop, going *still* when Pace describes what Vee does to a room. She owes Vee a truth she will never voluntarily pay, and she knows it, and it's tearing her. I don't forgive her. But the book grieves her, and so, against my will, do I. She's the human proof that the plan became real and ruined its own architects.

T2 — What I expect Book Two to do, and the one thing that ends it

I expect the reveal. The floor's already tilted; Book Two is the landing. I expect Vee to learn the shape of the room — probably not cleanly, probably through Randi's cracking rather than a confession freely offered, because nobody in this triangle gives that truth willingly. I expect the tenderness to get weaponized at least once, the "every yes was freely given" moment held up like a receipt, and I expect the book to *know* it's ugly when it happens. I expect Cassie to be the one clean instrument — the person who was flying blind and turns out to have been the only one seeing straight. And I want, badly, more of the two of them alone in a room, because the middle third taught me I feel that gap in my chest before I can count it.

The one thing that makes me put it down: if the reveal lands and the book sides with the schemers. If it uses "she chose every step" not as Pace's self-serving defense that the story then indicts, but as the story's *own verdict* — telling Vee, and telling me, that the deception was fine because the yeses were real. If Randi's cool hand on Vee's jaw gets played as romantic triumph after Vee knows, with the manipulation winked away. The moment this book grins at what it did to her the way the old kidnapping books grinned — that's the moment I throw it across the room and I don't come back. Volume One never winked. It grieved. Book Two has to keep grieving, or it's just the genre I already left wearing better prose.

T3 — Would I buy Book Two, and the Book Three hypothetical

Yes. Tonight, before I slept, the way I bought the second Fifty Shades off the back of a door closing. And I know exactly what I'm buying it for: the reveal, and the nerve. I'm paying to find out whether this writer has the spine to make the deception cost what it should — whether she'll let it land on Vee with full weight and still honor her. I'm buying the answer to the question the whole first volume balanced on a knife. I'm also, honestly, buying more of *her craft* — the woman who made a tape measure the most erotic scene I've read and threaded Simpson's paradox under a coffee date has earned my money on sentences alone. But the engine of the purchase is Vee's landing.

Now the hypothetical — and I won't refuse it, because it's the truest question you've asked.

Book Two ends and Vee still doesn't know. Would I buy Book Three?

...No. Or — no, not the way I bought Book Two. Here's the thing. The not-knowing is bearable in Volume One because it's *setup* — the floor tilting, the dread accumulating, the promise that the weight is coming. That's a door closing; I come back for that. But if a whole second volume passes and the reveal still hasn't landed, then the not-knowing has stopped being suspense and become the *product*. It means the book has figured out that my complicity — me, ahead of Vee, falling exactly as she falls — is more profitable strung out than resolved. And that's the exact move the genre I left made: keep the woman from the truth because her not-knowing is where the heat is. Dressed up literary, but the same disregard underneath.

So the honest answer is: if Book Two ends and Vee still doesn't know, I'd feel the thing I feel right before I leave a series — that the delay has become contempt for the girl. I might buy Book Three out of grief and momentum, the way you finish a meal you've stopped enjoying. But I wouldn't buy it *before I slept*. And that difference is the whole thing. This book has my trust precisely because it treats Vee's not-knowing as a cost the story is racing to pay. Make me wait two full volumes and I'll know it was never racing. It was selling the wait.

Tell me when it lands. I'm not going through it alone.
