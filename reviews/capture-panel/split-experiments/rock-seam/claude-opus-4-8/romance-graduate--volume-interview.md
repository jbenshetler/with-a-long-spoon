T1 — Where it leaves me with each of them.

Vee. Right now, a day out, I'm tender toward her in a way that's almost protective and almost unbearable. She's the only one of the three who walked into this owing nothing, and the book has spent a whole volume charging her for it without her seeing the bill. What guts me isn't the seduction — it's the toenails and the zipper going back up in front of Cassie. She's editing the one clean love out of her own life to make room for the people hunting her, and she thinks that's what growing up feels like. So my feeling is dread braided with real affection: I like her, I believe her interiority completely — the girl who couldn't reconstruct a sentence because she was too busy being chosen is *me* at twenty — and I'm bracing for her to be the one who pays. She doesn't know. That's the whole taste in my mouth.

Pace. This is the one I can't file cleanly, and I've stopped trying. I want to trust him and I know that wanting is the trap the book built for me on purpose. The measuring chapter, the silk matched to *her* and not the dress, "not tonight" as a door with a time on it — I believe every gentle thing he does is true. And he was handed a girl as a game and hasn't told her, and by the fire we learn he was *himself* handed to a nineteen-year-old at fifteen and calls it luck. So my feeling toward him is the most complicated thing in the book: I'm furious that I'm rooting for him, and I'm also aware that "Peter" walking into the light means the predator might be a wound wearing a courtly older man. I'm not comfortable. I'm not supposed to be.

Randi. Strangely, she's the one I feel the *most* for right now, which I did not expect at Gate 3 when she was the coldest thing I'd read in a year. She started it and she's the one being eaten alive by it — pouring her own wine, leaving after for the first time, going still when Pace says Vee can't see her own worth because Randi can't see her own either. "She got past me before I could help it." I don't forgive her. But I ache for her, because she's the one who knows exactly what she's doing and can no longer afford to stop. She owes Vee a truth that would cost her the thing she now can't live without.

The book leaves me loving the one clean person offstage — Cassie — and unable to hate a single one of the three guilty ones. That's the trick and it worked.

T2 — What I expect Book Two to do, and the one thing that would make me put it down.

What I expect: the reveal engine finally turns over. Volume One was the building of the trap and the hunters falling into it themselves; Book Two is where the pressure has to find a seam. I expect the scar to open (he put a time on it — "not tonight" is a promise the second volume has to keep), I expect "Peter" and Daphne to reorganize Pace the way the bench reorganized Randi. I expect Cassie to become the fuse — she's the only one who can see, she took the address like a dossier, and a book that spent this much on that friendship is loading it. And I expect Randi's crack to widen into the actual fight, the one where "selfish" gets its real answer. Somewhere in there, the thing all three are terrified of: Vee finding out how she was chosen.

The one thing that would make me put it down: if the telling-chapter shape hardens into scaffolding. I already flagged it — the "glowing girl comes home and recounts everything" beat ran twice too close in the middle (Gate 18 next to Gate 22), and my thumb sped up. It survived because each telling still turned the screw, the clean version to Cassie and the real version to the huntress feeding on her. But if Book Two opens the same slots on the same schedule — debrief, lunch, debrief — and the recountings stop *advancing* and start *summarizing*, I'll feel the machine and I'll be gone. I've quit series at book two for exactly this. I can name the chapter every time. The heat won't save it; repetition of shape is the thing I can't read through.

T3 — Would I buy Book Two, and what for.

Yes. I'd have bought it before I set Volume One down — I said so in my verdict and I meant it. It ended wanting, not tidy: the game turned real on its makers, the scar unopened, Cassie being lost by inches, Vee still in the dark, and "Peter" just stepped into the light. That's the exact place a series is supposed to leave you and almost none of them do; they leave you *satisfied*, which is death.

What I'm buying it for: the welding. Not the heat alone — I have a Kindle full of heat I could recite. I'm buying it because this writer makes depth and eros the same substance, because a chapter of a man measuring a woman for a dress was hotter than entire books I've DNF'd, and because it made me ache about a friendship, which erotica told me it couldn't do. I'm buying the feeling of not being able to file the man. I'm buying the horror-and-gift braid. I want the seat this book keeps putting me in.

The hypothetical — Book Two ends and Vee still doesn't know. Would I buy Book Three?

I won't refuse it, but I'll answer it honestly, because it's the real question and the answer has a condition on it.

It depends entirely on *why* she still doesn't know. If Book Two withholds the reveal because the three of them are being pulled further into something true and terrible — if not-knowing is costing them more every chapter, if Cassie's closing in, if Randi's about to break and can't, if the not-knowing is a live wire and not a stalled one — then yes, instantly, the night I finish, same as I bought Two. That's a series earning its length.

But if Book Two ends with Vee not knowing because the book *kept her* not knowing — protecting the secret as a plot resource, running the seduction rhythm again in the same slots to fill the pages, dangling the reveal as bait it has no intention of paying — then no. That's the version where the middle starts repeating itself, and I've quit exactly there before. The secret is the book's best asset and its biggest liability: it can drive two more volumes or it can become the one text that never gets sent, the manufactured conflict I came here to escape. So: I'd buy Three if not-knowing still hurts. I'd walk if not-knowing has become the author's convenience. This writer has earned enough trust that I'd bet on the first — but I'd be watching the shape the whole way.
