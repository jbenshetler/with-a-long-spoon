TELL A FRIEND: Okay, so — remember how I stopped reading this whole genre because I got tired of being asked to find kidnapping romantic? This is the book I was hoping existed. There's a man in it who asks, out loud, with actual words, *may I* and *would you be bothered* and *tell me your word, tell me again if you forget* — and then he does the harder thing, which is stop when she stops and leave the last inch where it is so she has to come and get it. There's an hour-long scene where he measures her for a dress and she's on a box in her underwear and he's fully dressed with a pencil behind his ear, and it is the single hottest thing I've read in ten years and he never touches her below the ribs. She's the one who takes her shirt off in the truck. She's the one who kisses him first. Her wanting is *hers*, rendered from inside, shame and heat running on the same wire and the shame finally not closing her — I actually got emotional about it. Now the bad news, or the bad-news-shaped news: the whole thing starts as a game between two other people who pick her off a lawn like a dessert, and you know it from page one and she doesn't, and you will spend the entire book with your stomach somewhere near your knees. If the book winked even once I'd have thrown it. It never winks — it makes the two schemers pay, in private, in ways they can't say out loud, and the woman who started it is quietly the most heartbreaking person in it; she's in love with both of them and can't afford to know it, and she's been hollowing herself out since chapter one to keep from being seen. Fair warning that the roommate is the best person in the book and I'm fairly sure something is going to happen to her, and there's a reveal at the very end about a fifteen-year-old and a nineteen-year-old that the author is smart enough not to explain to you. It ends on a door closing with a time on it instead of a wall, which is exactly how I like to be left. Buy both books. Do not start it on a work night.
