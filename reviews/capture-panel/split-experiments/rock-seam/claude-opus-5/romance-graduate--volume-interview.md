**T1 —**

**Vee.** Not where I expected. I thought I'd end the book feeling sorry for her and instead I feel something closer to proud, with my stomach in a fist. Track what she actually does across it: in chapter three she's being *pointed at*, and by twenty-three she pours her own wine, and in twenty-four she takes her own underwear off so the line runs clean, and in twelve she knocks on the glass herself. Every single time, the move is his and then the move is hers, and by the end she's the one doing it. That's a real arc and it's built out of small physical things instead of announcements. So no, I don't pity her.

What I feel is *braced*. Because the thing she's been handed is a life that was chosen for her by somebody else's appetite, and she has been genuinely, unfakeably happy inside it, and that happiness is real even though the frame is a lie. And the loneliest thing in the whole volume isn't the not-knowing — it's the editing. Twenty-one, the zipper stopping halfway because the next thing off wouldn't be an edited story. She lifts the middle out clean, hands Cassie the surface, and calls it a kindness. That's a girl who's learned that the truth costs other people, and it's going to cost her worse than the reveal will. I closed the book wanting somebody to be told the middle. Anybody.

**Pace.** Warmth, and under it a hum I don't like. He is the first man in a decade I've read who's made of specifics instead of adjectives — the graph paper, the shoe closet, the small bowl he always uses, matching the cloth to *her* and not to the dress. He's careful in a way that isn't a personality trait, it's a practice, and chapter twenty — *would you be bothered if I was too hard* — asked on his knees, seriously, is the most decent thing I've read in this aisle in years.

And he is walking a girl toward a door she doesn't know is a door, and every step is hers, and he'd say so with a straight face, and he'd be *right*. That's the part I can't get comfortable about. He hasn't done anything wrong. He also can't come out of this clean, because he took delivery of a person. What twenty-seven did — Daphne, laid out in that awful good order, every reason in its slot, the coastline, *Peter*, handing her the thing that broke him and calling it luck — is that it gave him something to lose that isn't pride. He's the one with the most at stake and the least fault, and that's a horrible, delicious place for a book to leave a man.

Honestly? I'm a little afraid the next book will make him grovel for it. I don't want that. I don't want him retroactively converted into someone who should have known.

**Randi.** She's the one I'm still carrying around a day later, and my feelings about her are not clean.

I like her. That's the problem. She's the con and she's also the only person who's reached into that girl's chest and taken the true thing out — *nobody measures over a bra*, and Vee handed it over *relieved*. Nineteen is a rescue performed by the person doing the damage, and both of those are sincere at the same time. Twenty-two, she hands Vee her own permission back over a smoothie and I genuinely could not tell which one she thought she was doing. Neither could she. That's the engine.

Where the book leaves her: twenty-six, in his shirt, in a warm kitchen, going still in the wrong way while he describes what Vee does to a room. She heard, in his mouth, a description of something she is constitutionally not. She's stopped playing. She's spending her own money on the bit. And she's now got two things she can't have and one of them is the friend.

How I feel about her, right now, honestly: I want her to survive this. Not be forgiven — survive. I'd be angry if book two handed her a comeuppance scene and a shamed exit. She's the best character in the book by a distance and the shape of a thousand other books says she gets punished so the couple can be clean. If that happens I'll feel cheated of the only genuinely unpredictable person in it.

**T2 —**

What I *expect*: Cassie comes back and the flat *Goodbye* gets paid. The circle of people who know widens by one — somebody on the outside puts it together, and the pressure comes from there rather than from Randi confessing, because Randi confessing is the one thing I'm sure she can't do. I expect the heat to get less clothed and I'm half-nervous about that, because the top three chapters in this book — the fitting, the wine, the rain — had nobody undressed, and the book's real specialty is want with everything still on. I expect Daphne to come back off the shelf and cost something. And I expect at least one chapter where Randi gets the camera to herself for real, not filtered through Vee, because twenty-six was the crack in the door.

What would make me put it down — the erosion first, then the kill shot, because they're different.

Erosion: the debrief slot. Five times by chapter eighteen I could feel it coming before I turned the page, and I stopped being surprised by *where* I was. It earned it more often than not — twenty-one turned it into the best use of the pattern in the book. But that's the thing being run closest to empty, and if book two opens on Vee telling somebody about the end of book one, I'll start skimming to get to the happenings, and skimming is how it dies slowly. Related, smaller: the two-page chapters are still a coin flip to me. Twenty-seven's quilt chapter proved the short form can be a whole meal; six was a snack. If they start showing up as a way to skip work instead of a way to land a blow, I'll clock it.

But the one thing — the actual drop: if the reveal gets *withheld by contrivance*. A phone taken out of a hand. Walking in at the wrong second. Somebody starting the sentence and getting interrupted. I have read that book four hundred times and I do not need it again. This book has been beautifully honest so far — it never manufactured anything, the tension is structural, it comes from three people wanting real things in incompatible directions. The second it reaches for a cheap delay, it's telling me it doesn't trust its own material, and I'll believe it.

Close second, and I'd rather name it than not: if the book starts explaining itself. If anyone says out loud what *I point, you pick* means. If Randi delivers a paragraph about her own motive. Twelve worked because he walked back out into the rain and the book let me figure out why at her speed. The day it starts glossing itself for me, it's over.

**T3 —**

Yes. I said tonight, full price, no waiting for the sale, and a day later that still holds.

What I'm buying it for is not the reveal. Let me be exact about that, because the reveal is the least interesting thing in this book and it always was. I'm buying it for Randi — I want to know what she thinks she's doing, and I want the book to not know either, for as long as possible. I'm buying it for the register where a man with a pencil and a girl on a box for an hour is filthier than anything else on my Kindle this year; if book two can do that again in a new key, I'd pay for that alone. And I'm buying it for being ahead of her, which should be unbearable and is instead the whole charge.

Now the hypothetical. Book Two ends, Vee still doesn't know.

I'm not going to pretend that's automatically a no, because I've bought the next one the night I finished plenty of times on exactly that shape — left wanting, not left tidy. But I'd want to know what got *spent*. That's the test. This book didn't hold the secret still — it moved every single chapter. Randi went from playing to paying. Vee went from being pointed at to pouring her own wine. Cassie went from telling the truth for free to *Goodbye*. The not-knowing cost somebody something new every time.

So: if Book Two ends with Vee not knowing, but the price has gone up — if Randi's spent something she can't get back, if one more person knows, if Pace has started to feel the shape of the floor under him — then yes, I buy Three, and I buy it that night.

If Book Two ends with Vee not knowing and everything in the same place it started, just warmer and wetter, then that's not a cliffhanger, that's a stall, and I'll know it by about chapter nine, and I'll be able to tell you the exact chapter a year from now, the way I can for the other two series I quit at book two. That's a no, and it'd be a no on Book Two's back half, not on Three.

One more thing, since you asked what I'm buying: I'd also take Book Two ending with Vee knowing. Everyone assumes the withhold is the asset. This book's real asset is that all three of these people are worth watching in a room together, and I'd genuinely rather read three hundred pages of the aftermath than another volume of the wind-up.
