REACTION

Okay so after the *bomb* of 41 — Randi's own father sitting on the worst point in the county, "Cheating bastards" ragged and first-time and aimed too near — I came into 42 braced, wound up, wanting the fallout. And the book just… poured me a bath. Five nights apart, the drive, the doorway, the pizza, the shirt. And I'll say the thing I don't always get to say about a comedown chapter: I didn't resent a second of it, and I think it's because the book *earned* the right to slow down by gutting me the chapter before.

The sex was the best kind of this book's sex, which is to say it was barely about the sex. It was about her body publishing numbers she'd tried to keep private — "the exact sound of a woman who had told herself a careful story about needing time to study and was being shown now, by her own throat, just how true the story had been." She came too fast and lay there with her face hot because her hips had just read out the tally her mouth never would. That's the whole engine of this book in one image: she keeps trying to file her hunger down to fit the room he gives her, and her body keeps ratting her out. The stripping-in-the-hallway bit was genuinely *funny*, which this book almost never lets itself be — the jeans inside out at her ankle, the socks, "You're not helping" / "I'm helping" — and I loved that she lost the little game and put her mouth back on him anyway. The comedy was him enjoying it too plainly. That's a man who has her and knows it.

But the shirt. God, the shirt. I knew what it was going to be the second she knelt over the bag and found it folded into a square, and it still got me — the hot prick of tears came up in *me*, not just her. She wore his flannel every night she wasn't there until it went stale and then went to nothing, "one day last week it didn't smell like anything at all," and she had to *wash the dying thing* and then hand it back and ask him to refill it with himself. "I need you to wear it for me. The whole weekend." That is the loneliest, most naked thing she's done in forty-two chapters, and here is where the book has its hooks all the way into my sternum: he receives it *perfectly*. The warm slow smile that costs him something. He pulls it on over the shirt he's already wearing. "Smelling like you." / "Smelling like me." And it's beautiful. And it's exactly, precisely the trap. He will give her the shirt soaked in him every single Sunday for the rest of her life. He will give her the drawer, the pizza where she can taste every separate flavor, the perfect receiving of her every hunger. He will give her *everything but the word.* She's rationing his smell out of a flannel across the nights she's not allowed to count out loud, and he lets her, and calls the letting love. The jacket told me this: he gives her everything but a word for what they are. Chapter 42 is just that sentence, lived.

What I'm sick about at 1 a.m.: she just sat in a glass room and watched Randi's face go bloodless over her father's fraud, and did the *kind* thing — gave Randi her hands to hold, didn't say the name. Vee is so good. Vee is so careful with the exact woman who harvested her. And that tenderness she keeps spending on Randi and Pace is the thing that's going to cost her the most when she finds out. The shirt chapter is tender and I let it be tender, but I couldn't stop seeing the ledger underneath it. She can taste every flavor now because he rebuilt her appetite — and I keep thinking, an appetite that big in a woman with no word for what she is, that's not a gift, that's a debt coming due.

I noticed the dates: Nov 20, five nights since the peak. And I noticed the table — "the long, unusually proportioned table that stood in the middle of his bedroom," her thong landing green on it, and nobody remarking on why there's a *table that size* in a bedroom. I clocked it. The book wants me to clock it. Thanksgiving is loading and there's a table I don't have a use for yet.
