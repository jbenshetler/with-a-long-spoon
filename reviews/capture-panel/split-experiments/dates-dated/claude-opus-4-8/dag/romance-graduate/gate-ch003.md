REACTION

Oh. OH. I did not see the shape of this until it closed its hand.

So I spent the first two chapters assuming Vee was our girl — the jacket told me so, twenty-one, quietly starving, the one being steered. And this whole series opened on *Randi* instead. On the bench. Begging. Crying on the leather. And I let myself read her as the confident one, the glamorous friend, the operator — and then the book pulled the camera back one more notch and showed me Randi is the one being worked. By Pace. With the exact same patience he used on the bench. He's not finding her a woman for a threesome. He's building her the next bench.

That's the thing that actually scared me and turned me on in the same breath, which almost never happens anymore. The pointing game is a *seduction technique dressed as play* and the book knows it and shows me him knowing it — "I had no name for the game yet, only the live private charge" — but it never once explains the subtext to me, never nudges me and says *see what he's doing.* It just lets me watch her stop being a good sport a quarter mile back and start *shopping.* That line. "She was shopping." God.

And here's what kept me from rolling my eyes, because on paper "boyfriend points at girls on the quad until she picks one" is a premise I'd expect to be gross or dumb: the interiority is doing all the work. The three-beat landing — confusion, propriety offended, then the picture snapping into place — I *felt* the recoil in her hand before her face changed. The follow breaking frame before the lead calls the step. He's reading her like a dancer reads weight and the prose reads her the same way. That's the grown-up thing I'm starving for. It's not telling me she's turned on; it's showing me her body answer ahead of her and then watching her scramble to dress the answer as something survivable. "Only in a threesome. With you." The way she manufactures a version where she's a generous girlfriend and not a woman who wanted a stranger on sight — and he *lets the camouflage stand* because he's already got what he needs. That's chilling and it's hot and it's smart, all at once.

What I'm suspicious of — genuinely, the good kind of suspicious — is how little anyone is choosing freely here, and the jacket already told me that's the whole point. "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." I believe it now. Randi's "yes" by the dining hall arrived "already true before she got to it." That's not consent being coerced, it's consent being *harvested.* And then the gut-punch of the last page: "Do you think she'll like the bench?" Randi, who sobbed on that bench three days ago and locked herself in the bathroom and decided *never again* — is now cheerfully planning to deliver another girl to it. She's doing to Vee exactly what was done to her, and she doesn't see it, and the book trusts me to. That's the deadbolt turning over from chapter one coming back around.

Also — and this is small but it landed — the food. "I can't eat that," automatic, no glance back, right after Pace fed her thin apple slices like a man rehabilitating a starved animal. The portioning. These people are all starving in the same way and feeding on each other.

What I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *the villain of this book might be the nicest man in it and I cannot tell if I'm supposed to want him to stop.*

The prose occasionally reaches — the maple burning, the two-centuries-ago columns — but it earns the reach, and it never does the thing I hate where it stops to admire itself and explain. And the heat is real and it's load-bearing; that third orgasm triggered by a *question*, before his body accounts for it, is the single most erotic beat because it's purely psychological. That's the both-at-once I almost never get.

I am not setting this down.
