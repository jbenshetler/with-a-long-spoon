**REACTION**

Okay. I need to text someone.

I read the whole first half thinking she was Vee. The jacket tells me Vee is twenty-one and starving and Pace sees her completely, and here's a twenty-one-year-old in cashmere who rations her food and won't eat a thick apple slice. I was fully settled into "this is the hero falling for our heroine." Then he says "There you are, *Randi*," and I actually sat up. That isn't a cheap twist. It changes the whole book. The woman Randi steers Vee toward is the woman Pace already broke open on a bench he built in secret. And the chapter closes with Randi deciding, "quiet as a deadbolt going over," that nobody gets to see her like that again. So what's Vee for? A proxy? A way to keep Pace pointed somewhere other than her? I'm already suspicious of everyone, which is exactly where I want to be.

The heat is the real thing, finally. No fade, no "his length," no coyness. The apple game on his lap is one of the hottest openings I've read in ages, and it's because it's *about* something: food, permission, what she lets herself take. He's attentive, he asks before he takes, and he isn't another interchangeable brooder. He's patient in a way that's honestly a little frightening. Each escalation on the bench has a reason behind it, and that one line, "it was still her please... the voice she said please in when she asked a waiter for a glass of water," got me in the stomach. That's the book I've been looking for: the sex scene and the character work happening in the same breath.

Then the aftermath, which is where it really earns me. She presses the edge of a bottle cap into her nail bed while he's being tender with her, and he doesn't see it, or doesn't clock what it means. She flinches at "you're beautiful." She locks the door, which she has never done in his house. He sits outside thinking about Daphne and how proud he is of himself for respecting doors, while on the other side of it she's sealing him out for good. Then the last image: he holds her, "quietly happy about her," with no idea, and the bench sits there "full of what it was." I got chills. It's genuinely sad, and it makes Pace a little sinister without the book ever saying so.

My gripes. In places the prose talks itself in circles: "The *please* was different. The *please* came from below. The *please* was the *please* of..." I get it. And the bathroom section explains her a lot. "It had come from being seen. He had looked past the face..." spells out a turn I'd have felt without the narration walking me through it. The "the face had" repetition is a deliberate device and mostly works, but it skirts the edge of telling me what I just watched. It's also long. A shorter chapter wouldn't have dropped "Good girl" into the middle of all that precision and handed me the one beat I could recite from memory.

But I'm in. This is grown-up, filthy, and sad, and I want to know what Randi does next.
