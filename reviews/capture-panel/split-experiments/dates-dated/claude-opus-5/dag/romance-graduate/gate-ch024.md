Oh, they finally opened the Daphne door, and they opened it the exact right amount — which is to say: not all the way, and from the inside, with him holding the handle.

I have been waiting ten chapters for whatever is behind this man and what I got was a hallway. Seventh grade, he asks a question his teacher can't answer, and she moves his desk into the hall and hands him long-division worksheets. For *the year*. And Vee says the thing — "they took the thing you loved and made it the punishment" — and he doesn't flinch into gratitude or get weird about being seen, he just says *yes, that is approximately what happened*, and then he comes back **up** out of it, because the hallway isn't where the story lives for him, it's the door you walk through to get to the rooms where people thought it was beautiful. That's the most generous piece of characterization this book has done for him and it cost two paragraphs.

And then. Nineteen and fifteen. Assigned to him. "To help the child find his feet." Two years. I actually put my hand flat on the table. He lays it out in that order-of-operations voice — nobody did anything wrong, it would have been stranger if she'd stayed, it was only sensible — and the *good order is the tell*, the book says it once, lightly, and then shuts up about it, and I could have kissed whoever decided not to explain it to me. Vee does the arithmetic she doesn't want to do, the clean ugly grown-up word starts to assemble in her mouth, and she **holds it under her tongue** because it isn't hers to say. That restraint is worth more to me than any confrontation scene could have been. She doesn't say sorry. She doesn't name it. She turns over and puts her palm flat on his chest *over the place the careful voice came out of.* That's the whole chapter. That's the bond moving.

What it does to my read of the entire book, though. He was a fifteen-year-old in a dormitory full of men who got loved by someone four years up who was handed authority over him, and he came out of it believing that the thing that hurt him was his good luck, and he's built an entire ethical system — *she chooses it, every step of it, or it isn't anything* — on top of a foundation where nobody ever asked *him*. He's twenty-two. She's twenty-one. He's been steered at her by a woman with a plan, and he thinks the asking is the ethics. He learned the asking from the one person who never had to ask him. Nobody in this chapter says any of that and it is *screaming*. I would have thrown the book across the room if a single line of it had been spelled out.

Also he started lifting at fifteen because he was a child in a building of grown men and it was "a useful incentive," and I had to sit with that for a second. "I never had to find out. Which was the point." Jesus.

And *Peter.* That's the one I'll carry. Vee asks what his mother calls him and he answers with his real name instead — he took his mother out of the answer and Vee *let him*, and I love her for letting him, and I noticed, and the book noticed I'd notice and didn't underline it. Then at the end she says it once into the dark, not asking for anything, "the way you say a word to keep it." Fine. I'm a person made of mush now.

The scar. Her fingers find it before she's decided to, and he puts his hand on top of hers and presses it *down* onto the seam and says *not tonight* — and doesn't move her hand. A door with a time on it that isn't now. Then she turns her palm up under his so they're palm to palm over it. That is the first time in this entire book someone has been allowed to touch his damage while he was awake for it, and I've been waiting for it since the truck, since her hand holding his shaking one against her belly. It's the companion piece to that moment and I got it with interest, exactly as I asked.

Heat is nearly nothing and I don't mind for once: his whole palm just *holding* the weight of her breast under the quilt, not pressing, not asking, "as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm." That one sentence did more than most of my Kindle.

The math did wobble for me. Gabriel's horn, the coastline, the three sentences of Newton — I know what it's for, it's him unlocked and lit and showing her the one room he's never been thrown out of, and the book is smart enough to have Vee watching him more than listening. What's got its hooks all the way in is the last page, and it is *cruel*. "For no reason she had done anything to deserve." "She had never been so fortunate, or so happy." I know what the stumble was. I've known since the back cover. And having her say *one piece*, *it came to her all of a piece*, using his own beautiful line about the leaf and the stars to describe the thing that was engineered — I want to scream.
