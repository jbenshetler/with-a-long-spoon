REACTION

Okay so first: the diner. Sheri is worth the whole chapter and I'd have read forty more pages of her. Five feet of her, two-tone hair, makeup worn like a dare, stealing his fries, and the only person in fifty chapters who has looked at this man and said the actual sentence. *"Then not saying it matters more to you than having her."* And then — this is the part — she doesn't let him off it, she doesn't soften, she holds his eyes until he looks down, and then she says *"from this seat, that feels pretty shitty."* From this seat. She even tells him it's her angle and not God's and it still lands like a dropped anvil. Everybody else in this book hands Pace the authorship of their own feelings and he collects it like rent. Sheri handed him a bill.

And she *knows about Randi.* "She find out about the brunette, then?" — just tossed off, over a cheeseburger, like it's a thing they've discussed. He took Randi *dancing at the hall.* Sheri hugged him hello and Randi looked at her like a harem applicant and never asked her name. Sheri's been carrying that little humiliation around saving it up for a punchline. And now there's a Brooks and Dunn cover band at that same hall on Valentine's weekend, second Saturday in February, and Sheri has already decided he's coming, *and I don't care what the redhead's got to say about it.* I have a date on the calendar and a woman with no stake and no manners and full information who is going to be standing in that room. I would read a hundred more pages for that night. Text to my friend at 1 a.m.: THE BIKER GIRL KNOWS AND SHE'S BRINGING HIM TO A DANCE.

Then the chapter goes home and gets drunk, and this is where I split.

The whiskey night itself — gorgeous. The bottle somebody gave him that he had to go hunting a glass for, because he doesn't drink alone and has never gone looking for a reason to. Not drunk-as-character-beat; drunk as a man picking a lock on himself. And the book never shows us the sick. It just gives us bleach reaching the bedroom in the morning and a mop and bucket in the hall with the foam crusted above the waterline and *he stepped around them and shut the door.* That's the writing I signed up for. Same with Randi's cashmere blanket slid off the bed, folded and put back where it lives — he was holding her blanket. Nobody says it. And the freezer: rows of chicken, rows of broccoli, every bag the same as the one behind it, *enough in there to go on like this a long time.* The man who makes water-buffalo mozzarella pizza for a girl has a freezer full of prison food for himself. His cooking is a thing he does *at* people. He doesn't feed himself. That one detail did more for me than the entire Daphne section.

Which. Yeah. The Daphne section.

I have been waiting for Daphne since two gates ago, I wrote in my own notes that she'd gone thin and it worried me, and what I got was a stack of short flat sentences telling me the answer. *She had been his tutor, then his best friend, his first girlfriend, his first lover, his first love.* *Then she left.* *He had taught himself that the words were the difference.* *He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him.* I don't need that last one. I had that last one. I've had it since chapter 47. This book spent forty-nine chapters trusting me to read a hand opening on a couch arm, a wine glass moved an inch, a thread of glaze on a lip — and then it stopped and explained its own thesis to me in a voice like a case file. The prose literally changes register: short, subject-verb, no air in it. Maybe that's meant to be the hangover. It read to me like the author got nervous.

And *Then she left* is the second cheat. I still don't know what happened. I got the size of the crater and nothing of the woman. "Her departure consumed his life" is a sentence about a sentence. Meanwhile *I don't go back* — the parents, the actual locked door — is still locked, so this chapter answered the question I'd mostly solved and left the one I hadn't.

But then the truck. God, the truck. His head lifting before he knew he'd moved, the fork stopped halfway, his whole body turning toward a cut in the hill — and *he knew at once and whole that it was the wrong sound, and he did not let go of it.* He kept it on purpose for one more second. That's the most human thing this man has ever done on the page, and it's the only moment in the back half where the book shut up and let me do the work. And then the ugly breakdown over a bowl of boiled chicken, and *Pace picked up his fork. He was not hungry. He ate anyway* — the man who built his whole love language out of appetite, eating with the sound off. That's the chapter. That's Vee's crackers coming back around onto him and nobody had to say so.

The fantasy got me too, and not in a hot way, in a sick-making way: he's not picturing a threesome anymore. He's picturing a *morning after* one. Vee in his sheet with too much honey in her tea, Randi with her feet up, the two of them laughing at something he missed, nobody hurrying. He wants a household. He's been adding to it for weeks like a model railway. And he still cannot say four letters to one of them. The gap between what he wants and what he'll spend is now so wide I can see daylight through it, and *he still thinks the reasons count.* "I'm there every weekend. The weeknights were hers and I gave her that." Buddy. She wanted you to fight for those nights. You handed her the authorship again and called it generosity and Sheri clocked it in one line: *she didn't ask you for reasons.*

What I'm uneasy about structurally: that's three in a row of a person alone in a room with a phone in their hand. Randi in Gstaad, Vee in Ohio, Pace in the recliner. It's a shape, I see the shape, the triptych is deliberate and 48 and 49 were both strong — but 50 is the third chair in the same circle and by the end of it I could feel myself wanting two of these people in a room together more than I wanted any more interiority. The book has now fully explained everyone to me. There is nothing left to find out about why; there's only what happens. So happen, please.
