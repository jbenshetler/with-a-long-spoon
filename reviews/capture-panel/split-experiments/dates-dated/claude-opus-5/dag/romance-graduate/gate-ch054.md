Okay so I opened this expecting a breather and instead got handed the thing I've been shouting about for four chapters, and it was handed to me over a sink, with a plate in his hand, in the flattest voice in the book.

*I want to meet your people.*

That's the doorway. That's December. That's *out there, what are we, how do I say what this is to Cassie* — and three weekends later he answers it himself, unprompted, while drying a pot. And of course he answers it the only way he knows how: not with the noun, with *logistics*. He will not spend the four letters but he will build her an entire room. I noticed it and she didn't, and I'm sitting here with the same feeling I had about the two shirts in the kitchen — furious and undone at once. The man's whole ethic is *believe what I do*, and now he's doing something so enormous and so exactly aimed at the wound that she will never need to ask the question again. Which might be the most elegant dodge he's pulled yet. Watch: she retires it, like she retired the crackers, except this time she gets paid for retiring it, so it'll stay retired.

And then the listening. God. *Your heel came up out of the right one when you pushed off.* She said one half-sentence about Meg's boots on a mountain in October and he filed it, and *Kayla's the one who can talk the paint off a wall* is her own line coming back out of his mouth like a receipt. Fifty-four chapters of a man who won't say a word and he's been keeping every single one of hers on a shelf with her friends' names on it. That's the counter-argument to everything I've been holding against him and I resent how well it lands.

**"Tables have ends. Somebody ends up at one."** That's him. That's the whole man in nine words — the person who notices who's uncomfortable before they know they're going to be, who built Vee a dress because a dress was the shape of the problem. And it's the structural answer to what she couldn't solve two minutes earlier: she ran every restaurant in town and found somebody being a good sport at every table, and *she'd watched somebody be fine about it before. She wasn't going to look at when.* The book didn't explain that line to me. Thank you. It's Chili's. He was fine about it for two hours and she mistook it for shame at being seen with her, and she's half an inch from knowing, and she put it down. The way she did it — *the picture didn't want anything from her. It just stood there until she'd seen it* — and then she turned it into a kindness: *will you pick the place?* She used the thing she learned about him to protect him without once saying she was doing it. She's become fluent in them. It's beautiful and it's going to cost her.

Theo. She volunteered Theo, started explaining, and he said *bring Theo* and reached for the next pan. She read it as security. I read it as: a man who won't claim you can't be jealous where you can see it. The absence of a flinch is not the same as trust and she doesn't have the vocabulary to tell them apart yet.

But here's what I actually did, out loud, alone in my kitchen: **let me handle it.** He's picking the place. He knows the area. A room where the table has no ends, a floor, everybody at once — *it's the hall.* It's Sheri's hall, the Brooks and Dunn cover band, second Saturday in February, Valentine's weekend, which Sheri has already ordered him to attend and already told him she doesn't care what the redhead has to say about it. Vee is going to build, out of pure love and her own two hands, the one room in the state that contains all of it: Randi who has nine words of math with him in public and a locked bracelet under her sleeve, Sheri who knows about the brunette and never learned her name, Cassie who knows nothing, Kayla who cannot stop talking, and the two people who chose her together having to perform strangers in front of her floor. He said *I'd like it.* He has no idea. Or — he does, and he's doing it anyway, because he wants the household and this is as close as he can get without a word.
