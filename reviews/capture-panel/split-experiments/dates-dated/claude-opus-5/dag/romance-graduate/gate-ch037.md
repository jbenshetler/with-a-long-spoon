REACTION

She swims. Of course she swims — and I didn't know, and now that I know I can't believe I didn't. A girl who's spent the whole book negotiating with her own body, bracing in doorways, taking small steps to keep a slit shut, holding a hand at her hip in a room full of somebody's parents — and she has had, since she was twelve, seventy-five minutes three times a week where the body just *works* and has no opinions whatsoever. "Her body, which had opinions all day long every day of her life, had none at all down here. It only worked." That's the best single thing anyone's told me about Vee and it arrived without a man in the room. Nobody taught her this. It isn't Randi's, it isn't his, it has no fingerprints on it but her own, and the chapter knows that and doesn't say it. Thank God. Finally something in her life that nothing was lent to her for.

And the green-and-white suit with nowhere left to go on her, and *she looked at the water instead of at that.* One clause. Didn't explain itself. The lane she took her first week back and kept taking until two strangers organize themselves around her every Tuesday without a word — that's a whole portrait of how this girl occupies space. She doesn't ask. She just keeps showing up in the third lane.

The thing I'll be chewing on: four strokes, somewhere in the seventh piece, where she couldn't tell whether the man she was turning over was Pace or somebody she'd built out of wanting him. *Whether he was that good or she had made him that good.* That is the first time in thirty-seven chapters anyone inside this book has looked at the arrangement with any suspicion at all, and it came from *her*, underwater, where there's no one to perform for and no one to route it through. And it got four strokes, and then the wall came. That's exactly right and it's unbearable. Also: *Do you love him* arrived with "a pressure under her ribs with a dread riding it." Dread. Not shyness, not *it's too soon*. Dread. She doesn't know why she said maybe. I think she does know and it's sitting in a lane she won't swim.

What bugged me, and it bugged me enough to notice twice: I already watched the drawer. Chapter 35 jumped me forward to the next Friday, showed me him folding his entire ordered life into one drawer to give her the top one, no note, never a note — and then this chapter sends her up and down the pool worrying *did he mind*, and I'm sitting here with the answer in my hand going, honey, I've seen it, he made you a place. It takes the air out of one of her two engines. I don't mind a bridge chapter — I've said the best scene in a book was two people talking — but I minded being ahead of her on the small question when the book's whole trick is keeping me a step ahead on the *big* one.

And the shape is starting to worry me. Thirty-four was a peak — the brunch, the blue stall light, a thread of sweetness on her lip from a pastry she never touched, which is still the most frightening sentence in this book. Then thirty-five gave me a week of sex in past-perfect recap (*they hadn't made the kitchen, they hadn't made the hall*) — lovely, warm, and told to me rather than given to me; the book has enough credit with me to spend one of those, but it spent it. Then the dorm, then the pool. That's three chapters with nothing happening on the page in real time with another body in the room, and the only person who has *said* anything consequential since the glaze was Cassie, twice, in four flat words. Randi has been offscreen since she drove off into traffic and I am starting to feel the withholding rather than the tension. The glaze is still on that lip, book. I know you know.
