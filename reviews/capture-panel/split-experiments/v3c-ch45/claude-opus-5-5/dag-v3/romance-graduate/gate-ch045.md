I asked for him and I got him, and it turns out it's the same night again. New Year's Eve. Randi in a hotel bed beside a perfect stranger, Vee in her twin bed under the swimming trophies, and Pace in a recliner with whiskey and a phone full of pictures. Three rooms, one night, and nobody texting the person they're thinking about. I'll admit the triptych got me. I've been complaining about solo cold-bed vigils for three chapters, and this one is the third panel that makes the other two make sense. I forgive the last two a little now.

The diner is wonderful. A double-wide that turned into a diner around the Carter administration, Elvis under the tabletop glass, "The usual?" The kid sets the double in front of the man and the single in front of the woman "with the grave confidence of arithmetic," and Sheri slides the plates back and pulls the shake toward her like it's owed. Sheri is the best thing to happen to this book in ten chapters. She's five feet tall, her makeup is slightly off and worn like a dare, and she said exactly what I've been yelling since chapter 47: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you, from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." He looked down. GOOD. Then she stole his fries. I love her. Her Paige story, the girlfriend who kept saying "call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas," and his "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice," is the first time I've seen him say the good stuff to someone who isn't in the game. "Don't waste the good stuff on me" is perfect.

"The philosophy of your food." "Lord almighty. Get over yourself." I laughed out loud. Someone finally said it.

The bombshell I didn't see coming: Randi hasn't been to his house since the green panties. Six weeks. I had no idea. I've been picturing the pipeline running the whole time, and it's been shut since November. So in Gstaad, "Pace went straight to her" was about a man she'd already walked away from. And he won't call her, because "the choosing was hers." That's his whole religion, consent as a reason never to reach. It's the same reason he didn't follow Vee's taillights. It's decent and it's cowardly, and Sheri basically said so.

Then the fantasy, and my stomach dropped. Drunk, missing both of them, he's still building it: the three of them in his kitchen, Vee in his sheet, Randi with her feet up. He's been "adding to it for weeks." And "Whether the room would ask more of her than she had in her." He's still running the plan, while both women are lying in other rooms falling apart. My suspicion of him is back in full.

Then the morning, which wrecked me. The mop and bucket, the bleach, the foam crust above the waterline. He was sick and cleaned it up alone. Then the freezer: rows of identical bags of chicken and broccoli, "prison food." The man who builds those slow, aching meals for everyone else eats boiled bags alone at a cold counter. I've been tracking food as Randi's wound this whole book, and it turns out it's his too. "It went down because he told it to." That's the best line in the chapter.

The truck going past, his body leaning toward the turn-in, and him sobbing over the bowl. Yes. That's what I needed. He's not a carved figure. He's a twenty-two-year-old who got left once and never recovered.

It's earned, so it didn't push me out. It did push me back a step.

And Daphne, "a girl with brown hair and glasses who laughed with her hand over her mouth." She isn't a predator. She's a girl he loved who left. My dark Daphne theory just got a lot softer. I'm still not sure what "her departure" means, and the scar is still a closed door.
