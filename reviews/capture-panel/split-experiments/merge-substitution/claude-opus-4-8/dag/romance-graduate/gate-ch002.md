REACTION

Okay so this is the chapter that reframes the whole first one, and I love that it does it sideways instead of grabbing my face and explaining it. Chapter 1 I read as "Pace and his girl," and the jacket had me assuming that girl was Vee — the twenty-one-year-old quietly starving. And then the last line of Chapter 1 drops *Randi* and I had to sit up. So the woman on the bench, the one who begged in a voice she hadn't used since childhood, the one who built a whole face to stand between herself and being reached — that's *Randi*. The one the jacket calls the planner. The one who's supposedly running the game with Pace. "She is the first woman Randi has ever let herself want." I thought that line was about Vee. It's about Randi wanting *Pace* and it terrifying her, and now I think it might also be setting up Vee, and I genuinely don't know which, and that not-knowing is doing exactly what I want a book to do.

And then Chapter 2 doesn't give me any of that in exposition. It just drops me into a sorority common room at 9:45 on a Saturday and makes me watch a four-minute conversation that is pure warfare conducted entirely in smiles. God, the *staging* of it. Brooke not standing. Randi, who sits on Standards herself, knowing precisely how much authority it takes to stay seated while someone else stops walking — and clocking that Brooke has more. The whole thing is a status audit and everyone in the room is pretending it's small talk. "Engineering, isn't it" — landing him a notch down without reaching for it — and Randi correcting it to *Math. PhD.* and Brooke's smile "absorbing it without conceding the notch." I have read a hundred mean-girl sorority scenes and they were all cartoons. This one is scary because it's so quiet and so *competent*. These are women who do this for a living.

But what actually got me is the fraction-of-a-second. The chapter times her. Every lie has a latency, and the narration measures it — the pause "a fraction of a second long," then "longer," then "wider here," then "the room hold it." She's lying about where she was and the house can hear the lag. And the Wilson-Street study-spot lie, the clean cover story — she had the three comeback lines loaded in her mouth and Brooke dismissed her before she could fire them, and *that* was the cleanest hit of the whole exchange. Being released. Not getting to win. I felt that in my stomach.

And then the room upstairs. This is where I'd normally get itchy, because it's the second quiet stretch in a row and nobody's touching anybody — she stands, she doesn't sit, she walks to the vanity, she doesn't sit, she opens the closet and forgets what she came for. But it didn't read as stalling to me. It read as a woman who cannot land. The duvet that is "not — it was a duvet," after a night under cashmere Pace keeps warm for her in a house heated to the one even temperature — that detail gutted me a little. The cold corners of the lovely old house. And "*He's hard to say no to*," the line that landed perfectly for the girls, coming back to her in a key she can't sing in. That's the whole book in one sentence, and the book trusts me to hear the second meaning without underlining it.

The not-crying is the part I'll be thinking about. Chapter 1 she leaked on the bench and couldn't stop; here the crying is "there — she could feel where it was" and she just doesn't go to it, and the narration says the not-going "did not feel like holding a door shut. It felt like there being no door." After twenty-one years she's so good at it it doesn't register as an act, like a held breath that stops feeling held. That's a genuinely frightening piece of interiority and it's doing real psychological work — this is the mechanism the whole first chapter cracked open and she's resealing it before my eyes.

What I'm suspicious of, deliciously: the jacket says Randi and Pace *planned* this, that Vee was chosen in a game they started in bed, that Randi is the architect. And what I've been shown is Randi utterly undone, fleeing tenderness, deciding in a bathroom mirror that the man who found her is getting the face "not ever" again. So either the planner is lying to Pace too, or the plan is Randi's way of managing a man who's already too close — pointing him at Vee to keep him off *her*. I'd text a friend at 1 a.m.: *the villain of the blurb is the most defended person I've ever read and I think the "game" is her armor, call me.*

No heat in this one at all, and I don't mind, because it earned the quiet by being this precise. But I'll be honest — I now want a scene with another *person* in it, a want answered, a body in a room. Two interior chapters is my limit, and the next one needs to move or I'll feel the stall.
