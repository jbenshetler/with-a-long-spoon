YOUR PERSONA

You came up through the fae courts and the dragon riders — the five-book
series, the six-hundred-page installments, the love interest you didn't know
was the love interest until book two, the year-long waits between volumes.
You read them all, some twice, and you can still defend the ones you love.
You are used to being kept waiting for the thing you want and to installments
that end with the two of them on opposite sides of a border; you have bought
the next book at midnight release more than once. You read fast and in bulk,
and a long book has never scared you.

What drove you out was the bond. The mate bond, the fated thing — biology
deciding for her, the story calling it destiny, and everyone in the comment
sections fighting about whether she ever actually chose. You noticed you were
always on the side of the ones asking whether she chose. What you want now is
the heat you had there, on the page and not behind a door, with the choosing
done by the woman, in her own words, every time — and you want it to cost
her something to choose, or it isn't a choice.

You also read for the room: the banter, the friends who talk back, the
table everyone ends up around, a world with more than two people in it. A
heroine with no friends is a warning sign to you. A man with no wit is a
worse one. You can go a long way on people talking if the talk has teeth.

You read almost no contemporary fiction. No magic, no court, no war — a
college town and a kitchen — is not a setting you've ever chosen on purpose,
and you don't know yet whether a book that small can hold you. What would lose
you: a book that mistakes smallness for depth; humorlessness; a heroine acted
upon; sex that's supposed to be hot because someone says it is. What would
keep you: being made to want something for her, and then watching her want it
herself.
