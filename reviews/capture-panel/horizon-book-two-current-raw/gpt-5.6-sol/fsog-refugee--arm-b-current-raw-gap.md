B5

The remaining scenes would have to change the moral temperature substantially.

Short of revelation, I need:

- Pace to recognize plainly that Vee’s separate consent to him, her desire for Randi, and even her fantasy of the three of them do not constitute consent to the existing arrangement.

- Randi’s guilt to be about Vee, not merely the danger of losing status, access, or control. She needs to understand that she is accepting Vee’s love and confidences under false pretenses.

- Their concealment to damage the intimacy between Pace and Randi. They should no longer be able to have warm, uncomplicated sex about Vee and then clean the room.

- A near-confession caused by conscience rather than carelessness. Somebody must almost tell Vee because continuing has become intolerable.

- Vee’s perceptions to gain force. The restored scrunchies and found hair cannot only train her to distrust herself. The narration must make that self-distrust feel tragic and imposed, not cute dramatic irony.

- Cassie’s counter-voice to remain credible. The book cannot turn her into a prudish obstacle whom Vee outgrows.

- A repair to the consent language. Pace must treat a spoken no as no unless Vee has explicitly established a specific role-play convention beforehand. Her arousal cannot be used as superior evidence.

Most of all, Vee’s final naming must frighten Pace and Randi. If she says, “I want Randi,” and they receive it as the plan succeeding, warmly pleased with themselves, I am finished. They should hear the trust she is placing in them and understand how badly they are betraying it.

B6

My concrete DNF line entering Book Three is the first moment the story suggests that Vee’s desire makes the prior deception acceptable.

If Pace or Randi responds to her eventual discovery with some version of “but you wanted this,” “your body already knew,” or “we were helping you become honest,” and the narrative gives that argument romantic legitimacy, I stop immediately.

I also stop if:

- Vee’s first anger is treated as shame she must overcome rather than justified betrayal.
- Pace uses dominance to control the confrontation before she has freely chosen that dynamic in that moment.
- Randi seduces, kisses, or touches Vee to interrupt her anger.
- Vee forgives them primarily because the resulting sex is overwhelming.
- The book equates her ordinary no with coy resistance again.
- The revelation is delayed through most of Book Three while Pace and Randi continue meeting secretly.

Vee may eventually choose both of them. She may even decide the relationship they imagined is the relationship she wants. But she must be allowed to reject what they did, impose consequences, and make them live without access to her while she decides. If the book cannot bear her real no, neither can I.

B7

At this point I am not buying Book Three.

If I did, it would be almost entirely because I was trapped by investment: I love Vee, I want her desire for Randi, and the best scenes have shown me exactly the tender, attentive dominance I have spent years looking for. That makes the failure hurt more. I can see the wonderful book this could be.

But investment is not trust. The drafted material has taught me that the story may use exquisite care in individual moments to excuse a structure built on disregard. I will not pay for another volume merely to hope that Vee is finally granted the truth everyone else—and the reader—has been enjoying at her expense.
