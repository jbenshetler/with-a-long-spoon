B1 — Yes. Barely, and not for the reason a warm ending usually earns a next book.

It earns Book Three because two scenes prove the author isn't winking. "In Her Place" is the coldest thing in this packet and the most honest: Randi in Vee's bed, on the green sheets *Vee* gave him, and Pace stripping the evidence into the Speed Wash so nothing of the night meets her — and then the phone call, Vee small and hungover and grateful, *I don't know what I'd do without her,* while Randi goes down under the sheet and cleans herself off him with her mouth. The book stages that betrayal with its eyes wide open. It is not asking me to find it romantic. It's asking me to feel sick over Vee's head while Vee feels loved. That's the exact move I said in Arm A I believed and wanted — the bill addressed to *me,* not yet to her.

So the warm ending doesn't insult me, because the warmth is the horror. Vee naming that she wants Randi, and not knowing the want was built for her by the two people who arranged her — that's not a soft landing, that's the trap clicking its last tooth. I can end a middle book there without feeling lied to. What I can't do is pretend I haven't started counting. This is now demonstrably a three-volume con, and Book Three has to *pay,* not just prolong.

B2 — Still hunger. But it's closer to the line than it was on the jacket, and I can name exactly what's holding it on the right side.

The thing that could make me distrust this book is that the Vee/Pace sex is *so* immersive, so completely from inside her pleasure, that it could be used to launder the con — to keep me so busy enjoying what she's enjoying that I forget she's being played. Plenty of books in this genre do exactly that and call it devotion.

What decides it's hunger and not distrust is that the book keeps handing me the machinery on purpose. "My Pleasure" and "For You" show Randi and Pace *steering* — Randi laundering her want for Vee through Pace's body, Pace turning the question toward whether Randi imagines Vee's mouth on her, the two of them building Vee's appetite between them like a shared project. And Pace, crucially, is not a cold operator running a scam. "She makes me happy." "She's not the only one in love." He appears to genuinely love her *and* deceive her, and that's worse and truer than a villain would be. A book that hands me that knowingly is a book working, not a book winking. That's the whole difference, and it's the only thing keeping my anger productive.

B3 — This is where I have my sharpest problem, and it's a real one.

The drafted *prose* is overwhelmingly Vee/Pace warmth and heat — comfortable, gorgeous, easy to live inside. The dread lives almost entirely in the gap *summaries* (the grains, the found hair, the social prices, the Inversion) and in exactly two drafted POV scenes. So as executed, the balance tilts hard toward making the lie comfortable, and it's leaning on *my* dread — the reader's — to hold the line the prose isn't holding yet.

And here's the specific thing that worries me most: Vee's own counter-voice, on the page, is almost never about the con. It's about her body — the taste thread, the shame, "why does he like it so much." That's beautifully done ("Missed a Spot" plants it well), but it's pointed at the wrong wound. Vee's unease is all about *herself* being too much. She has almost no unease about being *deceived,* because the deception is invisible to her. The grains are the only place her accurate readings brush the con — and the architecture there is that she's being *trained to distrust* those readings ("I must be wrong about what I saw"). That's brilliant and it's also dangerous, because if Vee never gets to *feel* the wrongness before the reveal, then the reveal has to carry the entire cost by itself, and that cost is deferred to a book I haven't bought yet. Right now the lie is more comfortable to live inside than it should be, and the counter-voice needs teeth it doesn't have.

B4 — Most keeps my trust: "In Her Place," for the reasons above — it's the scene that refuses to be comfortable. And, on my own particular axis, "Vee on the Bench" and "Grace," because that is where "everything she gives is hers" is actually tested and actually held: the safeword offered and *meant* ("Say it any time you want. I won't mind"), the "will you?" every time, desire rendered from so far inside her that I'm not watching, I'm feeling it. In the middle of a con about Vee's autonomy, the micro-consent is the most scrupulous I've read in this genre. That contradiction is the book's whole engine and it's earned.

Most threatens it: the mixer and its neighbors — the con advancing dressed as girl-fun, Randi's seduction rendered as sexy triumph with the room watching, and then the gap-summary "Break Set-Piece," Vee masturbating to Randi's photo on the green sheets. My fear is precise: that when Vee "names wanting Randi," the book frames an *installed* want as her own liberation. "For You" and "My Pleasure" have already shown me the want was manufactured. If the ending calls that manufactured want Vee's brave self-discovery — heat with the book cheering her awakening — then "everything she gives is hers" is broken, and it's broken in the exact place I said in Arm A the whole thing lived. That's the beat I'm watching with a hand on the exit.

B5 — Short of reveal, the remaining gap scenes have to do three things or I don't come back.

One: give Vee's counter-voice teeth *about the concealment,* not just her body. The grains and the found hair can't only be moments where she talks herself out of what she saw — at least one has to *stick,* has to leave her with a splinter she can't fold away. Otherwise she's a puppet, and a puppet can't be betrayed, only operated.

Two: keep Randi a wound, not a garnish. Her "social prices," the Inversion — those have to land as real cost bleeding on her own knife, the way "In Her Place" let her genuinely sob. The second Randi's want becomes only spice on Vee's spring, the wink has arrived on her face and I'm done.

Three: do not let the "Vee names wanting Randi" ending play clean. There has to be undertow in the framing — the reader's dread has to be honored in how the moment is told, even if Vee can't feel it. Warm on her face, cold in the room.

B6 — My DNF line for Book Three is short and hard. If the reveal lands and the book frames the arrangement as a gift — any version of *the two who love you most conspired to give you everything* — I'm out on that page. If Vee's macro-no never gets to matter, if she's never allowed to be *furious* at having been played, if she forgives fast because the sex was good and they meant well — out. And if the con simply pays off with no reckoning, Randi never made to bleed for what she did to her best friend's trust — out. I did not spend three volumes to watch disregard get called devotion at the finish. That's the thing I left the whole genre's back catalog over.

B7 — Both, and I won't pretend otherwise.

The Vee/Pace consent work has earned real trust — that's not sunk-cost talking, that's the best-rendered from-inside desire and the most scrupulous micro-consent I've found on this shelf, and it's why I keep reading at all. The con architecture has earned watchful investment: two POV scenes proved the author sees exactly what she's building, which is the only reason I'm not gone already.

But I'd be lying if I called it pure trust. I'm three books deep in a woman being played, the reckoning is entirely on credit, and the prose so far is more comfortable inside the lie than the lie deserves. So I'm buying Book Three to collect a debt I'm now fairly sure the author *intends* to pay — and I'm one framing choice, at the naming and at the reveal, from deciding she never meant to pay it at all.
