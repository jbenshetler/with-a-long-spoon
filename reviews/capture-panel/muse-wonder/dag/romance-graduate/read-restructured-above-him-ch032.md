REACTION

Oh, this one got me in the throat, not anywhere else, and I mean that as the highest compliment I have in me right now.

Because of course the drawer. Of course it's a drawer.

I've been sitting here braced for Pace to finally say *something* — not even the big word, just *stay*, or *yours belongs here*, or literally one sentence I could hold onto the way Vee is trying to — and instead he goes downstairs in the night, presumably, and crams his own razor and blades and comb too-tight into the bottom drawer so she can have the top one. The easy one. The one your hand goes to first. No note. There is never a note.

And Vee standing there with a hand on each open drawer just *knowing* — he hadn't told her it didn't matter, he'd gone and made her a place instead. That's the whole man. That's the whole impossible problem of the whole man. If a friend texted me that at 1 a.m. I'd be like *marry him*, and in this book I'm sitting here wanting to shake him until words fall out, because a place in the bathroom is not the same as a place he will *name* when somebody asks, and Vee knows it, and she has decided — tonight, warm, fed, fucked twice, held — that she can live inside the everything-he-did and not go hungry for the saying. She actually lays it down that plain: *I love him, there, he has never once said it, he has to feel it.* My chest hurt reading that because I believe her and I don't believe her at all. That was not a woman at peace. That was a woman negotiating with herself in the dark next to a sleeping man, counting the spin at the door and the salmon and the tomatoes still warm off the sill like coins, trying to make them add up to the one coin he won't spend.

And god, the cruelty the book does so quietly I almost missed it until it hit me a minute later — her at his little table in his shirt, feet tucked up, happily telling *him* how she met Randi. *Walked right into her outside the dining hall, small world, she came and sat right down with me next morning.* Telling the lie they built for her as if it's her own favorite memory, and smiling into her wine about it. And the goodbye kiss story. She tells him about the kiss-kiss — *it's French, I guess* — while doing that thing with her tongue on her lower lip, the pastry glaze still on her from Randi in the last chapter sitting right there in my head, and I watched his grin spread and then *leave*, and under it the look she knows from his bed, and she laughs it off, *not that kind of French*, goes to him at the stove, finds him hard, says *I'll pretend it was that kind of French after you feed me* — joking about the exact true thing, in the exact room where it's true, to the exact man who already knows. I had to put the thing down for a second. She is handing him every thread and he is standing there with a spatula letting her.

Also — *Peter*. Whispered to him asleep, barely breath, not meant to be heard. That did something awful to me. She gets his real name only in the dark, only when he can't answer it. She gets the top drawer, the scrunchies by the dozen, juice held to her mouth, a man who brings her all the way over more than once before he takes anything and then comes back an hour later like he'd been thinking about her through the whole salmon. Everything. Everything except a word for what they are, exactly like the jacket promised, and tonight the everything felt so real I understood completely why she'd stay, and completely why it's going to wreck her.

The little tail of worry she carries out of the bathroom the first morning — *had he minded? he hadn't said, he never said* — that's the tell. Even her happiness has a tail on it. And then a week later her fingers close on nothing by the tap and her stomach drops straight through the floor, *he'd cleared it, he'd minded after all*, and I dropped right with her, because that is what silence teaches a person to expect. The reveal of the drawer rescued her and honestly it rescued me, but it doesn't un-teach the lesson. Next silence, she'll drop again.

I am so gone for these two in the domestic register it's embarrassing. Him folded around her at the mirror with his eyes half shut, mouth at her shoulder, asking for nothing. Her eating like a field hand now, eating the last tomato too, bras not fitting, bag finally brought and its whole disordered spill across his one clean surface. This is the grown-up intimacy I have been starving for in every interchangeable billionaire book — somebody's actual life making room. I just also know Randi's mouth is on her lower lip while she sleeps in that bed, and Pace knows that too, and let her joke about it. The tenderness and the rot are in the same kitchen. They always are in this book. That's why I can't leave it.
