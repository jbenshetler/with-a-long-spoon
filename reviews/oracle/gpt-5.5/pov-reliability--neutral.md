**Question asked (verbatim):** When you're inside a character's head and they're describing someone they love or want — Vee on Pace, or on Randi — do you take that as how the person really is, or as colored by what the narrator feels? Walk me through how much you trust each viewpoint's read of the others.

---

I take it first as colored by the person wanting, but not therefore false. With Vee especially, I trust the bodily truth of what she feels before I trust the completeness of her interpretation. When she describes Pace’s care — the pencil given back, the moleskin on her heel, the drawer, the warmed house, the way he cooks — I believe those things happened and I believe they mean tenderness. But I don’t fully trust her conclusion that tenderness equals security, because again and again she edits around what she cannot bear: the cropped photo, the flannel becoming a substitute, the missing word.

With Randi, I’m even warier, because Vee is so hungry for Randi’s naming. Randi’s warmth feels real to me; I specifically remember the “dear friend” moment, the shoe fitting, the long hug in “My Friend Randi.” But Vee receives Randi’s precision as love, while I also feel the steering in it. Randi often understands Vee accurately, but that accuracy is part of her power.

Pace’s view of Vee I trust in a different way. When he talks about her to Sheri — the walnut chair, the math book, the room going brighter — I believe he sees something real in her beyond her body. But I don’t trust Pace to see the whole moral situation clearly, because he keeps answering the adjacent question: actions instead of words, care instead of naming, patience instead of disclosure.

Randi’s view may be the most emotionally true and the least safe. In “Gone” and “Fairytale,” I believed that she genuinely loves or wants Vee, and that Vee has gotten past her defenses. But Randi also converts feeling into strategy almost instantly. So my trust is split: I trust the intensity of these viewpoints, and often the details they notice, but I don’t trust any of them as full accounts of the other person. Desire in this book is a lens that reveals and distorts at the same time.
