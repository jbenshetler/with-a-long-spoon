**Question asked (verbatim):** Is the book working on you — where does it run hot, where does it go slack?

---

Yes, it is working on me, strongly. It runs hottest where erotic consent, shame, and being seen are all tangled together: Randi on the bench when her “please” becomes real; Vee being measured for the dress; the green panties and first sex in “Famished”; the spanking discovery in “We Find Out”; and the coat-with-nothing-underneath return to Pace. The book’s best heat is rarely just mechanics. It is the body giving Vee away before her mind can manage the story.

It also runs hot in the talking scenes with Randi: brunches, practice room, shoes, confession. Those scenes are dangerous because Randi names Vee’s desire so beautifully that I feel the relief before the manipulation. The sapphic charge becomes more and more undeniable, especially around the mouth kisses, the foot-in-hand shoe fitting, the “sticky fingers” ambiguity, and Vee being unable to finish touching herself because Randi’s imagined looking enters the room.

Where it goes slack is not often, but sometimes the symbolic lectures and metaphors feel a little too perfectly aimed: “latent variables,” Simpson’s paradox, the stats material mirroring the emotional plot. I usually forgive it because Vee’s body carries the scene, but I do notice the author’s hand. Pace also risks becoming over-capable at points: mathematician, cook, furniture maker, dressmaker, powerlifter, perfect attentive lover. The book mostly saves him by giving him emotional blockage and secrecy, but the pile-up occasionally gleams too much.

The deepest tension, and the reason I kept wanting the next chapter, is that the tenderness feels real and the consent in each scene often feels real, while the larger arrangement remains dishonest. That makes the heat more troubling rather than less convincing. It is lushest when I want the thing to happen and dread what it means at the same time.
