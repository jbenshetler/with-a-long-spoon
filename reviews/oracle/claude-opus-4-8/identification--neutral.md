# Oracle (grounded) — identification · neutral

*model: claude-opus-4-8 · probe: identification · tier: neutral · stage: end (whole book) · battery: oracle-battery.json*

**Question asked (verbatim):** Put yourself in each of their shoes. Do you get why they did what they did — could you see yourself doing the same in their situation?

---

Yes, and to different degrees, which surprised me — the ones I understood in my gut weren't always the ones I expected to.

**Vee.** Her I get completely, all the way down, and yes, I could see myself doing it. Every single "yes" she gives is legible to me from the inside — because the book kept showing me the machinery under it: a girl who ate apple slices trimmed thin, who carried the rolled towel at her side and not against her, who went underwater because it was the one place nobody wanted anything. Once I understood she'd been taught her own appetite was shameful, I understood why being *seen* and told the wanting was good would undo her. Walking to Pace's door in the coat with nothing under it ("Nothing Underneath") — that's the bravest, freest thing she does, and I felt exactly why she did it. The part I most recognized as human was smaller and sadder: editing the middle out of the story for Cassie ("Toenails"), zipping her dress back up rather than being honestly naked in front of the one friend who loves her clean. Protecting the good thing by curating it. I've done a version of that. I'd do it again.

**Pace.** Here's where it forks. The *seduction* I understand — the froyo, the fitting, the dress matched to her and not to the color she named — a man reading someone down to the angle of an apple slice. What I could not, in the end, put myself inside was the refusal. "Seconds," "Believe Me," the diner with Sheri ("The Usual") — a man who will give her his whole body, cook for her, clear a drawer, crack his voice on "I missed you," and still not spend three syllables. Sheri named it plainly — "not saying it matters more to you than having her" — and he looked down and didn't argue. I don't get that from the inside; I only get that it's a rule he holds like a wound (the hallway desk, Daphne, "I don't go back"). And the coldest thing — the pointing game, choosing a stranger off a lawn as a game with his lover — I understand it as appetite and control, but no, I can't see myself doing it. That's the line the book kept me on the wrong side of.

**Randi.** She's the one who moved me most, and the one whose *why* I understood against my own better judgment. After "Gone" and "Fairytale," I could see the whole shape of her: a woman hollowed by being perfect, who learned at that Gstaad breakfast table that the face was all anyone wanted, who is warm only where another body touches her and carries the cold back out. Her decision not to tell Vee — "let Vee arrive at it on her own, keep the brunches exactly as they were" — is a handler's plan and a coward's love in the same sentence, and I understood it completely, which frightened me. "She was not brave enough to lose her. She had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be." Could I see myself keeping a person close by keeping one word under cover, because saying it might cost me them? Yes. That's the answer I least wanted to give. That's the one that got closest to me.

**Cassie**, for contrast, is the one whose choices I understood as *virtue* rather than damage — declining to mine Vee, letting the "maybe" stand, saying "keep the shirt" instead of "wait." Reading a friend and choosing not to force the door. I'd want to be her. I'm not sure I'd manage it.

So: I get all of them, but understanding isn't the same as being able to do it. Vee and Randi I could inhabit — one out of hunger, one out of fear. Pace's withholding and the original game I understood as facts about him without ever being able to feel myself choosing them, and I think the book meant me to sit exactly there: close enough to the devils to sup with them, not quite able to pick up their spoon.
