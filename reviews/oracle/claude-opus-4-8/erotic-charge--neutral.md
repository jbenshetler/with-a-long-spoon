**Question asked (verbatim):** Is the book working on you — where does it run hot, where does it go slack?

---

Yes — it worked on me, and it worked in a way I couldn't hold at arm's length even when I wanted to. The trick it pulls, and pulls almost every chapter, is that I read the whole book ahead of Vee. From the very first chapter, when "There you are, Randi" turned the bench scene over in my hands, I was carrying the thing she doesn't know — that she was pointed at across a lawn, collided with on purpose, chosen as a game. So the swoon and the dread ran on the same wire the whole way, and I couldn't unbraid them, and I stopped trying to. That doubling is the book's whole engine, and when it's firing I was helplessly in it.

Where it runs hottest for me, oddly, is almost never the actual sex. It's the clothed, sublimated stuff. "A Round" — a fully dressed man with a tape measure and a girl on a box, the wet satin tracked in real time — is more charged than most graphic scenes I've read. The dance-frame lesson in "Lesson" ("close it — come all the way in"). The truck in "Leave No Trace," where he stands out in the rain with his back turned and she undresses on her own nerve and he never touches her. The measuring, the withheld bedroom door in "Two Towels," the burgundy dress "matched to her, not the dress." That's the book's real heat: being seen, choosing to be seen, the shame turning to want against the mother's voice — and it peaks when nobody's hand is on anyone. "Famished" and "We Find Out" earn their consummations, but they land because forty pages of restraint sit behind them.

It also runs hot in the cold chapters — the ones with no heat at all. "Water Wings" (Vee underwater, "the one part of it that had never been taken") undid me more than any sex. "Standards," the morning-after face performed under Brooke's interrogation. "Rock," where the Daphne story recolored everything I'd read about his courtliness. Those landed because the interior life is genuinely there; the tenderness is real, which is exactly what makes the machinery under it frightening.

Where it goes slack: the debrief structure. Vee has a good time and then tells someone about it — Cassie, then Randi, then Cassie again. "Rye," "What to Wear," "Turned Up," "Toenails," "Fed" come in a cluster, and by the third or fourth "Vee retells the shirt/the fitting to a friend" I could feel the shape of the beat before it arrived. Each one earns its keep on something — Cassie going rapt, the confession with a hole ladled out of it — but the pattern got visible. Same with the twin brunch-extractions ("How It's Done," then "Fed"): the second is gentler and Vee's own contribution keeps it alive, but I flagged to myself that a third run at that exact set piece would show the gears. And the late break — "Seconds," "Under the Rug," "Bare" — is three chapters on one wound (the withheld word), and while each finds a fresh image and a colder register, I was ready for two people back in a room by the end of it.

The one recurring device I felt as a thumb on the scale is the stats lecture that's secretly about the plot — latent variables, substitution, Simpson's paradox, outliers. Clever the first two times; by "All Told" I felt handled rather than swept, the professor reading the book's own thesis over Vee's oblivious head. It usually wears comedy as cover, so I forgave it, but I saw the hand.

What keeps it from ever going fully slack, even in the aftermath run, is that the book kept moving my feeling about the people. Randi didn't flatten into a villain — "Gone," "Fairytale," "The Outlier" (the ragged "cheating bastards" aimed too near to be meant for strangers) made her tragic and culpable at once, and I couldn't collapse the love into the con. Pace stayed genuinely unresolved: the more perfectly he met her, the more it looked like knowing her file, and the book never let me decide, right through Sheri telling him plainly that not saying the word matters more to him than having her. And Cassie was the clean floor under all of it — the one warmth with no angle, and I trusted the writing partly because it kept her honest.

So: it worked. It made me root for Vee's joy while being a little sick about where it pointed, exactly as the jacket threatened. The slack spots are structural — a few too many retellings, one over-used lecture-device, a break stretched a beat long across too many short chapters. But the hot spots are hot in the specific way I read for: interior, consent-charged, shame-into-heat, and never quite separable from the dread. That last quality is the whole book, and it held.
