**Question asked (verbatim):** Does Pace feel to you like he's calculating or managing Vee, rather than simply loving her? 0-5, with what earned it.

---

3.

Not because I ever caught him being cold — the opposite. The trouble is that his calculating and his loving are the same gesture, and the book never once let me pull them apart, so I have to score the doubleness itself.

What keeps it from being lower (purer love): the machinery is real and I watched it. He built the bench in chapter one. He ran the pointing game — literally pointed at Vee across a lawn, said "Her," sent Randi to collide with her "on purpose." He pre-warms the house, sets out the water bottle in advance, stocks scrunchies by the dozen, arranges the hunger before the food ("the hike had made her hungry first. That was what it was for"). And in "Gone" I heard him say it out loud over Randi's chest — "I want her to get there on her own" — his courtliness named as method, the seduction's pacing discussed like a project. That "you always get bored" line gave him a pattern. So no, I can't call it simple love. The managing is on the page in his own words.

What keeps it from being higher (pure operator): scene by scene the tenderness is not faked, and the book kept showing me that from the outside. In "Rock" he hands over his own childhood wound. In "hills-and-valleys" and "The Usual" he's caught out helplessly in love by Sheri — "the air crackles," "I want Vee, I miss her" — undefended, blind to his own face, no plan anywhere in his conscious head. Sheri, who owes the game nothing, treats him as an ordinary man being an idiot, not a schemer. And "We Find Out": "he hadn't planned any of what came next, she could tell, and that was the thing that undid her." I believed the improvised care.

So 3 is honest to the vertigo the book kept me in. The single sharpest evidence for the "managing" reading is the one he'd never admit to himself: he'll give Vee his whole body and every true thing except the word — and even undone, spent, wall-down in "Under the Rug," he still withholds "I love you." A man that precise with language, keeping the one word behind a rule, reads to me as someone protecting an architecture. That's what tips me to the middle rather than below it. But the warmth was too consistent, and too often shown from vantages he couldn't be performing for, to call him a calculator who doesn't love her. He's both, all the way down, and the book meant me not to be able to choose.
