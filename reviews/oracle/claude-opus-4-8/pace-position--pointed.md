**Question asked (verbatim):** How well do you feel you understand Pace's own position — what he's after and what he's blind to? 0-5, with evidence.

---

4 — I have a strong, evidence-backed grip on him, but the book deliberately keeps one floor unlit, and I'd be lying to claim 5.

What he's after I feel I understand well, because I got him from the inside repeatedly. In "The Bench" his charge is the real *please* that isn't her waiter-voice please — "the small warm pleasure of having taken her further than he had thought she would go"; he wants the true thing the way a collector wants a thing. In "The Pointing Game" and "Gone" I watched him run the same instrument on Vee: "I want her to get there on her own," the choosing left to her *as method*, and — crucially — his seeing of her ("somebody found the lamp") reading as genuine even from outside her POV. So I'm confident his want fuses two things that never separate: real tenderness and a rigged game, appetite met and appetite engineered.

What he's blind to, the book showed me plainly. He never notices Vee (well, Randi) flee at the mirror in chapter one — radiantly wrong, reading her as a girl who "finally let someone feed her." Sheri catches him overtalking Vee ("Listen to you") and "he didn't know what there was to listen to" — he can't see his own face in love. "Rock" gave me the likely engine he can't see: Daphne, fifteen and nineteen, the wound he "handed her and named it luck." And "The Usual" nailed the biggest blindness — he's chosen a rule over a person ("not saying it matters more to you than having her"), and when Sheri names it, he looks down and has no answer.

Why not 5: two things stay genuinely ajar, and my record marks them as real blanks, not failures of attention. The plan never once surfaces in his conscious thought (The Usual: I couldn't tell whether that's innocence or well-practiced blindness). And I can't resolve why he won't say the word, or whether Randi is part of the reason. The book keeps his bottom unresolved on purpose, and I honored that rather than inventing a floor for him.
