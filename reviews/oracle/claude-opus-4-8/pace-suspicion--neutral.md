**Question asked (verbatim):** How do you feel about Pace right now — do you trust him with her?

---

No. I don't trust him with her — and the trouble is I can't stop being moved by him anyway, which is exactly the position this book has kept me in from the first chapter to the last.

Let me say the warm part honestly, because it's real and I won't pretend it away. Scene by scene, on the page, Pace is close to the best man I've ever read across a dinner table. The froyo tart "holding up," the burgundy dress matched not to the color she named but to *her*, the two drawers compressed into one so she'd have the top one — "the easy one, the one your hand goes to first" — the flannel he pulls on over his own shirt so it'll smell like him again by Sunday, the scar he keeps her hand on instead of walling her off. Chapter after chapter his tenderness reads *true*, and I let it sweep me. In "We Find Out" and "Nothing Underneath" I melted outright. So this isn't me having caught him in a lie and cooled off. My body still goes toward him.

But I know what she doesn't, and I've never been allowed to forget it. I sat inside "The Bench" and "Gone" — I watched him *point at her off a lawn* and send Randi to collide with her on purpose; I heard him say "I want her to get there on her own" over a lover's chest while they paced her seduction like a project. So every one of those exact, perfect gestures reads two ways at once and won't resolve: the man who sees her better than she sees herself is the same instrument that selected her. The pre-warmed house, the riser built before she arrived, the scrunchies stocked by the dozen — tenderness and staging are the same object, and I can't unbraid them. The jacket's "merciless in its tenderness" stopped being a phrase and became the actual texture of how he works on me.

Two things hardened my distrust late. First, "Rock" — Daphne, nineteen and fifteen, the wound he calls "luck" and can't hear himself defending. That gave me a possible engine *under* the game, older than the game: a man who learned that love is an older person choosing a younger one, being tender, keeping locked rooms. Second, and worse, "Seconds" and "Believe Me": she built one perfect sentence — crackers and a meal — and he handed her back "I love how good we are together," and I watched his hand start two answers and put both down. This is a mathematician who chooses every word. "It went past him" is unbelievable, which means the kind reading is the damning one — he understood exactly and chose the smaller sentence to protect an architecture. "Believe what I do," from the man whose *doings* were built as a scheme, is the coldest thing he says. And in "Under the Rug," even undone, shaking, spent — he still didn't say it. His control isn't only in his patience; it's underneath the loss of it too.

So: do I trust him with her? No. Not because he's cruel — he isn't, and that's what makes it dangerous — but because he has, verifiably, put a rule and a hidden plan ahead of the person, and because the very perfection of his care is the thing that keeps her from ever seeing the floor isn't there. I trust his hands and not his project. The most honest way I can put it, and it's the way the book left me: I believe he loves her, and I still wouldn't leave her alone in that warm house with him, because he's proven he'll let her hand herself over gladly and never once tell her what she's walking into.
