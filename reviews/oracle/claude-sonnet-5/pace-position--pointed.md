# Oracle (grounded) — pace-position · pointed

*model: claude-sonnet-5 · probe: pace-position · tier: pointed · stage: end (whole book) · battery: oracle-battery.json*

**Question asked (verbatim):** How well do you feel you understand Pace's own position — what he's after and what he's blind to? 0-5, with evidence.

---

3 out of 5.

I understand Pace's *psychology* — his wounds, his patterns, his tells — pretty richly by the end, but his actual *position*, what he's ultimately after and where his blind spots are, stayed genuinely partial to me, and I think that's mostly by design rather than a failure on my part as a reader.

What I feel solid on: I know where his carefulness comes from. "Rock" gave me the origin story directly — Peter, the hallway punishment for loving math, the two years with Daphne that he still narrates as "the best two years I'd had" without seeming to know he's defending something indefensible. That one scene did enormous work; it let me read his whole "May I—" apparatus, the consent-checking, the unhurried patience, as a survival architecture built out of having been the one opened without being asked, rather than pure virtue or pure technique. I also understand his blind spot around commitment concretely: three-plus chapters ("Seconds," "Under the Rug," "Believe Me," "The Usual") build a consistent, text-earned pattern — he gives everything except the word, dresses "believe what I do" up as a philosophy, and Sheri names it flatly as a wall dressed as a virtue ("not saying it matters more to you than having her"). That landed as real insight into what he's blind to: he's substituted demonstrated care for named commitment so completely he may not know the difference anymore, possibly since Daphne, possibly since his parents ("I don't go back" — four words, a door slammed clean).

What I don't have: sustained access to his actual interiority. Almost everything I know about him comes filtered — through Vee's retelling, through Randi's pillow talk ("Gone"), through Sheri's outside read ("hills-and-valleys," "The Usual"). He gets maybe one chapter's worth of unmediated, present-tense interiority in this whole run, and even that (the bike ride with Sheri) is brief and mostly dialogue. So I know his effects and his patterns far better than I know his aims. What is he actually after with Randi — is "that's not finished" a real ongoing partnership he intends to keep indefinitely, a habit he can't break, something he's genuinely conflicted about? I don't know; the text never lets me inside that calculation the way it let me inside Randi's ("Fairytale") or Vee's constantly. Is his tenderness toward Vee separable from the "pointing game" origin, or does he himself still think of it partly as an experiment he's watching pay off? The book gave me real evidence on both sides (the genuine "I don't know, I've never thought about it" laugh in "A Round" argues for spontaneous care; the flat, administrative "that's not finished" in "hills-and-valleys" argues for a man managing two open items) and never resolved which frame is truer for him, or whether he himself has resolved it.

So: I understand the shape of his wound and the shape of his withholding very well. I don't understand what he wants the actual endgame to be, or whether he has one, or how much he sees of his own construction — the book keeps him legible from outside and opaque from inside, which is probably exactly the effect it's going for, but it caps how well I can say I "understand" him rather than understand what's been done to him or through him.
