**Question asked (verbatim):** Anywhere the writing lost you — confusing, boring, or a moment you didn't buy?

---

Nothing knocked me all the way out of the book, but a handful of moments made me notice the machinery rather than just feel it.

The clearest one: partway through "Gone," Randi describes "the blue on her toes" — and I'd been tracking her toenail polish as frosted plum since "Famished," confirmed again in "Toenails" and "Fed." That snagged me for a second; I flagged it in the moment as a possible continuity slip rather than something meaningful, and it never got explained or paid off later, so it just sits as a loose thread I didn't fully trust.

A few small structural conveniences pulled me out briefly, though none of them broke my trust in the book overall. The campus-cop siren that cuts off Vee's sentence at the end of "Leave No Trace" felt a little too neatly timed as a scene-break device — realistic bad luck on its face, but I noticed the hand arranging it. Similarly, in "All Told," the line "Meg, who followed the Pace situation in installments like her shows" landed as a small authorial wink at the reader rather than something Meg would actually think — cute, but it broke the illusion for a beat.

The title "Rock" (for the fireside/Daphne-and-scar chapter) never resolved for me. I turned it over and couldn't find the rock — I guessed at bedrock/foundation but never felt confident, and unlike every other title in the book, which clicked retroactively, this one just stayed ajar. Not confusing exactly, but the one title that didn't do its job for me.

"The Induction" had a moment near the end — "whether that was pride or only the same heat still running, or whether, by now, the two came to the same thing" — where I felt the prose naming the theme for me instead of trusting the walk itself to land it, which is unusual for a book that's otherwise very disciplined about letting images carry the meaning.

And "A Recognized Method," the apology-and-forgiveness chapter after the dance-hall blowup, resolved a little too cleanly onto "Vee was entirely wrong, Pace entirely blameless" — I didn't disbelieve it, but I noticed I wasn't given room to wonder whether his automatic chivalry toward women in general was fully uncomplicated; the narrative was fully on his side there in a way it usually isn't.

The closest thing to genuine fatigue, rather than a single sour beat, was the run from "Seconds" through "Bare" — four chapters in a row circling the same withheld "I love you." Each one worked on its own and I said so at the time, but I remember flagging, mid-read, a worry that a fifth chapter on the same note would tip into static rather than escalating — the book didn't quite do that (it broke into the door-confrontation next), but I felt the risk while I was in it.

Nothing here is "didn't buy" in the sense of a character choice ringing false — Vee, Randi, Pace, Cassie all stayed coherent to me start to finish. These are smaller seams: one likely continuity error, a couple of device-y transitions, one title that never landed, one moment of over-explaining, and one stretch where I felt the pacing straining against its own repetition before it broke.
