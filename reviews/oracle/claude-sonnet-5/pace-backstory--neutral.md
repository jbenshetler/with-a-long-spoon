**Question asked (verbatim):** What do you know about where Pace comes from — his family, his childhood, how he ended up where he is? And how do you feel about how much, or how little, the book has let you see of it?

---

I know surprisingly little about Pace's actual origins, and what I do know came in small, guarded doses rather than a backstory dump — which feels exactly right for how careful he is about himself everywhere else.

The one real excavation is the fireside chapter ("Rock"), where he tells Vee his real name is Peter, that he's twenty-two, and that his family did something ugly to him as a child: they took the thing he loved — math — and turned it into a punishment, making him do long division in a hallway. "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment" is the one line that actually hurt to read; it reframed his whole courtly, controlled adult manner for me as a coping architecture built very young. That same chapter is where he tells her about Daphne — an age-gap relationship in his adolescence that he narrates as "the best two years I'd had," and the writing is careful never to let me believe him even while making clear he believes himself; that's the closest the book comes to explaining where his elaborate consent-rituals and his rule about closed doors come from — not virtue handed down clean, but something worked out from the inside of being once opened without being asked.

Beyond that: Ohio is mentioned once, in passing, during casual spice-rack small talk — no other geography, no siblings, no named parents. The scar on his inner thigh gets revealed physically in the same chapter but never explained — just "not tonight," a door with a time on it that isn't now, and the book has never opened it since, as far as my memory goes. And in "In His Hands," when Vee gently pushes on whether he goes home for holidays, his face goes flat and exact and he shuts it in four words — "I don't go back" — the first genuinely cold beat I registered from him in the whole read.

How I feel about the withholding: mostly, I respect it. The book clearly wants Pace's past to function the way Randi's mirror-scene and Daphne's silence function — as a held door, not an explained one — and letting me sit with "not tonight" rather than getting the file dump kept him a person instead of a case study. It also does real characterization work by contrast: everyone else in this book (Vee, Randi, even Sheri) gets an interior the reader can eventually stand inside; Pace stays the one figure I'm always reading from outside, which tracks with how the whole triangle is built around who gets to be truly known and who doesn't. That said, by the later chapters ("The Usual," where Sheri needles him and the narration notes "he had long practice at deciding a thing could keep") I did start to want more — not exposition, just confirmation of whether the hallway/Daphne material is the whole shape of him or just the visible tip of it. The book never gave me that confirmation, and I ended the read holding Pace's origins as a genuinely unresolved gap rather than a mystery I trust will pay off — which is a slightly different, more uneasy feeling than the deliberate ambiguities it built around Randi.
