## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

I opened this chapter expecting Vee. The jacket trained me to expect her — the college junior walking toward Pace while Randi steers. So for a long stretch I read the woman on the page as *her*: the sorority dinner, the monogrammed overnight bag, the blue eyes, the sorority girl's question about the lights. And then, at the very edge of the scene, he says it: "There you are, Randi." That name-landing reset the whole triangle for me. The woman being broken open on that bench is the steersman, not the steered. The first thing this book does is show me the couple at the center of the plan — and show me that the plan's architect is herself undone by being seen.

I'm drawn to Pace and chilled by him at once. His noticing is the engine of his charm: the honeycrisp sliced thin, cut sides up, because he'd noticed she ate them faster that way; the water bottle waiting on the nightstand; the cashmere blanket she'd brought after the second week. The erotic patience of him — each "Not yet," the three passes that turn back before they arrive, the long refusing — worked on me. It reads as love. But his certainty is total: he is never once unsure of what she needs, and his narration after is the pride of a man assessing a production — "how much further she had gone than he had thought she would," "he would answer it again, as often as she wanted to bring it to him." Kindness and blindness arrive as one thing in him, and the chapter lets both stand.

But Randi is where the chapter hurts. The bathroom passage is the real climax, and it turned the whole evening over for me. The "face" she has been looking at since thirteen — the face that takes being-looked-at and costs her nothing because "the face was not her" — is the most precise description of dissociation I've ever read in a sex scene. Her pleasure was real; the text insists on it: "she had been more alive on that bench than she could remember being anywhere, ever." That's what makes the cost unbearable. The thing that made her alive is the thing that removed her armor. And the tenderness waiting on the other side of the door is the one thing she cannot let herself receive: "Out there tonight there would be no deciding." The smile she rebuilds in the mirror is the saddest thing on any page in this chapter.

The heat genuinely worked on me. The apple game, the slow undressing from one knee, each "Not yet," and then the turn when the *please* changed — "the *please* came from below... 'I'm begging, please, please—'" — the build is as hot as anything I've read, and the "There you are" is intimate and gutting in the same breath. Then the chapter deliberately lets the charge fall cold — the leaking tears, the bathroom lock clicking over "which was not a thing she had ever done before in his house." That click is the chapter's moral sound.

My suspicion of Pace is earned by the page, not manufactured. He decodes her interiority with total confidence, and then the chapter hands us her interior to contradict him — he thinks she met herself; she is deciding to disappear behind the face "not ever." The paragraph about Daphne, a past door he did not respect, sits directly before the lock clicks, and the book is telling me this is not a one-time thing in him. The one small friction: the bench's mechanism description ("well-oiled, silent") reads a little like a maker's manual, though it is Pace's voice. And "the small economy of two people who did not perform for each other" is the author tipping that the chapter already knows more than Pace does.

The title "The Bench" did not spoil me; it sat there like the object itself — hidden under a tabletop, revealed, then covered again — and by the last line it's the book's engine: "full of what it was, doing what it will continue to do, waiting for the next time." The volume title, **A Polite Invitation**, now chills me: every gesture of Pace's is an invitation that is also a design, and the blurb's closing beat — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — fits this chapter exactly. Randi's yeses were free, and her freedom is precisely what she cannot take back. The series title, **With a Long Spoon**, calls up the old proverb about supping with the devil: it warns that this intimacy is dangerous, and that distance is the thing these people are losing. I want Vee to walk in. My guess from here: Vee is the next body on that bench, and Randi will help put her there, smiling the whole time. I dread it, and I want to keep reading.
