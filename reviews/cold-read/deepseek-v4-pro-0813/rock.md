## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

This is the quiet giant. A whole chapter on one couch, tea going cold, a fire at their backs — and it turns out to be the heaviest thing the book has handed me yet. The Daphne revelation lands exactly where Vee says it lands: "low and heavy in her stomach before it was a thought." Nineteen and fifteen. The page never speaks the word — Vee holds it down because it "was not hers to say," and the telling itself goes the same way. What stays with me is not the arithmetic but that Pace "handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck." He runs through the reasons in good order, in the same underselling voice he's used all evening, "held the careful way a person carries a full glass so it won't go over the rim." I sat in that sentence for a long time.

That's the chapter's turn, and it moves Pace for me more than anything since ch1. I came into this book trusting the consent practice and carrying the checkpoint memory that he once crossed Daphne's door and it cost him. Now the light reverses: a nineteen-year-old assigned to "help the child find his feet," a fifteen-year-old boy alone in a dormitory full of men, two years with her as "the soft middle," and then "she went on. Which was only sensible." He defends it against a word neither of us on the page will say. The man who never pushes, who leaves the last inch, now reads to me not as a craftsman of consent but as someone built out of having been the boy told his violation was fortune. That isn't suspicion of his kindness — it's the page showing me the shape of his blind spot and asking me to sit in the cold with it.

The swoon is real and I don't want to shortchange it. "It's the lift. It's the same face" — she gets there in her body before her head, and it's the truest thing anyone has said about him. And the ending undid me: "for no reason she had done anything to deserve," and Vee counting her fortune. The page has handed me the math she's missing — the staged collision, the coordinated recruitment — so her luck reads as an arranged meal she thinks fell from the sky. That ache is what the book has been building toward, and it peaks here, right when she's safest.

The charge: it runs under everything, but the engine is intimacy, not arousal. It peaks early — the robe fallen open, his palm on her breast "as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm" — and again when her fingers find the scar and he says "Not tonight." That deferral is the most charged moment in the chapter: a promise of more person, not more body, and he keeps her hand there. The middle goes deliberately cool; the family stories are lovely but not hot. The furnace line at the end — "like a furnace banked for the night" — is the book's own voice settling the temperature.

Friction, honestly: the Ohio-food stretch runs a beat long; the spice rack is charming, but it's the second spell of Vee-being-charming before the turn arrives, and I felt the chapter marking time. A small thumb on the scale: the résumé — fourteen, doctorate, patent, powerlifter, furniture, the dress — is starting to feel hero-shaped, and Vee's "least informative modesty" reads like the book winking at exactly that. And the withheld word: I honor it as fidelity to "not hers to say," but it also means the boy's version stands on the page unchallenged except by my own arithmetic. I can't tell yet whether that's the book's honesty or its blind spot.

The titles. "Rock" — flat, heavy, oblique; it promised nothing before reading, which is the right kind of chapter title. Afterward it gathers: the black ridge, the steadiness, the stone under Vee's tongue, the door with a time on it. The volume line I was given, with no tagline: **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION**. "A Polite Invitation" darkens at this chapter — the invitation turns out to be to a long meal at a table where you'd better keep your spoon. Daphne is the first time I've seen what the invitation actually serves: hunger answered at the wrong table, reframed as luck. The long spoon is the distance the polite frame depends on, and here the book finally lets me see the dish.

I want the scar's story now, and I dread it in equal measure. I expect the collision — the arranged friendship — to catch up with Vee's "so fortunate," and Randi's hurt to press harder against this new depth Pace just gave Vee and never gives her. Guess from here: the Daphne fact will matter most when Vee learns she was chosen — the book seems to be asking whether being picked by Pace is a gift or another hallway, and this chapter is the first time I really believe the question is being asked of him, too.
