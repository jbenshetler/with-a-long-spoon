There's no plausible-deniability left in her. She isn't being a good sport about his little game anymore; she has come to his couch wanting to hear, in the voice she keeps for his bed, about another woman. That gladness is the engine of the scene, and it reads as genuine tenderness on his part rather than exploitation. He doesn't hand Vee over whole. When he thinks of the embarrassing thing Vee said on the walk — the true thing — he *does not hand it over. It was hers.* That stopped me, and it's the one moment in the chapter where the triangulation has a boundary. He's feeding Randi's appetite, but he won't give her Vee's insides without permission. I trust him a little more for it, even while the whole arrangement pulses with risk.

The swoon is in the wine. The title told me nothing until the glass entered, and then the whole scene tightened around it: the red poured and left to open, the kiss with it still in her mouth, *the tannin catching at the inside of his lips*, her mouth already darker. That's a kiss I felt. And then the detail that electrifies the ending — he had not poured himself a glass, and he tastes nothing else. Everything he gets of this he gets through her, filtered. He is not drinking; he is being drunk *of*. That's the pull of the whole chapter, really: desire moving through a third body, no one touching the person they actually want. The scene doesn't go slack anywhere. It cuts off exactly at the peak, with the glass empty and her hand out and the taste still in his mouth. It's a small, hot, suspended chapter, and it works on me completely.

The unease is small and real and earned. When Pace says *She's easy to listen to*, Randi goes quiet — "the wanting, held. He reads it as desire growing too big for its container. I read it too as a woman feeling the scale of what she wants and also, maybe, a flicker of something else — that Vee was listened to, that Pace was the one listening. It's not jealousy exactly. It's the cost of being seen wanting this. He is tender about it, but he's also standing in it and enjoying standing in it. I don't think he's sinister. I think he's in over his head in a thing he thinks he's managing.

The book's cover line — *WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION* — now rings sharper. The politeness is everywhere: *may I call you*, the held door, the poured wine. But the long spoon, the proverb about supping with the devil, suggests that all this civility is the distance you keep from something that would burn you if you got close without it. The invitation feels polite on its face and, underneath, like the setup for a meal with something dangerous at the table. I'm pulled forward, and I'm starting to dread the moment Vee learns that the warm new friend and the man she may call are not two separate doors but one room.
