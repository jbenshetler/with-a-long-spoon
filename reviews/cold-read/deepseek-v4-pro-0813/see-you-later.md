# Cold read (grounded) — See You Later

## Reader reaction

I have to say it straight: this chapter got me in the chest and then turned cold on me, in that order. On its own it’s the sweetest thing in the book so far — a crush chapter, a two-hour-later best-day-of-her-life chapter — and I was inside Vee enough to feel the lift of it. But I came into it knowing exactly what Randi was doing, because I just watched her do it. So I read the whole hour with a knot under the sweetness. The swoon was real and it belonged to Vee, and the dread was real and it belonged to me.

**Vee.** I love her already, more than I was expecting to. She’s so *excavated* here. The way she reads every room she’s unsure of, “all at once and in detail, taking its measure to find her own place in it” — that’s not a diagnosis, that’s a survival habit, and the chapter lets me feel it before it explains it. The Chi Latte window inventory is the best part of the chapter for this. She clocks the matched sorority girls, then the two “floozies” — and she’s caught, immediately, between her mother’s voice and a longer, unexamined beat of *looking*. She knows exactly what her body would do in those clothes, knows she has “more than they had,” and also knows why she’s the one covered to the collarbone. That whole passage hit me as true and specific and a little sad. She wants to be seen. She’s afraid of what seeing would mean. She has no place to put the want except *being chosen*, and Randi arrives to choose her. It worked on me completely, even knowing it was a setup.

What moved me most is the part where the transcript goes blank. She hangs on every word and later cannot recover one of them. “She had not been keeping the words. She had been keeping the feeling.” That line landed on me as the whole tragedy of the chapter’s sweetness, because it’s so legible: the experience was not about anything Randi actually said; it was about being admitted into a brighter room. Vee has been outside the yellow door. Now she’s inside, and the lateness of being let in is worth more than the content. She doesn’t know she’s being collected. I wanted to reach into the page and say *the words being gone is the tell, not the gift.*

**Randi.** This is where the chapter left me stomach-sick. I already had sympathy for Randi, after the bathroom in The Bench — that quiet locked door, the face she put back on in the mirror, the hum “the size of a key turning in a lock.” I understood her as someone who is also trapped behind the shine. But watching her aim all that machinery at Vee is different. It’s not a defense here. It’s a tool. The warmth swings “total, undimmed” from Vee to Cassie and back, and it’s so total that it becomes impersonal. “Love you, girl” — I physically winced at that, because I’ve now watched Randi perform warmth twice for audience, and this was the biggest delivery yet, aimed at a girl who would take it all the way in. Vee says the flattering thing landed “like a key in a lock.” It did. That’s the problem.

The worst beat, for me, was *“There you are.”* It’s the same line Pace says to Randi at the end of the bench scene — the line that found her from below the face. Now Randi says it to Vee at the end of the row. I don’t think that’s an accident on the page, and as a reader it made me cold: Randi has learned the exact shape of what was done to her, and she’s now wearing it back onto someone else. She found Vee. She named her as found. And Vee reads it as arrival. That is the moment the chapter became genuinely upsetting to me, under all the yellow light.

**Cassie.** I liked her immediately and I can’t explain why the page achieved that with so little. She doesn’t take the bit’s open door — she just says “Cassie” flat and picks her pencil back up. Vee smooths it over, calls her a tea-leaves person, and Cassie doesn’t mind and doesn’t warm. She’s the only person in the room not performing for Randi, and she declines the coffee with no sulk. But then: “Goodbye, Vee.” That word sat wrong for me exactly as it sat wrong for Vee — “the whole word, flat and finished, the word for a longer parting than a Wednesday.” I don’t know what it means yet. I don’t want to guess too hard. But I trust Cassie’s truth more than anyone else’s in the scene. She sees something, or feels something, and she’s not fixing it. That makes her dangerous to Randi and, I suspect, the only real friend Vee has in the room.

**Trust vs. suspicion.** I have no suspicion of Vee’s innocence — it’s too convincingly built to be an act. I have no suspicion of Cassie; if anything, she’s the one I’d trust with my keys. My suspicion is entirely of Randi, and frankly the page doesn’t have to convince me, because the Pointing Game already did. What this chapter did is show me how *good* she is at it. That’s the thing that moved my feeling: I’d been holding onto some pity for Randi, and the chapter complicated that by making her the active hand. I can still feel for the girl who locked herself in the bathroom. I just can’t keep that feeling warm while she says *we should sit together* to a girl she picked out for Pace.

**Erotic charge.** It’s a simmer, not a burn, and that feels right for where the book is. The charge lives in Vee’s unexamined wanting: the way the two “floozies” hold her eyes, the thought that her body would do more in their clothes, the “flat helpless want” she has for Randi that she never names as wanting. The high table is the erotic peak — suddenly displayed at the window, feet off the floor, knees near Randi’s, “a girl at the high table at Chi Latte.” That moment was charged precisely because it was about being seen, not touched. It didn’t go slack anywhere, but it also never became a sex scene, and I didn’t want it to. I felt Vee’s desire as ambient heat, and I felt the more I know about what Randi’s doing as a cold draft over it.

**Friction as a reader.** Only a little. There were a couple of phrases that felt like the author pressing a thumb on the scale — “the better thing was right here at her elbow,” and then later “because the better thing was moving and Vee went with it.” I get what it’s doing; it just told me twice what the scene already knew. Also “like a key in a lock” is one of those similes that was already doing work in the sentence before it arrived. And the professor’s lecture about latent variables — “the factors you can’t measure directly, that you have to infer from the ones you can” — underlines itself a little hard. But I didn’t feel talked down to. The chapter’s real work is in the rooming and the blank transcript and the two goodbyes, and those all landed without forcing.

**The titles.** “See You Later” now casts the whole chapter in two lights at once. On Vee’s side, it’s the better farewell — not *let’s do this again,* but “flat fact, a thing already scheduled in some calendar Vee couldn’t see.” That’s the warmth. But the chapter gives us the counter-word: Cassie says “Goodbye,” not “see you later.” The title chooses Randi’s promise over Cassie’s parting, and that made it feel ominous to me. A “see you later” from Randi is a hook, not a goodbye. I suspect the book will make Vee understand that later.

The volume title — **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION** (no tagline supplied) — sits even more strangely now. “A polite invitation” is exactly what this is: coffee, high table, *love you girl.* And I know what the invitation actually is. The long spoon phrase gives me the feeling of something you’d need if you were going to sit at a table with something you shouldn’t be close to. That’s where the book seems to be taking me: a polite surface with a dangerous meal under it, and Vee being handed the spoon without knowing what it’s for.

**What I want / expect / dread next.** I want Vee to get one real sentence from Cassie before it’s too late. I expect Randi’s “later” to come true very soon, and I expect Pace to enter Vee’s life through Randi’s hands — which I dread, because Vee will believe she’s being courted by something good. I’m guessing, from here: the next move is Pace meeting the red-haired girl, probably arranged as the next “walk,” and Vee won’t know what she walked into. I also had a smaller, sadder guess I’ll mark as such: Cassie’s odd goodbye may be her stepping back before the hurt, not after. I want to be wrong.
