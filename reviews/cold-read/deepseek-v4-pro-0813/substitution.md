## Reader reaction

This one landed on me as a slow, warm trap closing — and the worst part was that I kept wanting it to be real, too, because the page kept making it *feel* real. I was inside Vee's skin for the whole scene, and I ached for her: she's smart, she's hungry to be seen, and she has no schema for what she's sitting inside. Her econ answer — "the gap between what they say they're doing and what they're doing" — is her describing the man who asked it, to his face, without knowing. That line went into me.

Pace is the problem the book has set for me, because he *is* good at this. The teaching is genuine. He returns the pencil at the line where she can solve it herself; he asks *what's drawing you to it* instead of *how do you like it*; he doesn't check his phone. I felt the pull Vee feels, and that made it worse than if he'd had a line. A line you can see. This is just him, and him is a man hunting with his girlfriend at the next chair. The "May I call you?" is courtly and pitched exactly right, and my stomach dropped at how well it was pitched.

Randi is where the scene went cold for me. One word, "Randi," with no adjective bit — she's the only one at that table who isn't meeting a stranger, and her performance of not knowing him is the scariest thing on the page. The n-minus-one question is cover; the afterward gentle shove — "You should go out with him" — is her warm hand closing the snare. The detail I kept coming back to: her cup coming up to her mouth twice and going back down, no sip. That's the same self-soothing tell as the bottle-cap edge under her thumbnail, and it reads here like strain: watching her boyfriend find Vee easier to see than he found her. But the page won't say, and I'm honestly grateful — it sits there, un-decoded.

The heat is a scalding 2, because every flush of Vee's has a countercurrent. It peaks at "She hadn't known she was allowed" — the borrowed pencil, the borrowed permission. The forearms, the cuffs, the shirt that gives nothing and hangs right: Vee's eye is working hard, and so was mine. The charge isn't sex; it's significance, the sense that the room has begun to rotate around a point Vee doesn't know is there.

Suspicion is everywhere for me, but none of it is on Vee; it's all above her head. The only person on the page who sees anything is Cassie, with the best line in the chapter: he looked at Randi "the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." That's the lie visible — the man who forgets everyone in the room somehow manages to be completely incurious about the most polished girl at the table. Cassie doesn't know why, and neither does Vee. I do.

The title "Substitution" clicks now: the chance meeting is a substitute, the tutor is a substitute, the whole math problem is a substitute for what's actually being solved. Pace's Euler speech hands me the method in plain words: "On their own these two are a struggle... Put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain." That's the ménage, and it's also what they're doing to Vee — she's the integral that "just won't go."

Cover board: *WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION.* The long-spoon proverb says supper with the devil, keeping your distance. Vee has no spoon. And "A Polite Invitation" is literally this chapter: a polite man offering polite help, politely requesting to call. The titles promise courteous predation, and so far the book is keeping that promise with terrible precision.

What I want next: the first hairline crack where Vee senses something off. What I dread: the call, the date, and the bench. Randi already asked, "Do you think she'll like the bench?" My guess from here is that Vee says yes to whatever comes next because being chosen by these two is the thing she's been waiting her whole life to feel.

A small friction: Vee's postmortem of his tutoring ethics gets a touch heady — "a tutor's path and not a mathematician's" — as if she's narrating the scene's moral. It pulled me out for a beat, but the pull back in came fast.
