## Reader reaction

I knew too much, walking into this chapter. That's the whole experience of it: I knew what Vee doesn't, and the chapter is built to make sure I never forget it. Pace is not a stranger. Randi is not a bored friend. The "random" man who crossed the coffee shop to help with an integral was invited, aimed, and sent — and the girl smiling at Vee's side is the one who picked her out on the quad. So every warm thing in this chapter lands twice for me: once the way it lands for Vee — charmed, held, chosen — and once the way it actually is, which is a hunt in a polite shirt. The title is a math joke and a confessional at once: in u-substitution you change the variable to make a stuck thing solvable. Vee thinks she's learning to solve the problem. She has no idea she's the variable being substituted in.

What disturbed me most is how good Pace is at this. From his own POV chapters I had almost talked myself into him — patient, attentive, a man who wanted to "find" Randi. From Vee's outside I see the instrument that tenderness is. He came there for her. He read her paper like a problem he was meeting for the first time. He asked what her professor had said, not what he would have said. He gave the pencil back at the exact line where she could finish it herself. And he said *"May I"* — a word from another vocabulary, and Vee melts, because she doesn't know it's a tool. The line that finally turned my stomach was not the charm; it was the deflection. She says the truest, most undefended thing she's said all chapter — *"This is the nicest a person has been to me in a really long time. And it's froyo."* — and he does not meet it. He says, *"Your tart's holding up."* That's the moment I stopped being charmed by Pace, and started being afraid for her.

And Randi. Randi is sitting right there. Vee reads her as a friend watching a friend be charmed, and that reading is so total that Vee even supplies the explanation for the small busy-ness of Randi's hand — *she's bored, this is going on too long for her.* I read the same hand on the same coffee cup and I see a woman holding herself together while her boyfriend executes the plan she smiled through in the pointing game. She gives him her name as a single bare word — *"Randi."* No adjective bit, no parents-had-a-sense-of-humor. You can't do the bit for the man who has seen you face-down and begging. And Vee, all unknowing, borrows Randi's own moves — *"Vivienne Thorne. V.T. Yes, like the university. No, my parents didn't plan it"* — a copy of Randi's bit, done earnestly, while the original sits across from her not doing it at all because she can't. The substitution is that literal: Vee is trying on Randi's performance for Pace, not knowing Randi is watching her wear it.

I love Vee. I loved her from "See You Later," and I love her more after forty minutes inside her head. She's smart and warm and she takes up room without apology, and she's also achingly lonely, lonely in the exact shape that makes her vulnerable to being chosen. *"She felt, absurdly, chosen."* The word appears, and it has appeared before: in "See You Later" she walked away from Randi's hug feeling "that she had been chosen, plainly, for reasons she didn't need to understand." Now she's being chosen again, by the man Randi sent. The irony is thick enough to choke on: she feels chosen, and she is — the way a target is chosen.

The math is doing more work than math. Pace says of Euler's formula: *"On their own these two are a struggle — you push and they won't give, and neither one quite looks like what it is. Put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain — easy to name, easy to know what to do with."* He's talking about exponentials and trig. He's also talking about himself and Randi: separately, after the bench, they're stuck — Randi walled off, Pace unable to reach her. Together, aimed at Vee, they become a single thing with a name and a purpose. The cross he marks in her margin is the substitution. It's also the mark he's leaving on her, and she keeps the page.

"With a Long Spoon" — the proverb says you'd need one to sup with the devil. "A Polite Invitation." This chapter is the invitation, and it's almost unbearable how polite it is. *May I call you?* Nobody calls it a trap when it asks that nicely.
