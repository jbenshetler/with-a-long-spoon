## Reader reaction

Oh, this broke me open. After spending the last few chapters in Vee’s agonizing, lonely head and Randi’s gilded, hollow New Year's Eve, we finally get inside Pace—and he is an absolute wreck. We’ve watched him be so controlled, so unshakeably steady, the man who builds the furniture and warms the house and always has the answer, and here he is getting blackout drunk alone in the dark, puking his guts up, mopping the hall with bleach, and sobbing over a bowl of boiled freezer chicken because a truck drove past without turning into his driveway. My heart completely went out to him, even while I wanted to shake him by the shoulders. We finally get the truth of why he withheld the words in *Cropped* and *Believe Me*: *"He had let it stand because it was safe, and because he was afraid, and because at the moment she had asked him to be brave he had been weak."* He does love Vee. *"He knew he loved her."* Having that confirmed on the page, plain and unhedged, after Vee’s torture in Ohio, is a massive relief and an ache at the same time.

And I love Sheri so much in this chapter. Every time she’s on the page she brings the oxygen of normal human candor into Pace’s overly curated life. The detail of the new waiter giving five-foot-nothing Sheri the single burger and Pace the double, and Sheri wordlessly sliding the double and the shake over to herself, made me smile; learning that she’s queer (dating Paige from the design school) and estranged from her own family ("call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas") suddenly clarifies why she and Pace understand each other so well. And thank god someone finally said to his face what I was screaming during *Believe Me*: *"Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty."* She doesn't let him hide behind his brick-by-brick list of all the nice things he does for Vee. And when he gets pretentious about Vee understanding *"the philosophy of it"* when he cooks, Sheri’s *"Lord almighty. Get over yourself"* made me laugh out loud.

What’s wild, though, is how Pace’s mind holds his love for Vee right alongside his domestic fantasy of the three of them. Even in his whiskey stupor, missing Vee and missing Randi (who hasn't called him in the six weeks since the green panties morning in *Four*), the dream he’s been adding to for weeks isn't just Vee—it's *"The three of them in his kitchen in the middle of a morning, after."* Vee in his sheet, Randi with her feet up on the lacquered chair, laughing together. On one hand, his tenderness toward both women feels completely genuine to him—he smiles in the dark remembering Randi’s *"Say something nice to me"*—and yet there is still this massive blind spot where he hasn't reckoned with the deception underneath it. He wonders *"how she would carry it. Whether the room would ask more of her than she had in her,"* while he himself couldn't even carry saying "I love you" to Vee when she stood at his door crying.

There isn't a physical sex scene here, and the erotic charge is mostly memory and longing, but when Sheri teases him about Vee—*"Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you"*—and Pace blushes and says the air *"crackles"* when Vee is in the room, you feel how badly he wants her body and her spirit both. The real pull of the chapter, though, is emotional: watching the man who feeds everyone sit in a cold kitchen eating unseasoned boiled chicken and broccoli out of plastic bags—*"prison food,"* *"everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted"*—while remembering Vee’s face opening at the first bite of the meals he made for her, and her speech about crackers and a meal. And the memory of Daphne ("a girl with brown hair and glasses who laughed with her hand over her mouth") finally explains his superstition about the words: *"There had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone. Daphne was the person he would have told."* He magical-thinked himself into believing that not saying "I love you" is what keeps loss survivable.

The chapter title, **The Usual**, hits three ways at once: it’s the diner order where Sheri and Pace don't even have to speak; it’s the Elvis song on the jukebox (*"Somebody always plays it"*) pressing on the holiday estrangement they both carry; and by the second half, it’s the grim, joyless baseline Pace retreats to when he’s surviving—the rows of identical freezer bags of plain chicken and broccoli, *"Enough in there to go on like this a long time."* Against **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION**, this chapter feels like the dead-of-winter trough before the term starts back up.

Now that the holidays are ending and everyone is coming back to town, I am desperate for Pace to actually go to Vee—or when they finally collide, for him not to be a coward again. My guess from here is that the Valentine's weekend Brooks and Dunn cover band dance Sheri just commanded him to attend (*"I don't care what the redhead's got to say about it"*) is going to be a major set piece down the road, especially after the Rusty Nail disaster.
