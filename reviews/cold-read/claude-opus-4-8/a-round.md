## Reader reaction

This is the appointment I'd been dreading and wanting, and it delivered on both — I was aroused and uneasy at exactly the same time, which is I think the point but let me be honest about where each landed.

The erotic charge worked on me hard, and it peaked in the *rhythm* — "closeness, two touches, withdrawal, the pencil; closeness, two touches, withdrawal." That structure is genuinely maddening in the good way. He touches, then leaves, and "the withdrawal left the place his hands had been ringing." That's the whole book's engine (he takes only looking, he stops) rendered as a physical tempo, and it got under my skin. The high point for me wasn't the nudity — it was her stumping him: *"Does this measurement change? Like. When they're not — like this."* and his honest *"I don't know. I've never thought about it."* The heat there is that her shame becomes a *problem worth solving* to him, and she feels it: "Watched the part of her she'd been apologizing for become, in his hands, something worth getting right." That's a real erotic idea, not just friction and skin.

Where it goes slightly slack is the sheer *length* of the inventory. Neck-to-shoulder, shoulder-to-wrist, waist, under-bust, across-bust, outside seam, inside seam — I felt the catalogue as a catalogue about two-thirds through, the prose working a little hard to find a new indignity per measurement. But it mostly earns it because each stop has a distinct interior beat.

Vee — I'm all the way with her. The wet-satin thread is the cruelest and most effective thing here: the thumbprint on the couch, then "big and dark and stupid," then the horror that "the hope was visible. The hope was sized to be seen across the room." Her body announcing her wanting against her will, dressed in the fabric she chose *with* hope — that's the shame engine at full power and it hurt to read in the right way. And then the turn, the closed-eyes brace where she expects the grin-of-the-joke-of-her and instead finds him craned all the way up, *not having looked down once* — I felt her spine straighten with her. "Her chin came up… she was rising, not on purpose, up onto her toes." That got me. It's the first time in these chapters I've felt Vee gain something rather than only give.

And then she *spends* it. The "Oops. Sorry" pressed-breast beat, him going red and losing the fight against the grin — that flipped the power for a page and I loved it, it's the most alive she's been.

Pace — and here's my problem, the stone in my stomach never left. On the page he is flawless: he warmed the house hours early, he had the water hot, the blanket, the riser already built, he doesn't ogle, he holds the line and *lets her hear it's a line* — "the choosing cost him." Read straight, it's the most romantic scene in the book. But I'm carrying what I'm carrying, and this chapter kept quietly confirming it. *He walked "right past Randi, Randi!, without even looking"* — she reads that as proof he chose *her*; I read it as a man who already has Randi and is running a play. The perfection is exactly what unsettles me. A man who has thought of *everything* — thermostat, water, riser, blanket, the bra "will lie to me" — is a man executing something rehearsed. When she thinks "he'd been right about the color the way he was right about everything he went all the way into," I felt the cold: he is never wrong, and being never-wrong is the tell.

The line that most had the author's thumb near the scale, for me, was: "She felt, distantly, that this was going to matter to her later, in ways she couldn't yet see." That's the narration reaching forward to tap me on the shoulder — I'd already felt the weight of the scene; I didn't need the promissory note. It's mild, but it's the one place the book told me what I'd earned on my own.

What the text *itself* earned as suspicion this time: honestly not much new — Randi isn't even here, and the scene is so warm that suspicion has to come from my memory, not from anything Pace does on the page. Which I think is deliberate and it's working: I can't point to a false note in him, and that's precisely what makes me not trust him. The measuring "changes the line, it'll lie to me" about the bra is chillingly good — a true dressmaking fact that also means *nothing may be allowed to hide from him.*

What I want next: I want to see the *cost* of this land somewhere she can't manage it — and I dread it, because every step up this staircase makes the eventual reveal worse for her. I want Randi back on the page; her absence is now loud. *Guess from here:* the finished dress is for the honor-society night, and I suspect that night — or whatever it's building toward — is where the two hidden objects (the closed door, the two towels) finally come home. I also keep waiting for someone to say "Peter."
