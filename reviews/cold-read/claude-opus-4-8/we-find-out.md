## Reader reaction

This is the chapter the whole book has been walking toward, and it landed on me hard — first as heat, then, about two beats later, as dread I couldn't get rid of.

The opening is the most alive Vee has been. She texts first. She arrives an hour early. She pokes him. And then the moment I actually gasped at: "she had just watched it flinch." A whole book of him as "a wall that picked her up and set her down where it wanted her," and one fist to a thigh cracks it. The chase in the den is *joyful* — genuinely funny, genuinely erotic, the best sustained play in the book. Her discovering she's faster than he is, cutting and reversing on the balls of her feet while "all that mass of him had to gather and come around like a ship" — I was rooting for her. And the flash, the top yanked up for a breath, "some small astonished part of her stood off and watched her do it and could not believe it was her" — that's the induction second step again, self-authored, no handler.

And then: "the running was the mistake, because the hall gave onto the bedroom." That sentence turned the temperature. Because it isn't a mistake. She ran to the one room with no floor to lose him in. I don't think she knows that, and I'm not sure the text thinks she doesn't.

The bet is where my pleasure and my unease split clean down the middle. "Either you admit you lost, or we find out" — it's beautifully constructed and it's a rigged coin. There's no outcome where she wins; the test *produces* the result it measures, and he knows her body better than she does. The hand going up the inside of her knee, slow, "slow enough that she could have stepped out of it the whole way and didn't" — that clause is the book's thesis in eleven words, and it made me cold. Freely given. That was the trap.

The finger held up, coated to the base, was the most erotic image in the chapter for me and the cruelest. Her lifelong secret shame — "the shameful plenty of the wet" — held into lamplight. And then he does the one thing that dismantles her completely: he doesn't flinch. "Like it was true. Like it was fine. Like it was hers, and good." I felt her relief in my chest. I also felt the hook set. He is curing her shame and taking the cure as payment.

The spanking itself I did not expect to be *tender*, and the swerve away from Randi's anecdote was the smartest move here. Vee braces for theatre and counting — Randi's story rising "in her like something she'd swallowed" (that verb!) — and gets instead a quiet room, a warm hand resting, gentling until "the skin woke under his palm and began, on its own, to want the thing it was afraid of." The mouth pressed to the pink of her. That's where the heat peaked for me — the softness, not the strike. And "her hips lifted into it before they sank, offering the thing her mouth was still refusing… she could not have said which one of them she was lying to." Yes. That's good.

The aftercare wrecked me on purpose and I let it. Juice. Literally juice, "sweet and cold and faintly ridiculous." "He hadn't planned any of what came next, I could tell, and that was the thing that undid her." *Wonderful, wonderful,* going quieter until the word and the hand were the same thing. "Treasured" — first time in her life. And I sat there with the ugliest thought in the book so far: he planned everything up to the bet and nothing after it, and it's the *unplanned* part that binds her. I don't know if that makes it clean or worse.

The scrunchies. Oh, that's a good object. "There were more of them than a person could go through quickly, far more, and the plenty of them said the thing no note would have." Provision as claim. A form of them. He bought a *supply* of her future presence. It's tender and it's inventory.

The bathroom monologue is the best comedy in the book — "My Victorian LARPing does me no good at all when I'm sweaty and sticky," said to a mirror, and "I'm going to start bringing a bag," and meaning it. That last line is the whole surrender in nine words, delivered as logistics. Then his antiperspirant: "there it was, his smell on her instead of her own." She leaves wearing him.

Where I resisted: the very end. The curry is lovely sensory work — toasted spices, "potatoes the color of cream," heat arriving late — but after "treasured" and the scrunchies the chapter had already peaked twice, and the food coda felt like a third dessert. It also stops rather than ends. And one small thumb-on-scale: "She was being offered up to her own wanting, and both of them knew it" — I'd already understood that from the folded blanket; the sentence explained a thing the image had done better.

Randi's absence is enormous here. She isn't in the room and she's all over it — she wrote the script Vee braces against. "no Sunday, no Randi to be told." *To be told.* Vee will report this. And Randi already knows how it went, because she helped set it.

**We Find Out** — as a title it's his line, the coin-flip, and I read it as the bet. On second thought it's colder: *we*. The plural. Two people find out what her body is, and only one of them didn't already know. And there's a third *we* offstage that will find out on Sunday. It's also, plainly, the book's method — everything here is discovered through her body agreeing before she does. *A Polite Invitation* has never felt more exact: every step was an offer she could have declined, presented courteously, and she declined none. *With a Long Spoon* keeps sitting there — you sup with the devil with one — and this chapter is where she puts the spoon down and eats with her hands.

What I want next: Sunday. Vee telling Randi, leaving nothing out. I dread it and I want it more than anything.
