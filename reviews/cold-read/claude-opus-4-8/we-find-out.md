# Cold read — We Find Out

*scene: scenes/we-find-out.md · read after: practice-room*

## Reader reaction

Okay. So this is the scene the last two chapters were pointing at, and it landed on me hard — I mean physically. This is the hottest the book has been for me, and the fact that it works *at all* after how queasy Randi made me is its own kind of achievement.

The chase is what does it. That whole opening — her turning up an hour early, telling herself she'll sit in the car; the poking, "Get up. Get up. Get up."; the shake slopping down his shorts; stealing them and waving them "like a flag over a taken field" — it's play, it's genuinely funny and light, and it's the first time in the whole book Vee has been the one on offense and *winning*. And then the thing that undid me: **she flinches him.** "She had never once seen anything she did land on him. He had been a wall since the day she met him... and she had just watched it flinch." That's a real character beat, not just foreplay — the wall has a corner. And then she finds out **she's faster than he is.** "all that mass of him had to gather and come around like a ship." For a few pages she has power in this house for the first time, and she's drunk on it, and she flashes him to *make him come* — "She had never in her life done such a thing." I loved her here. She's brave and stupid and alive.

And then — "the running was the mistake, because the hall gave onto the bedroom and the bedroom was small and had no floor to lose him on." That sentence turned my stomach over in the good-and-bad way at once, because I felt the trap close in the architecture of the *house itself*. Her own speed ran her into the one room with no exit. That's the whole book in a floor plan.

The spanking itself. I have to be honest about how it worked on me: **it worked.** The bet structure ("Either you admit you lost, or we find out" — that's the chapter title, said out loud), the slow hand up the inside of her knee with his eyes on hers "so she had nowhere to put her own," and then the finger. "She braced somewhere low for it to be difficult, for there to be at least the dignity of a threshold to cross. There was none." God. And him holding the whole coated length of it up in the lamplight — "the wet and then the shameful plenty of the wet" — the thing she's spent her whole life hiding, held up where they both have to see how *much* of it there is, and him looking at it "Like it was true. Like it was fine. Like it was hers, and good." That's the deepest the book has gone into her core shame and it's unbearably tender at the same time as it's an exposure she didn't consent to in words.

Here's what's carrying my unease under the heat. **Randi's story is in the room.** The text puts it there explicitly: "Randi's story rose up in her like something she'd swallowed: would she have to count out loud?" She's braced for the theatre Randi described — over the knee, count out loud, the hand that slips. And what she gets is the *opposite*: "What came was nothing like the story. Behind her he was quiet." The rest of me, the reader who knows what Vee doesn't, felt that as devastating. Randi *fed* her the loud version, the show version — and Pace gives her the quiet, no-audience, no-counting version, and the contrast is exactly what makes it feel like his is the *true* one and Randi's was a fiction told to prime her. Vee experiences this as "nothing like the story," proof that this is real and private. I experience it as the two of them, unknown to her, working the same girl from two directions and each making the other's version land harder by contrast. "no Randi waiting to be told" — she thinks of Randi as someone she'll confess *to*. She has no idea Randi already scripted the audience out of the room in advance.

The line that gutted me on the trust axis: "and she could not have said which one of them she was lying to." And "no one in the world to watch... Shame wants a witness, and in the small lit country of his bed there was no longer any such thing as a witness." That's beautiful and it's the trap — the tagline's trap — because *there is* a witness, there's always been a witness, she just doesn't know her name.

The aftercare wrecked me in the tender direction, and this is where the book keeps me sympathetic to Pace against my better judgment. **The juice.** "He hadn't planned any of what came next, she could tell, and that was the thing that undid her. No routine to it, nothing practiced." That's the tell the book wants me to hold: this part *wasn't* staged. Everything up to the bed has apparatus behind it — but him getting up for a glass of juice, making her drink before anything else, "wonderful, wonderful, until the word and his hand in her hair were the same thing" — "for the first time she could remember, treasured." I believe that's real. That's the horror and the ache of this book in one gesture: the man is genuinely tender and the tenderness is real and it is also, structurally, the softest jaw of the trap.

Then the scrunchies. This is the third time now — green panties traded, the heels/wardrobe, and now "a whole form of them, deep green with small white dots... more of them than a person could go through quickly." The **abundance-as-possession** motif, and the text names it flat: "the plenty of them said the thing no note would have, that he meant to be looking at her hair a long while yet." Deep green *again* — his color for her, the traded-panty color. He keeps buying her in quantities that mean *I intend to keep looking at you.*

And the bathroom monologue to the mirror — "Why do I keep pretending we're not going to sleep together" — is her finally, out loud, retiring the "Victorian LARPing." "I'm going to start bringing a bag... and meant it." She's stopped fighting the current. That reads as surrender dressed as practicality, and I felt the small grief of it under her lightness.

The scar. "her palm passed over the old scar high on the inside of his thigh... She didn't ask now either. She just knew it was there, the way you know a thing about a person you've decided to keep." Still deferred. But that phrasing — "a person you've decided to keep" — is her staking her claim on the unexplained thing, and it's poignant precisely because we know how much of him is sealed.

**Erotic charge:** peak of the book for me. It stays hot because it's threaded through her *specific* shame, not generic. It never went clinical. The only place my pleasure curdled — deliberately, I think — is the Randi-story intrusion, which is the point.

**Friction:** almost none. The prose is doing the over-explaining a *little* in the "Shame wants a witness" passage — it's gorgeous but it's the author underlining the thesis in ink I could have inferred. And "the plenty of them said the thing no note would have" spells out the abundance motif I'd already caught. But these are small; the scene earns them.

## The Titles

**"We Find Out"** — this is the plainest, most on-the-page title the book has given me. It's Pace's line, twice: "Either you admit you lost, or we find out." As a title it's almost too transparent — it names the bet that structures the whole seduction of the scene. But the charge is in the *we*. On the surface it's "we'll find out whether you enjoy it." Underneath: *we*, the two of them, find out together the thing about her body she'd hidden even from herself — and there's a colder third reading I can't unhear, that the *we* who "find out" about Vivienne Thorne has always secretly been three people, two of whom are running an experiment. "We find out" is the language of investigators. The reader finds out too — finds out how far she'll go, how ready her body was all along.

**With a Long Spoon** and the tagline — this chapter is the tagline made flesh. Every single yes here was freely given: the bet, the nod, "leave the shoes," "slow, that's part of the bet too" (which "hadn't been part of any bet he'd named" — the rules expand and she's too far down to argue, which is the *softest* version of the trap I've seen yet). And the sup-with-the-devil title keeps deepening: the sweetness is real — the juice, the "wonderful," the treasured sleep — and that's exactly why she has no spoon. You don't guard against the thing that's tender to you.

## What I want / dread next

I want to know if the "nothing like the story" beat ever detonates — if Vee ever learns Randi handed her the false version. I dread the confession scene where she tells Randi about *this*, because now I know Randi will receive her own successful priming as fresh gossip. *Guess from here:* the next stretch may be domestic, settling-in — "I'm going to start bringing a bag" reads like a move toward the house being half-hers — and I suspect the book will let the sweetness pool before it turns. I also keep waiting for Sheri's heat to land on someone, and for the scar/parents to finally open.
