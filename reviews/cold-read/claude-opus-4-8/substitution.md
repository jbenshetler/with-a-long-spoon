## The felt read

Oh, this one worked on me and scared me at the same time, and it's the *same* thing doing both.

I knew before Cassie said "that cute guy in line is looking at you" — I knew who was going to be walking across that coffee shop, because I've been in his bedroom. This is Pace. The man with the bench. The man who slices apples thin because Randi eats thin slices, who gives the pencil back at exactly the line where you can finish it yourself. And now I'm watching him do the *identical* move — read the problem like he's never seen it, ask what her professor said instead of telling her what he'd say, write the margin note instead of the rewrite, hand the pencil back — except this time I'm inside the head of the girl it's being done *to*, and she has no idea. That gap is the whole chapter, and it's excruciating in the good way.

Because on Vee, it lands as the most wonderful hour of her life. And the writing *earns* the swoon before it poisons it. "May I call you." She hears the *may* exactly the way I did — a word from another vocabulary, small and formal and not affected — and her body's nodding before her mouth catches up. The forearms. "What he did with his hands besides math." The clothes that give her "nothing to hold," a man who fits none of her categories. I felt the pull of him *with* her, honestly — that's what makes it awful. He is genuinely, precisely charming. I've just also watched him take a woman apart on a walnut bench until she begged in a voice that wasn't hers, and then watched that woman decide to go back behind her face forever. So every warm competent thing he does here reads double: *this is exactly how good he is at finding the person under the surface* — and Vee is all surface-hunger and open door, the "someone open, someone warm" he told himself he wanted on the quad.

My sympathy is entirely with Vee, and it aches. She's the girl from the last two chapters — the right pen, the high table, *love you girl*, the one starving to be chosen by exactly the person she'd have chosen to choose her. And here she even *borrows Randi's bit* — "Vivienne Thorne, V.T., yes like the university, no my parents didn't plan it" — she reaches for the practiced swerve she watched Randi do, tries it on, and it lands. She's already substituting herself toward Randi, becoming a version of her. Which, given the title, made my stomach drop.

**Substitution.** The chapter hands me its own key and I don't love that it's quite so legible, but it's dramatized well enough that I don't mind much. The math is the metaphor doing overtime: "turn the thing in front of you into a thing you already know how to do." An integral that circles back on itself no matter which road you take — Randi, closed, stuck, the problem that won't give. And Euler's, the thing Vee learned years ago and set aside as a curiosity that "didn't seem like it was *for* anything" — two things put together at right angles that "turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain, easy to name, easy to know what to do with." And *u-substitution* is the technique the title names. Who's being substituted for whom? Randi went behind the face and won't come back out. Vee is fresh, open, unsolved, willing. I can't shake the read that Vee is the substitution — for Randi, on that bench, in that too-warm house. And that lands even harder because last chapter closed on the professor saying *latent variables — the factors you can't measure directly, that you have to infer from the ones you can*. This whole book keeps telling me it's about the thing under the surface you have to infer. Cassie infers it in one line.

**Randi** is the coldest thing in the room and she's barely doing anything. She's *texting* — "thumbs quick, eyes down, the private, absorbed focus of a girl writing to someone who had her attention," and Vee thinks *some boy*. I don't think it's some boy. I think she's texting him in. And then that coffee cup — up to her mouth and down again twice with no sip happening, held a beat longer than a sip requires. Vee files it as boredom. I file it as something I can't name yet and that's what makes it good: is that jealousy? Nerves? The strain of watching a plan work? Triumph? The page won't tell me and I won't force it. But when she says, flat and easy, "He was cute. You should go out with him" — pushing Vee straight at him — I went cold. Whatever Randi is doing, she's *handing Vee over*, and she's doing it in the warm friend-voice. I don't trust her an inch, and I'm no longer sure I even like her, which is a real move from where I was two chapters ago when I mostly ached for her.

**Cassie.** God, Cassie. She's the reader on the page. "He looked at you like he'd forgotten there was anyone else in the room. He looked at her—" the chin at Randi "—the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." She names the exact thing, no flinch, the look that slides off her own self every day. And last chapter she said *Goodbye, Vee* — the whole finished word, wrong for a Wednesday — and now here she is again seeing the con clearly while Vee's face "smooths" the wrongness away. I trust Cassie completely and I'm frightened for what she can already see.

**Trust/suspicion:** total suspicion, and the text earned every bit of it — this isn't me being on guard, it's me holding memory the character doesn't have. The "coincidence" is not one. Randi's phone, Randi's cup, Randi's shove toward the date, Pace's word-for-word repetition of the technique I watched him run on Randi. The one thing genuinely ajar is Randi's *interior* — whether this is procurement (get Vee for the threesome she wanted helplessly on the quad), or substitution (feed Vee to Pace in her own place), or something she hasn't admitted to herself. I'll leave it ajar.

**Erotic charge:** it's a fully-clothed courtship and it still ran warm on me — the forearms, "asked for it with his eyes first," the *may I* landing in her body before her mind, the pencil-back as its own small intimacy. It's a simmer that does real work, not a peak. And for me it's shadowed the whole time by dread, which cuts the heat even as the craft raises it.

**Friction:** almost none, but the metaphor is the one thumb I felt on the scale — "Put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain — easy to name, easy to know what to do with." That's the book explaining itself to me a hair more than it needs to, and the title *Substitution* pre-loaded the reading before I got there. I'd call it a near-miss rather than a miss; the scene is charming enough to carry its own symbolism.

**The titles.** Chapter: *Substitution* — it gave something away before I read it, and then the chapter confirmed the giveaway. Reading it, I can't not see Vee as the substituted term, the fresh variable swapped in for the one that stopped solving. The cover board says **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION**, title **Substitution** (I notice the *chapter* and the *volume* share the name — that's a signal all its own, this chapter is the book's thesis). "With a long spoon" — the proverb is *he who sups with the devil should have a long spoon*, and I feel that in my teeth now: someone is dining with something dangerous, and the question the title poses is who brought the spoon and whether it's long enough. "A polite invitation" — *may I call you.* The politeness *is* the danger; the courtesy is the bait. It all points one way from here: an invitation extended, beautifully, to someone who has no idea what she's being invited into.

**What I want / dread next (guesses from here):** I dread that Vee is being brought in — for Randi, or for Pace, or for both — and that she'll walk toward it glowing, reading every warm signal exactly as she's meant to. I want Cassie to be wrong and I don't believe she is. I want to know what Randi actually feels watching this, because the coffee cup is hiding something. And I fear the tenderness Randi couldn't survive is going to be aimed at Vee, who won't have the armor to survive it either — but who also won't want to.
