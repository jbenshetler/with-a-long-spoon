# Cold read — The Bench

## Reader reaction

So this is where the book starts, and it starts *deep* in something already established. Pace and Randi — and the jacket told me her name is Miranda, "Randi to everyone," so when he finally says "There you are, Randi" at the end I felt the click of confirmation, but the chapter withholds her name until the last beat and that withholding turns out to be a move. For most of the chapter she's just "she," which pushed me right up against her interiority without a handle to hold her by.

Let me talk about the heat first, because it's the surface. The chapter is a slow, sustained erotic build and it *works* on me — but in a way I found myself increasingly unsettled by, and I think that's intended. The kitchen opening is genuinely warm and lovely: the unlocked door, the deadbolt she turns first, the sliced honeycrisp apple, "bite, kiss, bite." That's tender, domestic, sexy in a low simmering way. The line "the small economy of two people who did not perform for each other" — I believed it, I liked it, I wanted to be in that house. The apple detail especially got me: he sliced it thin because she'd only eat thin slices, cut-sides up because she eats them faster that way. That's attentiveness rendered as fact, not sentiment.

Then the bench. And here the erotic charge and my unease start braiding together, which is the whole engine of this chapter. The spanking-and-edging scene is graphic and it's competent at what it does — the withholding, the "not yet," the mapping of her body — but the thing the prose keeps my eyes on is not her pleasure, it's *him watching her lose access to her own performance.* "It was still her *please.* It was still being said in the voice she said *please* in when she asked a waiter for a glass of water." He's not just getting her off; he's hunting for the moment her surface fails. And when he deploys the sorority sisters — "What would Brooke think" — to break her, I felt the scene tip from sexy into something colder. He's using her shame as a tool. Precisely, deliberately. "Brooke was the president of the chapter; Brooke was the one she had told the lie about a paper to."

Here's what did the real work on me, though: the aftermath. The chapter doesn't let me stay in the afterglow. She cries and "did not seem to know she was doing it." Then the water bottle — she "set the sharp inner edge of it under her thumbnail and pressed — slow and steady, down into the nail bed, where it would hurt. She did not seem to know she was doing it." That stopped me cold. That's not sub-drop softness, that's someone hurting herself in a small hidden way, and he notices ("He took the bottle when she set it down") but files it. Then "You're beautiful" makes her *flinch* — "the kind of flinch a body makes when something lands somewhere it was not braced for." And then the bathroom. The locked door — "which was not a thing she had ever done before in his house." The crying she muffles. The face in the mirror. The smile she practices until it "settled," the face that "was lying" and "was good at lying" and "had been lying for years."

That last long stretch in the bathroom is where the chapter announced what it actually is to me. This is not a love scene. This is a scene about a woman discovering she's been cracked open by someone who wanted to crack her, and immediately having to reseal herself and walk back out and lie. "Something in her needed him not to know any more than he already did." The key-turning-in-a-lock image lands twice — the hum "the size of a key turning in a lock," and the very first thing she does on entering is turn the deadbolt. She locks herself in. I don't think that's accidental.

**How I feel about Pace.** This is the interesting one, because for most of the chapter he's rendered as the ideal attentive lover — the whole jacket promise of "asking before he takes and taking only what she gives." He checks in. "Tell me your word." "I won't be upset. Tell me anytime." The safeword, the aftercare, the blanket she'd brought, the water he set out in advance. On the surface he is *exactly* the man the blurb sold me. But the final two paragraphs from his POV chilled me: "He thought, with quiet satisfaction, that this had been a remarkably good evening. He had been right about her. He had been right about the bench." And the killer — "the long pleasant prospect of finding out who she actually was underneath the surface he had just begun to lift." He's not in love. He's *excavating*. He builds things — furniture, the bench — and she's become a thing he's building, or unbuilding. "He had begun building it seven months ago. He had met her three weeks ago." That line is doing a lot: he built an instrument for cracking open a woman he hadn't met yet. She's not special; she's *filling a slot.* That reframes all his tenderness as technique. I don't trust him. The care is real *and* it's instrumental, both at once, and the chapter refuses to let me resolve which.

**How I feel about Randi.** Enormous sympathy, and it grew steadily. She starts as the jacket's "poised, dazzling, certain of everything," and the chapter systematically shows me the cost of that surface — the food policing, the mother "who lived on egg whites and tofu, a thing she said with a knife in it," the plain unpainted nails as their own kind of discipline, the whole apparatus of being looked at. The thumbnail-press and the practiced mirror-smile made me ache for her. She's the one I'm rooting for, and she's the one who "never knew there was one" — the game — per the jacket. Right now I know something's being done *to* her that she can't name, and she's alone in it.

**Trust vs. suspicion.** The text earned my suspicion of Pace outright — not a hunch, the last two POV paragraphs are explicit about his satisfaction and his project. The jacket already primed me ("That was the trap," "the game," "They told her nothing") so I came in braced, but the chapter delivers the unease on its own terms, especially the seven-months/three-weeks line and the "finding out who she actually was underneath" close. What complicates it beautifully is that his cruelty and his care are the *same actions.* I can't cleanly hate him. That's the discomfort the book seems to want.

**Friction.** Very little. Once or twice the interpretive voice leaned in a hair far — "The bench was not the wanted position. The bench was the needing position — something required that you could not supply yourself." That's the narrator telling me the thesis of the scene a touch too cleanly, spelling out what the action was already showing. I'd have felt it more if it stayed unspoken. But it's a small thing. The prose is otherwise controlled and confident and I mostly forgot I was reading.

**The titles.** "The Bench" — innocuous on the way in, an object. Coming out, the bench *is* the chapter: the disguised massage table, the gift built before the recipient existed, the thing she'll walk past and refuse to look at, sitting "like an unwrapped gift... waiting for the next time." The title points at the instrument of the whole operation. And the book title, *With a Long Spoon* — I know the proverb, "he who sups with the devil should have a long spoon." So someone here is the devil, and someone is supping without enough distance. The jacket says the game got "as real for the two who started the game as for the girl who never knew there was one" — so maybe *everyone's* spoon is too short. The tagline, "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap," lands hard after this chapter, because I just watched a scene where every yes *was* freely given — the safeword offered, the door she locks herself, the position she chose — and it was still a trap. The consent is real and it's the mechanism of the harm. That's the disquieting thing the chapter proves out.

**What I want / dread next.** I want to meet Vivienne, who the jacket says is the actual target of "the game" — and nothing in this chapter is about her yet. This chapter is Pace-and-Randi, established lovers. So my *guess from here*: this is the "before," the machinery being shown to me — how Pace works on a woman, and who Randi is when she's the one on the bench — so that when they turn this apparatus toward Vivienne I'll understand exactly what's being done to her. I dread that. I dread watching them do to a stranger what I just watched him do to Randi. And I'm now watching Randi too — the jacket says she's "falling as hard as she is," so the woman running the game is also getting cracked. That hum in her ribs might be the first hairline of it.
