## Reader reaction

# The Felt Read

Well. That was a lot more assured, and a lot more disquieting, than I expected from an opening chapter — and I mean that as praise and as a warning flag both.

First, the body. The erotic writing works on me, and it works because it's *slow*. The whole apple sequence — thin slices, cut sides up because she eats them faster that way, bite-kiss-bite while she straddles him — is genuinely charged, and it's charged precisely because he's paying such minute attention to her. The undressing on one knee, the buttons, the pulse in her throat: I was there for it, and it's tender in a way that's unusual for this kind of material. The bench scene itself peaks for me not at the physical spanking but at the psychological turn — the "wanted position" versus "the needing position." When he starts naming Brooke, invoking the sorority sisters, and her *please* changes register from performed to real — that landed hard. So heat: high, and it doesn't go clinical, even though he's clearly a technician of some kind.

But here's the thing the jacket did *not* prepare me for, and where my stomach turned over. This is not, as I read it, a scene of pure mutual bliss. The last third recolors the entire chapter. She goes into the bathroom, locks the door — a thing she's never done — and we get her interior, and it's a small quiet catastrophe. "He could have the face... The one he had found was going back behind it, and she was not coming out again." She experienced something ecstatic and *she is fleeing it*. And then — the detail that chilled me most — she takes the water bottle cap and presses the sharp edge under her thumbnail, "slow and steady, down into the nail bed, where it would hurt," and "did not seem to know she was doing it." That's not aftercare glow. That's a woman hurting herself quietly.

So my feeling about **Pace** is complicated and moving even within this one chapter. On the surface he's the jacket's dream: "asking before he takes and taking only what she gives." He checks in, he gives her the safeword, he means it, he's warm. And yet. The chapter ends on him making "small contented plans about her," thinking "with quiet satisfaction" that he'd been "right about her," "right about the bench," anticipating "who she actually was underneath the surface he had just begun to lift." He built this bench *seven months ago* and met her *three weeks ago* — the text makes a point of that, and it made the back of my neck prickle. He is confident he's *found her*, that he's *feeding a hunger*, that he's doing her a kindness. And she has just decided, in the next room, to hide from him forever. He has no idea. The dramatic irony is total and it's awful. His satisfaction sitting on top of her private wound — that's the engine of dread here. I don't distrust his intentions exactly; I distrust his *certainty*. A man that sure he's read someone correctly, and that pleased about "changing" her "scene by scene," is frightening even when he's gentle. Especially when he's gentle.

**Randi** — and note the reveal that "she" is Randi, held back until the very last beat of the bench scene ("There you are, Randi") — is the one I ache for. The portrait of the food control, the mother on egg whites "a thing she said with a knife in it," the face she built her life around, the plain unpainted nails as her one honesty, the blue toenails as her one small rebellion — that's rich, specific characterization. She's not a bimbo; she's armored. And the armor is the tragedy. Her final line, "she was going to have to figure out what she was going to do about this," and the "key turning in a lock" — I read that as menace toward *him*, or at least toward the situation, though I can't tell yet whether it's flight, calculation, or something colder.

Now — the jacket. This is where I got genuinely disoriented, and I want to flag it honestly. The blurb told me about **Miranda — Randi** — "poised, dazzling... secretly the lover of a young mathematician," who with him picks **Vivienne Thorne** to be a third in their bed. So walking in, I *expected* Randi and her lover to be the schemers and Vivienne the naive target. This chapter shows me Randi and Pace — I assume Pace is the mathematician, though the chapter never says mathematician; he's a man who builds furniture with quartersawn oak and lives alone at the end of... well, a house you drive to. That fits "lives alone at the end of a long drive." So this is the founding couple, *before* Vivienne. And the twist the chapter plays on the jacket: the blurb framed Randi as the confident architect of a game, but what I just watched is Randi being quietly *undone* — the one who "started the game" being shown as, herself, a person with a trap already inside her. The tagline, "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap," now reads two ways: yes, it'll be the trap they set for Vivienne — but tonight it was *Randi's* every yes that trapped *Randi*. She consented to the position and then met something she "had not consented to even though she had consented to the position." That line is practically the tagline dramatized.

The **book title**, *With a Long Spoon* — "he who sups with the devil should have a long spoon." So: someone's dining with a devil here and their spoon isn't long enough. Who's the devil? Pace, with his pre-built bench and his benevolent certainty? Or is everyone at this table underestimating what they're touching? I don't know yet, but the proverb makes me read Pace's tenderness as the bait rather than the gift. That's a heavy frame to carry, and this chapter earned it.

The **chapter title, "The Bench."** Innocuous going in — furniture. Coming out, it's the whole apparatus: a thing built to look like a massage table, that turns out to be an instrument, that "she had seen without seeing," under which "everything a surface usually covered would hang free." The bench *is* the book's method in miniature: something disguised as comfort that's actually a designed exposure. And the closing image — the top going back on, the bench becoming "a low table of odd proportions again," "full of what it was... waiting for the next time" — that's a beautifully sinister button.

**Friction.** A little. The interior-monologue in the bathroom, while gorgeous, is *very* explicated — the "wanted position / needing position" gets spelled out more than once, and the face-as-armor thesis is stated almost essayistically: "Nothing happened to her. That was what the face was for." I bought it emotionally but the author's hand is visible there; it's telling me the meaning rather than letting me find it. Same with Pace's closing thoughts — "All he had done was find it, and answer it" — it's a hair too tidy, too willing to hand me his self-justification cleanly. The scene is confident enough that it doesn't always need to close the loop for me. But these are small. The thumbnail detail, by contrast, trusts me completely, and it's the best thing in the chapter.

**What I want next.** I want Vivienne, obviously — the jacket promised her and she's not here yet, so I'm holding a chair for her. But mostly I want to know what Randi *does* with that key-in-the-lock decision, and I dread the moment Pace's certainty meets her hidden refusal. *Guess from here:* the "game" of recruiting Vivienne is going to be, for Randi, a way of managing exactly what happened on that bench — putting a third person in the bed so she never has to be alone in it as the one who gets seen. She'll deploy the third as armor. And Pace will think he's doing kindness again. And the person who actually never knew there was a game — Vivienne — may be the only one who gets out clean, or the one who gets hurt worst. I can't tell which, and I want to.

Strong open. It made me uneasy in the right way.
