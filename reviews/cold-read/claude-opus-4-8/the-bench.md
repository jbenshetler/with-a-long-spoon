## Reader reaction

Well. I came in braced for Vee, and the chapter handed me Randi instead — and did it so quietly I didn't clock the swap until the name landed at the very end: *"There you are, Randi."* That reveal is the chapter's whole architecture. For pages I read a nameless "she" being undressed and worked over by Pace, and the withholding of her name turned out to be doing exactly what the scene is about — a woman who *is* only a face, a surface, until the name (the person) gets dragged out from behind it. When "Randi" arrives it lands like the thing Pace has been fishing for all night finally surfacing. Gorgeous, cruel little piece of construction.

So — the jacket promised me the *seduction of Vee*, orchestrated by "her glamorous new friend Randi." This chapter shows me Randi is not a bystander friend; she's Pace's lover, and the two of them are clearly the "two people who started it." I'm reading the origin, the thing before Vee, the machine before it's pointed at anyone. That reframes Randi completely from the jacket's throwaway "listens and draws her open" — here she's the one being drawn open, and hating it.

On the heat: it worked on me, genuinely, and slowly. The apple in the kitchen — thin slices, cut sides up, the man who *noticed she eats faster when the cut sides show* — is the single most erotic thing in the chapter and there's no nudity in it. The bite-kiss-bite game, his hands making unhurried passes up her sides and *turning back before they arrive*, "the point did not survive the next pass" — I was swept. The whole first movement is patience rendered as appetite, and it's the good kind of explicit-adjacent, where restraint is the charge. The bench scene itself is graphic and sustained and technically expert, but honestly it worked on me *less* as heat and more as psychology — by the time she's begging I was watching her come apart, not feeling turned on. Which I think is the point, but I'll be honest that the erotic peak for me was the kitchen.

Now the unease, and the page earned every bit of it. This is not a warm consent scene despite the immaculate consent scaffolding — the safeword, the "tell me anytime," the aftercare, the water bottle left in advance. All of it is real and all of it is *tender*, and the chapter then shows me the tenderness is the thing she cannot survive. That flip in the bathroom is the best writing here. Being seen is the violation, not the spanking. *"Nothing happened to her. That was what the face was for."* And the resolution she comes to — quiet as a deadbolt going over, *not tonight — not ever* — she's going to give him the face and put the real one away for good. That's genuinely dark and I loved it. She walks back out and lies with her whole body, and he reads it as *a remarkably good evening*, and the gap between them is the whole horror.

Which brings me to Pace. I don't fully trust the frame the chapter gives him. He's rendered as courtly, patient, self-aware, *good* — the Daphne memory is there to certify him as a man who learned to respect a closed door. And yet. He built a bench, in secret, disguised as furniture she'd been laying her underwear on for weeks. He slices the apple to exploit an eating disorder he's clearly diagnosed. He works her to a place she "could not have produced," and the last line I sit with is his *small warm pleasure of having taken her further than he had thought she would go.* The book keeps his interior generous — he means her well — but the machinery underneath is a man engineering a woman's undoing and feeling *moved* by it. I don't think the page wants me to condemn him. I think it wants me to feel the pull of him *and* see the thing he can't see: that the "hunger" he's proud of finding is a wound, and she's just decided he can never touch it again. The dramatic irony is exquisite and load-bearing.

The detail that chilled me most, small and easy to miss: the water bottle cap, the *sharp inner edge of it under her thumbnail... pressed slow and steady, down into the nail bed, where it would hurt. She did not seem to know she was doing it.* That's the tell that "quietly starving" and the portioned apple aren't quirks — there's real self-harm running under the gloss, and nobody, including her, is looking at it directly. The bare unpainted nails as "the effort and the cost of her" reads different once you watch her drive a cap edge under one.

**Trust vs. suspicion:** I don't distrust the *page* — it's being scrupulously honest with me. I distrust the tenderness *as safety*. The scene's consent is real and its damage is also real and those two facts are sitting on top of each other, which is precisely what unsettles me, earned entirely.

**Titles.** "The Bench" — innocuous going in, an object. Coming out, it's the whole thing: a piece of built furniture, disguised, purpose-made, that "sits there full of what it was... waiting for the next time." The last paragraph practically tells me the bench is the book — the apparatus that looks like ordinary furniture and is actually built to open someone. *A Polite Invitation* now reads as menace: everything here is polite, invited, chosen, *asked before taken* — and that's the trap, not the mercy. *With a Long Spoon* — "he who sups with the devil should use a long spoon." So someone's dining with something dangerous and thinks they've got distance. The jacket's closer, *"Every yes was freely given. That was the problem"* — this chapter is the proof of concept. Every yes tonight was real. And it cost her the one thing she had.

**What I want next:** I want Vee, now — because I've seen the workshop and I dread watching the tool get pointed at someone who doesn't know Randi at all. And I want to know what Randi *does* with the resolution she made in the mirror, because a woman who decides to weaponize her own face after being cracked open is a terrifying and fascinating thing to follow. *Guess from here:* the "game" the jacket names starts with Randi bringing Pace a girl — Vee — the way you'd hand someone the thing that unmade you and see if it unmakes them too. But I'm not sure yet, and I'll let it stay ajar.
