## Reader reaction

A short one, and a breather — and after the last chapter's slow annexation, this quiet is welcome and also awful, because the awful is all in what Cassie can see and Vee can't. This is the roommate holding the door open again, and the streetlight walking in lit up and not feeling the cold. That image — "the thin cold off the window found her at the door as it always did… and didn't feel it" — is doing the whole chapter's work in one line: she is anesthetized, warm all the way through from the car and the house, immune to the cold that Cassie deliberately keeps in the room.

Cassie is the best thing on this page and I love her flat, dry way of loving Vee. "You're doing the streetlight thing again. You're a public utility." That's affection with a needle in it. And then she does exactly what a good friend and a clear reader does — she lets Vee run herself all the way out, "no face on at all," and only *then* drops the two flat un-questions that land like stones: "You don't come home school nights." "Do you love him." She doesn't argue. She doesn't say *I know*. She lets the *maybe* stand and sends her to bed. That restraint chilled me more than a fight would have — Cassie sees the shape and knows she can't push on it, so she just keeps the window cracked and waits.

The thing that snagged me hardest, and I think it's meant to: the soup. Vee tells it as a sweet story — she slopped it down her front, he had a cold cloth on the burn before she'd finished swearing, gentle, bent over it, kept it watched two days after. And Cassie's only reply is "With not enough on," pleasant. Twice that phrase lands — "not enough on," "never once thought about how much she had on." Cassie hears it. She hears a girl who is underdressed, underfed of her own vigilance, in a house kept warm enough that she never notices how little she's wearing or carrying. From where I sit that "warm as a held breath" house is the anesthetic again, and the burn story is one more instance of him producing care so fast it reads as omniscience — *he noticed things before she did.* That's tender on its surface and, knowing what I know, it's surveillance rendered as devotion.

What moved for me: nothing new about Pace or Randi (Randi's fully offstage, not even scented here), but Vee's own reckoning ticked. She "went back over the week she hadn't counted and found her own bed had sat empty more nights than it hadn't." She's not counting her own nights. That's the residence question from last chapter answering itself sideways — she hasn't decided to move in, she's just *not been home*, and had to be told. And the *fine* speech is the most naked she's been: every man before him was "fine," she thought fine was the whole of what there was to have, and she can't find the word for what he is, so she puts her hands up "at the size of it." I felt the ache clean there. It's real love, or real something, and it's pointed at exactly the wrong man, and she can't even measure it — "It's all so fast." She says the trap's own tempo out loud and doesn't hear it.

No erotic charge here and that's right — it's a debrief chapter, all clothed, all kitchen and bean bag. The only heat is remembered and secondhand (the burn story), and even that Cassie reads as hazard, not sex.

Trust/suspicion: unchanged and total toward the operation; Cassie remains the one clean instrument and I trust her completely — she's the reader's proxy now, seeing the shape without the facts. My only friction is how *good* Cassie is at it; she borders on too-perfect a reader, but the flatness keeps her human rather than a mouthpiece. No thumb-on-scale line I want to quote against — the restraint held.

The title, "School Nights," is the quiet knife. On the surface it's just Cassie's line, the ordinary complaint of a roommate whose friend keeps disappearing. Read against the page it's the tell she can't get past: the empty bed on nights that are supposed to be structured, ordinary, *hers* — the encroachment measured not in a declaration but in absences Vee didn't even tally. It doesn't give anything away; it points at the residence creeping in under cover of ordinariness. The book's title and tagline sit heavy here in the softest possible key: "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap." There's no yes extracted on this page at all — just a girl who forgot to go home, freely, gladly, and had to be reminded she'd stopped. That *is* the trap. *With a Long Spoon* — the distance-as-tool — is now Cassie's, the friend holding the long spoon, keeping the window cold, feeding Vee the two flat questions from arm's length because she knows she can't grab.

What I want next: I want this to reach Cassie's ears more fully — she's one soup story away from asking the questions that matter (the money, the two-night weekends, the weight). And I dread the moment Randi and Pace resurface, because this chapter was clean of them and it felt like held breath. *Guess from here:* Cassie is being seeded as the one who eventually sees it whole, and the cruelty will be that Vee has already downgraded her below Randi — so when Cassie finally speaks the truth, Vee will have the wrong best friend to run it past.
