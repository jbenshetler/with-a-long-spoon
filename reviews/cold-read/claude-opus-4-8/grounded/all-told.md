## Reader reaction

FELT READ

After the flood of "Peekaboo" — the dress, the crying, the bare leg walked to him — this chapter is a comedown in the best sense, a bright, ordinary morning-after where the swoon is behind her and she's just carrying it around like a warm coal. And it worked on me exactly the way it was built to: I liked being here. Vee "underslept and overfull," late to everything and not touched by any of it, the made bed as a "tattletale in the cold morning light" — that image is lovely, her unslept-in bed telling on her to anyone who cared to read it. I was charmed the whole way through.

What I loved most is the stereo of her two girls. The seat left open between them, two-thirds back. Cassie who "never needed a preamble" going straight to "Your bed's made" — she solved it hours ago off the bed and let Randi have the reveal anyway. And Randi's lamp-warmth snapping on, "Vivienne Thorne. It is a Friday." The two of them run on completely different fuel and the chapter knows it and shows it cleanly: Randi wants the outfit, the plan, the shoes; Cassie wants the friend. That little beat where Vee realizes Cassie never went down into the numbers at all — "Cassie had spent watching what the dress had done to her friend, because the friend was the only part of any of it Cassie had ever been able to see" — that landed on me with real tenderness. Cassie's "Was it a good night?" and Vee's "Yes. Oh, yes," and "something eased in Cassie's face, and she picked her highlighter back up." That's the emotional center and it's quietly gorgeous. Cassie is the person in this book who loves Vee with nothing underneath it, and this chapter let me feel that plainly.

Which is exactly why Randi's "You lucky slut," low and delighted, rang a warning bell I didn't manufacture — the text put it there. The word "landed lower than Randi meant it to," and it went straight to the mother's shaming voice zone, and then in the same breath Randi's already "turning the dress over in her hands without a hand on it." That phrase is the whole Randi in miniature: hands on nothing, working everything. And the shoe date — "That's not a problem, that's a date. You and me, Saturday" — I felt Vee's warm yes and I also felt the machine tick over. Randi finds the one snag in the gold of the day and instantly converts it into more time alone with Vee. Meanwhile Cassie's eyebrow goes up and comes back down over the shoe talk, because Cassie "had never once in her life cared what was on anyone's feet." The chapter keeps setting the two women's interest in Vee side by side and letting me see they are not the same substance. I didn't need to be told; I was shown, and I trust the showing.

The erotic charge here is residual, not present, and that's right for a morning-after — but the one physical beat is sharp: Vee's body "keeping the night's account since eight, a good soreness no gym had ever given her, down low, and up the inside of both thighs, and in her nipples where they sat against her bra," and her certainty for a second that the whole room could read it off her. That's the good kind of exposure-heat, and it's tied to the shame-into-want circuit the book keeps running. It didn't peak into arousal for me, but it kept the ember warm.

Now the Simpson's paradox lecture. This is the chapter's thumb, and I want to be honest that I felt it as a thumb even as I enjoyed it. "A thing can run one way across every short stretch you measure and the opposite way across the whole of it." Vee dutifully writing "short stretches can lie against the whole" and underlining it "because it had the ring of an exam question." I know what this is aimed at — every short stretch of Vee's autumn reads as joy, tenderness, a man building her a dress; put the whole together and it may point the other way. It's the same trick the book pulled with latent variables and error-correcting codes, a stats lecture narrating the machinery to the reader over Vee's oblivious head. I find it clever and slightly too on-the-nose at once. The saving grace is that it's genuinely funny — the professor "reading the back of a cereal box," "the good student coming up for air from her own life" — so the device wears comedy as cover. But if I'm honest it's the one place I felt handled rather than swept.

My feelings haven't moved so much as sharpened. Vee I love and want to protect more with every chapter she spends this happy — she's "on time, now, for nothing," which is a sweet line and a dread-laced one, because the reader knows what's coming and she doesn't. Randi I trust less each time she reaches in and "solves" a problem that puts her closer to Vee. Cassie is my clean window and this chapter made her dearer — she's the only one in that row of three loving Vee without an angle. Pace is offstage but glowing from the night before; my double feeling about him is untouched.

The title. "All Told" — first I read it as the idiom, the sum of it, everything added up: which is precisely the Simpson's paradox point (add the two years and the answer flips). But it's also literally what the chapter is — Vee telling it, all told, the morning-after report. Except she doesn't tell all: not the dress made real, not the stripping bare, not the soreness. She tells an edited brightness. So "all told" curdles a little on second thought — she thinks she's telling everything and she's telling a curated version, and the reader knows the two people hearing it hold cards she can't see. That's a good, quietly cruel title. It didn't give anything away going in; it recolored coming out.

The book's title and series. "A Polite Invitation," "With a Long Spoon," and the tagline "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." All of that hums under this chapter without being touched directly — the shoe date is another polite invitation, freely accepted, and I feel the long spoon (sup with the devil) in Randi's hand-that-isn't-a-hand turning the dress over. Nothing here betrayed the promise; it kept ticking it forward.

What I want next: the shoe date, obviously — Randi and Vee alone, Saturday, and I dread and want it in equal measure because Randi's kisses have been escalating and now she's engineered another private afternoon. And the induction itself, the dress finally shown "to not one living soul," which the last line pointedly reserves. (Guess from here, marked as such: the shoe trip is where Randi pushes further, and I half-expect the induction to be the room where the whole architecture is tested — where being looked at either becomes her triumph or her exposure.)
