## Reader reaction

FELT READ

So this is the scene the jacket was whispering about — "it began as a game between the two people now closest to her, a secret choice made about her." I've just watched that choice get made, and it's Vee, and I have to sit with the fact that the chapter made it genuinely lovely to watch while I knew exactly what I was watching. That doubleness is the whole experience of this chapter for me: warmth I actually felt, laid directly over a chill I couldn't put down.

The opening bed scene worked on me straightforwardly. It's warm and generous and I liked Pace in it — "kissed her, slow and tender, for no reason but that he wanted to," the way he holds off his own release not as a power move but because he wants her to have "every joy while the question still hung in the room." And the question itself — "Would you like to taste another woman?" — landing in her body before it lands in her, pulling a third orgasm out of her "far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it." That's a beautiful piece of writing about a want arriving ahead of the person. The heat here is real and I let it be.

But then the chapter shows me its hand, and this is where my trust and my unease braid together and won't come apart — deliberately, I think, and earned. Pace reads Randi like an instrument he built. He watches her move the wanting onto him — "she had handed the appetite to him and made herself its generous instrument: not a woman who hungered for another woman, which was unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act. A different owner." He sees the self-deception perfectly and he lets it stand, because the deception is the wrapper the wanting needs to come out in. That is exactly the "psychologically merciless in its tenderness" the jacket promised, and it's the first time I've felt the merciless part land with full weight. He is being kind to her AND he is managing her out from behind her own defenses for his own live private charge. Both. I don't get to pick one.

And the pointing game itself gave me the cleanest unease the book has earned so far. Two people strolling a beautiful autumn quad appraising strangers — "the way she might consider a dish she had not ordered" — while I know one of those strangers is the girl on the cover. "I point. You pick." He points at a human being. The verdicts Randi hands down ("too expected," "she'd be a project," "God, no") are sharp and characterful and I even enjoyed them as banter — and that enjoyment is the trap the chapter set for me, the same one it set for her. By the time Vee comes out of the dining hall I'm braced.

And Vee — the swoon and the dread arrive in the same image. She's the one woman who doesn't offer "the grammar" anything to grade: lush, uncurated, copper hair the wind won't leave alone, freckles, "laughing like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen." That last clause put a hand on my chest, because being seen is the exact thing that broke Randi open on the bench and sent her behind the face — and here's a girl who's forgotten she can be seen, about to be seen very deliberately. The bare "Yes" out of Randi with nothing around it, the arrested mid-step, the follow breaking frame before the lead calls it — I believed her want completely. It's the realest thing she's shown since the bench.

Then the collision. The tradecraft of it — a decade of dance folded into three seconds so clean "it looked like nothing — looked, if anything, like the redhead had walked into her." That's chilling and I mean that as praise. Randi engineered an accident and made the target apologize for it. "Her name is Vee. And we've got a stats class together." The trap now has a schedule.

Where does my feeling move? Toward Randi, mostly — she deepened. Her lit-up grin, the "yes" squeaking out of her, the private in-bed register brought into daylight for one second: I ache for her a little, because I've read "Standards," I know the retreat that's coming or has come, and here she is briefly unguarded and delighted and doesn't know she's the one being hunted too. Pace I trust less than I did after "The Bench," not because he's cruel but because I've now seen the machinery from inside and it's aimed at a person who has no idea she's in it. That's the shift.

One genuine friction: the chronology confused me. "Standards" left Randi shut down under a duvet, having decided he could have the face and never her; this chapter has her openly, helplessly delighted, no costume holding. It reads as if it happens earlier than the bench — "the first week... She was not performing now," "Four weeks in." I couldn't place it against the retreat I just watched, and I'm holding that open rather than forcing it. If it's meant to sit before those chapters, the reading order threw me.

Erotic charge: peaks hard in the opening, then goes to a good simmer on the walk — the kiss where he lets her taste herself again in public and her breath "caught against his lips and held," her composure with "a crack in it." It never goes clinical. It stays warm even while the content turns predatory, which is precisely what unsettles me.

TITLES: "The Pointing Game" is flatly literal on the surface — he points, she picks — and that innocence is the point. On the turn it's colder: pointing at people, a selection, a hunt dressed as a couple's private play. The book title A Polite Invitation now has teeth: the "invitation" reached Vee before she knew there was one, and I've just watched it get written. With a Long Spoon — I still read that as the proverb, sup with the devil and bring a long spoon; this chapter is the devils choosing whom to invite to sup, and the long spoon is the deniability, the game-wrapper, the "a different owner" that lets everyone keep their hands clean. The tagline I was given, "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem," sits directly on top of this chapter — Randi's every yes tonight was freely, gladly given, and that is exactly what makes it awful.

What I want next: Vee, on the page, in her own light. I've only seen her from across a lawn, chosen. I want to meet the girl the jacket says never knew there was a plan, and I dread it a little, because I'll be watching her walk toward it "because everything in her has been waiting to," and I'll be ahead of her the whole way. Guess from here: the stats class is the next move, and Randi is the one who gets deployed first.

Randi deepened — her need to relocate the wanting to a survivable owner is fully consistent with the bench, and her helpless bare "Yes" is the realest she's been. Vee lands vivid in one paragraph but is still only a surface-and-a-laugh to me. No one flattened.

Pace — within the chapter: the opening earns its length; the walk is patient by design and mostly earns it, though the string of pointed-at women runs a touch long before Vee — I felt the catalogue stretch just before the payoff, which may be intentional patience but grazed tedium.

Pace — chapter to chapter: building. After the interior stillness of "Standards," this pushes the actual plot into motion and finally puts the jacket's central girl on the page. Momentum up — though the chronological relationship to The Bench/Standards left me briefly disoriented rather than propelled.
