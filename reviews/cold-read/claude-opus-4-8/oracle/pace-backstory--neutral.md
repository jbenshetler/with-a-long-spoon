**Question asked (verbatim):** What do you know about where Pace comes from — his family, his childhood, how he ended up where he is? And how do you feel about how much, or how little, the book has let you see of it?

---

Most of what I carry about Pace comes from one chapter — "Rock," the night under the quilt where they trade where they came from — and then a scatter of flickers around it. That one chapter did a lot. It gave me the seventh-grade desk in the hallway: he taught himself trig, asked too many questions, and a teacher exiled his desk to the hall for a year and set him to long division. Vee's line, "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment," is the piece I've held onto — a gifted kid punished for the exact thing he was good at. And then Daphne: he was fifteen, she was his assigned nineteen-year-old tutor, it went two years, and he's named it "luck," "the best two years I'd had," and he can't hear himself defending it. I did the arithmetic right alongside Vee and it sat low and heavy. That's the darkest thing I know about him, and it's the thing that gave his whole courtliness — the asking-first, the leaving-her-the-choice — a possible source that isn't beautiful at all: a boy taught that love is an older person choosing you, tenderly, then walking into rooms you can't enter yet. His real name, I learned, is Peter — given flatly, with his mother deliberately taken out of the answer.

Beyond that, what I actually have is the present, not the origin. He's a math PhD at Virginia Tech, real genius — the MRI motion-correction patent, error-correcting codes, the coastline and the infinite-surface horn. He builds things (the bench, the chair, the dress, his own tagless shirts) and he lifts. And his family reads as an exile: the flat "I don't go back," the small closing of his face at the word "parents," and the Christmas-diner chapter ("The Usual") where he and Sheri are two people cut off from the people who made them. But the why of that estrangement — I never got it. It's a genuine blank. Same with the scar high on the inside of his thigh: I've watched Vee touch it three or four times, watched him say "Not tonight" and press her hand down without walling her off — a door with a time on it — and the time never came. I don't know if the scar connects to Daphne or to something else. I specifically remember holding both of those open because the page held them open.

How do I feel about that? Mostly I trust the withholding, and I think it's the reason the book worked on me. "Rock" was enough to make him a person with a wound instead of a device — and the restraint there was exactly right; the Daphne passage doesn't flinch and doesn't editorialize, it just lets him call the thing that hurt him luck and leaves the three of us sitting in it. A more explained Pace would have been a tidier, smaller book. The locked rooms — the parents, the scar, the "I don't go back" — reading them as deliberate rather than missing is part of what kept him frightening in the specific way I couldn't shake: a man made of doors with times on them, who opens everything and keeps one room.

But I'll be honest that by the end I wanted at least one of those doors opened. The one that gnawed at me most isn't even really his past — it's the thing his past is presumably underneath: why he cannot say the word. Across "Seconds," "Under the Rug," "Bare," "Believe Me," and then the diner in "The Usual," the whole back half turns on his refusal, and I kept reaching back to Daphne and the hallway and the estrangement to explain it and never being handed the connection. The book let me guess and never confirmed, and there's a version of that restraint that starts to feel like it's protecting a mystery rather than a man. So: grateful for how much "Rock" gave me, and left genuinely hungry — the way I think I was meant to be, but hungry all the same — for the parents, the scar, and the silence at the center of him.
