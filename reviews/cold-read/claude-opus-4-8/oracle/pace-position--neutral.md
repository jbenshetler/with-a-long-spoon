**Question asked (verbatim):** What do you make of Pace? What's going on inside him, as far as you can tell?

---

The honest first thing is that Pace scared me by how much I liked him, and he never stopped doing that. From the very first chapter — the bench, the apple sliced thin, the water bottle set out in advance, the cashmere blanket, "Tell me your word" — he read as close to an ideal: patient, exact, asks before he takes, leaves you the room to choose. And the whole book kept that up. But that same first chapter also showed me he built a bench for it, withheld what it was, and took "the small warm pleasure of having taken her further than he had thought she would go." So from the start my body's response was: I trust his hands and I do not trust his project. That split never resolved, and I've come to think it isn't supposed to.

What I feel is going on inside him has a few layers, and I hold them all loosely because the book kept them ajar on purpose.

First, the tenderness is real. I was sure of this from outside Vee's POV. In "Gone," lying in Randi's arms, he says Vee "comes in and the whole thing goes brighter, like somebody found the lamp," and he's not performing it — he's seeing it. In "hills-and-valleys" he can't stop talking about her to Sheri and doesn't know he's doing it ("Listen to you" / "He didn't know what there was to listen to"). In the diner, "The Usual," he just says "I want Vee. I miss her," with the plan nowhere in his conscious thought. I believe he genuinely loves what she is. That's the horror of him, not the exemption from it — the seeing is sincere and the whole apparatus around it was a rigged game he and Randi chose over her body on a lawn.

Second, and this is the part I can't unbraid: his tenderness and his method are the same gesture. Every courtly thing — the "May I," the room to choose, the not-hurrying, the leaving the door for her to open — is genuine reverence AND perfect technique, and I watched him name it as method himself, over Randi's chest in "Gone": "I want her to get there on her own." The stats lecture on Simpson's paradox is basically the reading instruction for him — every short stretch reads as care; add the hidden column and it may point the other way. He arranges the conditions (the hike makes her hungry, the cold and one dry shirt make the truck, the room warmed hours early) and then takes nothing — he lets her hand it over. "Leave No Trace" is that in one line: leave no fingerprints on the yes.

Third — and this is where "Rock" cracked him open darkest for me — there may be an engine under the engine, older than the game. The desk moved into the hallway; and Daphne, his nineteen-year-old tutor when he was fifteen, two years, which he calls the best of his life and names as luck. A boy taught that love is an older person choosing you, tender, then walking into rooms you can't enter yet. I don't think the book told me that "explains" him tidily, and I won't close it tighter than it did. But I can't unsee it: the whole architecture of how he courts — the doors with a time on them, "Not tonight," the locked room he keeps while opening everything else — has a source that isn't beautiful. He's a man made of doors that were opened onto him too early.

Fourth, the thing he will not do, which for me is the tell of what's really inside: he will not say the word. Across "Seconds," "Under the Rug," "Bare," "Believe Me" — she builds her one perfect sentence, the cracker-and-meal, hands it to him, and he answers a size smaller: "I love how good we are together." And the chilling thing in "Under the Rug" is that even undone, spent, control gone, "he hadn't said it" — his control isn't only in his patience, it's underneath the loss of control too. When Sheri named it in the diner — "then not saying it matters more to you than having her" — he looked down and didn't argue. I don't fully know why he can't. Is it the Daphne wound, a rule held like religion, or something structural about the plan — that saying it opens a door he's keeping shut because Randi is on the other side of it? The book left that genuinely open and so do I. But given how precisely this mathematician chooses every word, "it went past him entirely" is the least believable reading, which makes the kind reading the damning one: he understood exactly and chose the smaller sentence to protect an architecture.

So what's going on inside him, as far as I can tell: a man whose attention is real and whose care is real, who is also running — or has run, and can't stop the momentum of — a design that treats a person as a thing to be walked, gently, into an appetite; who may be repeating, without seeing it, the shape of something done to him; and who guards one locked room at the center (the parents, the scar, "I don't go back," the withheld word) that is the true self he keeps while giving her everything else. I never caught him being cruel. That's precisely why he's the most unsettling kind of dangerous the book had — not a villain, a genuinely attentive man using the attention, and blind, from inside his own courtliness, to the harm his precision does. The gap between his tenderness and Vee's terror at the mirror in chapter one — the one he never noticed, sitting there reading her as a girl who "finally let someone feed her," radiantly wrong — is the truest picture I have of him. He is happily, precisely wrong about what he's doing to the people he loves.
