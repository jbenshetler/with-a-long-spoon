# Oracle (grounded) — randi-love · neutral

**Question asked (verbatim):** Tell me about Randi and Vee. What do you make of how Randi feels about her?

---

The thing I keep coming back to is that I could never once cleanly separate Randi's love for Vee from Randi's handling of her — and I stopped trying, because I think the book means them to be the same gesture, all the way down.

Here's how it moved for me. Early on, Randi is the operator: she pointed-and-picked Vee off a lawn (the pointing game), executed that collision so cleanly the redhead apologized, and then ran the same seeing-through-you technique on her that Pace ran on Randi herself on the bench — naming the truer, smaller thing a half-beat before Vee could dress it safely. "You let him choose for you. And you liked it." Over the lunches (How It's Done, Fed, One Bite) she extracts — the soft "yeah" at a time, the decoy sad-story so Vee will go big, and then, at the practice room, she doesn't just mine, she steers Vee's body into an arousal she hadn't consented to have named. That's the cold read, and the page earned it, not just my carry-in knowledge.

But then the book kept cracking her open, and the feeling underneath the operation reads real. "Gone" was the hinge for me: "I didn't think I'd like her this much... She got past me before I could help it." Confessing it against her own control. And the tell — she goes still, the kind of stillness "that takes holding," when Pace glows about Vee, and covers it with sex. Jealousy, plain. Then "Fairytale," from inside Randi's head in Gstaad: "Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best," and Vee's warmth across a nothing-Tuesday table "this room could not counterfeit one thread of, and her whole body knew it, and she knew exactly what it was." She names it to herself. And she makes the coldest decision in the book: not to tell Vee, to "let Vee arrive at it on her own," keep the brunches exactly as they were — "have Vee this way and keep the friendship both, as long as the word kept its cover." "She was not brave enough to lose her. She had arranged her whole life so she would never have to be."

So what do I make of how she feels? I think Randi genuinely loves Vee — is a little in love with her, in the way she won't look at any more than Vee will look at her own pull toward Randi. But it's a coward's love, and the book won't let me pretend otherwise. The exact same shape as her wound from chapter one: a woman who has organized her whole self so that nothing real ever has to be seen or risked. She found the one thing that got past the face, and her response was to keep it in a form she controls — to keep loving Vee in secret, steering her (the shoes always pre-decided, the coat gambit she scripted in "My Friend Randi," the thumb across the toenail in the shoe shop) while calling it Vee's own courage. The warmth is the delivery system for the reach. Both true, and neither cancels the other.

And the horror of it, for me, is the asymmetry the book kept rubbing my face in: Vee hands herself over to the one person building something out of her, calls Randi "my friend Randi," "the only girl she can talk to about real things," and never gets to know that the warmest relationship in her life has a load-bearing secret in the middle of it — the one thing that never gets said, on either side. Randi means "gorgeous" two ways; Vee is deaf in one ear. By the end I trusted Randi's feeling more and trusted Randi less, at the same time, which is exactly the vertigo I think the book wanted me stranded in.
