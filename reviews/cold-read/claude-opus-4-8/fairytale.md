# Cold read — Fairytale

*scene: scenes/fairytale.md · read after: believe-me*

## Reader reaction

This is the chapter I've been waiting for without knowing it. We finally get inside Randi, and it undoes me a little.

The whole thing is a mirror held up to everything she does to Vee. Randi — who *produces* the fairytale, who conjures brunches and stories and reframes shame into gift — spends this chapter being *produced for*. John appears out of a lift line, cuts in with a con ("Play along... I'm with her"), and proceeds to conjure the whole approved fantasy: the ski day, the champagne, the white-tie ball, the flown-in chef, the sleigh with bells and grey horses, the high room with the black mountains in the window. He handles her exactly the way she handles Vee — and it touches the surface of her and goes no deeper. She's on the receiving end of her own method and she feels *nothing*. "It had been good... And it had meant no more than a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon: the pleasure real, complete, and gone before the plate was cleared." That's the coldest, truest line in the chapter, and it's about the best the world can offer her.

My feeling about Randi has *moved* hard. All book I've watched her be the operator — the one who never spends anything, un-sipped cup, makeup like skin, the extractor. Now I see the cost of it from inside, and it's not calculation, it's a kind of tragedy. She "ate her revulsion" hugging her father. She invents lovers at her parents' table because "the invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table." Her father tells her to keep an eye out for an eligible bachelor and she laughs and hopes it doesn't sound as brittle as it feels. This is a woman raised to be presented, who can perform the approved life flawlessly and cannot feel a single thread of it. The concealment I clocked as sinister in "The Outlier" — the reflex to bury — I now understand as the whole architecture of her. She hides the white-gold band from her mother's tennis-bracelet light. She doesn't mention central Florida. She's ashamed of something everywhere she goes.

And the reveal I half-suspected is now on the page in her own voice: **she's falling for Vee, and she knows it, and she's choosing not to tell her.** "Something came off Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday that this room could not counterfeit one thread of, and her whole body knew it, and she knew exactly what it was." That "nothing Tuesday" against all this Gstaad gold is the whole point. And then the decision, laid out with terrible clarity: she *could* tell her, over eggs, plainly, the way Vee says everything — but "she could not picture Vee's face while she said it." So she picks the other way: "Let Vee arrive at it on her own. Keep the brunches exactly as they were... as long as she never once said it out loud." That's the jacket's trap being *built*, in real time, by a woman who's decided that risking the truth would cost her the only real hour of her week. It's not villainy. It's cowardice made of love, which is worse, because I can't hate her for it.

The Pace material recolors him too, and after last chapter that surprises me. From inside Randi, Pace is the terror and the real thing both. His light lead — the thing she's always had to be good enough to *catch* — she's exhausted by tonight, and she tells herself "easier was better," letting John steer her around the marble in her sleep. But the chapter refutes her: the easy lead leaves her cold, and "Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best... Pace went straight to her. That was the whole terror of him, that he found the person she kept hidden and did not look away." So the wordless man I was coldest toward — the one who crossed his arms and wouldn't name Vee — is, in Randi's account, the one who actually goes underneath. It doesn't forgive him. But it complicates my read: his whole method is *reaching the hidden person*, and that's exactly what Randi both needs and can't survive being presented to her parents' table.

John I actually liked, which is part of the cruelty of it. He's charming, deft, the con on the lift line is genuinely funny ("we have three children, all sent off to ski camp"), he skis with everything in reserve and never once spends it proving he's better. He's *good*. And that's the horror: he's flawless and it's nothing. "There was no flaw in him to point to. The flaw, if that was the word for it, was hers."

**Trust vs. suspicion:** I trust this chapter completely as the truest window I've had. But it makes me trust *Randi-with-Vee* less than ever, because I've now watched her consciously choose the managed lie. That final text exchange is the machine laid bare — Vee sends real warmth from her warm Ohio kitchen ("i'm here if you want to talk. or not."), and Randi "gave her the fairytale... Every word of it true. None of it the thing." That's her whole doctrine in one gesture: technically-true, load-bearing-false. She even adds the one line she means and buries it in the performance.

**The timeline gives me friction / a real question.** Last I knew, Vee had walked out on Pace into the cold ("Believe Me"), devastated. Here it's New Year's Eve, and Vee's text to Randi is warm, stable, no crisis in it, no mention of Pace. Did they repair? Does Randi not know? Is Vee just not bringing that pain into a holiday text? I genuinely don't know, and I'm holding it as a gap, not a flaw — but I noticed it.

**Erotic charge:** deliberately anti-erotic, and it works *as* that. The sex is summarized, glancing — the one visceral image is the lipstick ring she finds on him afterward, "where she had taken him in her mouth and felt her own mouth do it from a long way off." Dissociation rendered as an image. There's no heat meant to land on me; the point is the emptiness, and the emptiness lands.

**The cold** rhymes straight across from Vee's last three chapters. Vee lay bare in the cold rather than take false comfort; Randi lies here "warm only where she touched him," and knows "the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers. She had brought it in with her and she would carry it back out." Both women carry their own cold. Pace's whole thing was warmth-that's-your-own-heat-given-back — and here Randi has only borrowed heat, along one seam, and knows the difference exactly.

**The title.** "Fairytale" is doing double, bitter work. It's the literal night — the sleigh and the ball and the dress bought for the story she'd tell a daughter she doesn't have. It's the word Vee uses ("it's honestly a fairytale out here") and the word Randi feeds back to her. And by the end it's the indictment: the fairytale is the approved, flawless, empty thing that touches only the surface, and the real thing is a nothing Tuesday across a cafeteria table. On reread the title will sting — Randi *gave* Vee the fairytale, the same as John gave it to her, and it means nothing precisely because it's perfect.

**The book title / tagline,** through this chapter: *With a Long Spoon* keeps meaning the tender-distance thing, but now I read it on Randi too — she sups with everyone at long-spoon length, close enough to be fed the approved life, never close enough to be reached, and she's built it that way on purpose so nothing can touch the hidden person. "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap" — tonight I watched *Randi's* yes to concealment be freely given, out of love, and it's a bar of the same cage closing on Vee.

**What I want / dread next:** I want the collision. Randi's decided to keep the brunches exactly as they are and let Vee arrive on her own — but Vee and Pace are in crisis, and Vee has no idea Randi is Pace's lover, no idea there was ever a game. *Guess from here:* the thing Randi's protecting is the thing that detonates her — the day Vee learns that her best friend and "Peter" are one secret. And I'm now desperate to know about that band: hidden from her mother, physically un-slidable off her wrist, unexplained for the whole book. Tonight it "caught on the heel of her hand, unable to be slid off." *Guess from here:* it's a claim she can't take off — and I'd bet on Pace before I'd bet on anyone.
