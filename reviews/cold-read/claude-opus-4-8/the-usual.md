# Cold read — The Usual

*scene: scenes/the-usual.md · read after: old-acquaintances*

## Reader reaction

Oh, this is the one I've been waiting for without knowing it. The Pace-side mirror. All this time the debriefs have been Vee and Randi over brunch, and I kept flagging in my own head *watch for a Pace-side mirror* — and here it is, the same architecture flipped: a booth, a plate, a friend who wants nothing off him, and the thing he can't do getting laid bare across the table. Except the friend across from him isn't an operator. She's Sheri, and she's the closest thing to Cassie the man has in his life, and she does to Pace exactly what I've been wanting someone to do since the door slammed on Vee: she calls it.

Let me start with what moved. **Pace.** I have spent the whole break holding him at arm's length — the wordless man, the hole the exact size of the word he won't spend — and this chapter is the first time I've been *inside* him at real range, not through Vee's ache. And he doesn't cheat. He doesn't come off nobler than I feared or worse. He comes off *stuck*, and honest about being stuck. "I told her she was the most important thing in my life. I said it, out loud, in words. And she looked at me like I'd handed her half of something." That line reframes the whole door-confrontation for me. I read that scene as Pace withholding out of some sealed damage; here I learn he thinks he *has* given, generously, brick on brick — "I could hear myself laying it up brick on brick and couldn't stop, because every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something." That's the tragedy in his own voice: he believes deeds are a currency that should *convert*, and it won't convert, and he doesn't understand why. It made me sympathize with him more than I have in twenty chapters and *also* confirmed the sadness of it.

But — and Sheri is the one who says it, which is why she's worth her weight — she won't let him hide in the reasons. "She didn't ask you for reasons." "So say it." "I can't." "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." That is the cleanest anyone in this book has ever put the knife exactly where it goes. And Pace *doesn't argue*. "There was nothing in it to argue with." So now I know: it isn't that he thinks he's right. He knows the price and pays it anyway, for a reason he won't or can't reach. The "I can't" is doing enormous work and the chapter is smart enough not to explain it. I don't get *why* he can't. That gap is the whole book, and I'm glad it stayed a gap.

**Sheri.** She was "loaded" in my ledger from her one prior appearance and now she's one of my favorite people in the book. Blunt, sallow under too much makeup worn "like she'd dare you to mention it," steals his fries, calls Vee "the one with the big tits" and Randi one of his "harem applicants." But under the ribbing she's carrying real grief — Paige, the design-school girl, the seven-at-the-table house Sheri *liked being in*, lost because Sheri won't talk to her own people and Paige couldn't stop picking at it. "Call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas." And then flat: "Ironic. Isn't it." She's another person carrying a sealed family wound, estranged, and the chapter lets Pace give her the one true thing he has — "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice" — and lets her deflect it: "Don't waste the good stuff on me. Save it for your redhead." These two are the same animal, both cut off from their people, both wanted-for-nothing by exactly one friend. That rhymes hard and I felt it.

The thing that gut-punched me: the diner *is the anti-Chili's.* The chapter opens him coming into "the warm griddle-smelling dark... grateful for that silence like a man come out of a crowded room into an empty one," and then goes straight to the memory — "the Chili's out by 460, screens on four walls... something in him had come apart very quietly over the hour... Her voice was down under all of it. He couldn't pull it clear, however hard he tried. Here he could have heard her whisper. He wondered whether she'd let him." That last line undid me a little. This whole book Vee has been trying to bring him into *her* world — the loud, lit, public, out-loud world — and it physically unmakes him. And here he sits in the one room where he could hear her whisper, wishing she were in it, knowing she might never even see the place. "Vee would either love this place or never see it at all, and he wanted very badly to know which." That's the tragedy of the two of them in a sentence: whether she can ever come to where he actually lives.

**Trust vs. suspicion.** No new suspicion of Pace — this chapter *lowered* my guard on him, if anything, by showing me he's not scheming, he's trapped. But it sharpened the dread. He says of Vee finding out about Randi: "No. Not like that." *Not like that.* Sheri asks "She find out about the brunette, then," and he says no — meaning the collision I've been braced for hasn't come, the fuse is still lit, and he *knows* it's there. And Sheri met Randi, got read as one more harem applicant, "never did learn my name" — that detail was in my ledger and here it is confirmed from the other side, Randi failing to see a person as beneath her notice. The gap between how these two women move through the world could not be wider, and Pace is standing in it.

The one thing I'll gently flag as the author's thumb — the food thread payoff. "She won't just tell me it tastes good — she gets what it's for. The whole idea under it. The philosophy of it." I've been carrying the taste-thread a long time and I *wanted* this, but hearing Pace say "the philosophy of it" out loud edged toward self-serious, and the chapter clearly knew it, because Sheri immediately punctures him: "The philosophy of your food. Lord almighty. Get over yourself." That deflation *rescued* it — turned a line that might have been the book patting its own motif into a laugh, "the first full one of the night." Good. That's the chapter policing its own preciousness, and it earned the warmth of the ending.

**Erotic charge:** low and that's right — this is a talk scene. But there's a live warmth in how Sheri drags the sex out of him against his will. "You mean the sex is good." / "That's not what I meant. But, yes. It is." / "Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you." His face going hot and going on anyway — "The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles." That's a man in love being ribbed into confessing it, and it's charming rather than hot. Peak isn't heat, it's tenderness under fire.

**The title — "The Usual."** It lands on the waitress: "The usual?... because there was no question and never had been, and they told her yes like people saying yes to their own names." That's the surface — Pace and Sheri are regulars, they have a standing order. But reread across the chapter it colors darker and warmer at once: *the usual* is everyone-wants-something-off-him and he pays it out without letting the cost show — and Sheri is the exception, the one un-usual thing, the person who wants none of it. And "somebody always plays it" — the Elvis Christmas song someone always drops a coin for, pressing the bruise — that's *usual* too, the recurring wound you can count on. And I think, quietly, there's a sting: Vee wants him to say the word, and *not saying it* is Pace's usual, his standing order, the thing that's true every time. The title's innocuous on the menu and aches on the reread. It didn't give anything away; it deepened.

**The book's title and tagline** sit where they've sat. "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap." This chapter widened it for me — because here's Pace, who genuinely believes he's given everything, freely, every brick true, and it's a trap for *him* too. He's got the girl and can't keep her and can't say why. The tender devil, fed just enough, isn't only doing it to Vee; he's caught in the same spoon. That's the first time I've felt the trap close on *Pace* as a victim of his own mechanism and not just its engineer.

**What I want / dread next.** I want the collision now more than ever — "No. Not like that" is a lit fuse and I felt the length of it. I dread it for Vee. *Guess from here:* term starts, they reunite because they can't not, and nothing is actually resolved — he still can't say it, she still needs it, and the Randi truth is sitting under the table the whole time getting closer. And I keep circling the "I can't" — I still don't know if it's the parents-wound, a promise, or something about Randi I can't see yet. Sheri, who sees clean, couldn't get it out of him. That gap is the engine now.

**Symbolism:** The diner as the anti-Chili's — the one room where Pace can hear, the physical shape of the world he can actually live in, set against Vee's loud-lit-public world. The "usual" order = the standing, unquestioned self, and by extension his not-saying as his standing order. The fogged glass = the sealed warm interior against the cold, kept where it belongs. The mis-set single/double plates (the kid's "grave confidence of arithmetic") — a small joke, but a math prodigy watching arithmetic get the portions wrong lands lightly as symbol of the world's confident wrong measurements.

**Characterization:** Pace deepened more than in any single chapter — first sustained interior access, and it holds him consistent (deeds-as-currency, "I can't," the true things "supposed to come to something") while adding the crucial new note that he *believes he's given generously* and doesn't understand the failure. Sheri deepened hugely: from loaded walk-on to a fully drawn person with her own grief, her own estrangement, her own lost love — and the book's clearest truth-teller. Nobody flattened.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Excellent control. The talk-around-nothing (California, Berkeley, the cover band) earns the descent into the real thing; the two grief-turns (Sheri's, then Pace's) are paced so neither rushes; the food-philosophy deflation lands the ending on a laugh instead of a lecture. Nothing dragged.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** Big momentum shift and a welcome one. After two solitary New Year's chapters (Randi in Gstaad, Vee alone in Ohio), this is the third principal alone with a confidant — completing the set and finally giving me Pace's interior. It builds by *deepening* rather than plot-advancing; the fuse ("No. Not like that") is explicitly still unlit, so I read this as the last held breath before term forces the collision. Doesn't sag — but I now badly need the next chapter to *move* the situation, not just illuminate it again.
