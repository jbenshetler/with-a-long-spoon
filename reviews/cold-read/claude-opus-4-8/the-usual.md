# Cold read — The Usual

## Reader reaction

This is the first time I've been all the way inside Pace's head for a whole chapter, and the effect is disorienting in the way I think it's meant to be: I *like* him here. He comes into this griddle-smelling double-wide diner "grateful for that silence like a man come out of a crowded room," he laughs the first full laugh of the book, he blushes ("His face was hot and he knew it, and he went on anyway"), and he says a genuinely lovely thing about Vee — "The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles." After a whole book of watching him operate from the outside, being handed his fondness this plainly is seductive, and I clock that: the apparatus is now working on *me*.

But the chapter is smart enough to undercut him with his own friend. Sheri does the thing nobody in the book has done to his face — she calls it. "Not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." She's exactly right, and the beautiful cruelty of it is that Pace *can't argue*: "There was nothing in it to argue with." He lays up reasons — I'm there every weekend, weeknights were hers, I told her she was the most important thing in my life — "brick on brick," and Sheri knocks the whole wall down: "She didn't ask you for reasons." That's the withheld word from the far side of the table, and even the person who loves him with no stake in it tells him it's a failure of his, not of hers. So the humanizing doesn't exonerate him; it isolates the cruelty and puts a spotlight on it.

The big move of this chapter, though, is **Sheri**. I came in carrying a hard suspicion — "Pace's placed eyes on Vee," a domesticated menace fed at his Thanksgiving table. This chapter dismantles that, and I have to be honest that it earned the correction. This Sheri doesn't even know Randi's *name* ("Never did learn my name. But I got to see her"), got "maybe four seconds" of Vee at the bar, calls her "the redhead" and "the one with the big tits" — she is not surveilling anyone. She's a country girl estranged from her family, gay (Paige, the design-school junior who made bird prints, lost after "a couple months" because Paige couldn't get right with Sheri not talking to her people), grieving at Christmas, going to school part-time now "that there was nobody behind her writing checks." She wants nothing off Pace — "She didn't want him, she wasn't impressed by him, and she'd say so to his face." That's not an operator. That reads as the one clean, un-transactional relationship in his life. Which means "She's alone" at Thanksgiving was *literally true*, and the sinister gloss was Vee's paranoia — and mine, running downhill after it. I'm revising: the menace I attached to Sheri was projection. (I hold a sliver of caution only because this is Pace's POV and this book crops even inside a head — but nothing on the page supports Sheri as anything but real.)

What his POV *doesn't* touch is telling too. In his own head, there's no "harvest," no co-conspiracy, no Randi-as-lover — Randi is just "the brunette... the sorority one" he brought dancing, and the confirmed fact is only that there are two women and "She find out about the brunette, then? / No." A man not thinking, in his own interior, about the machine I believe he runs — that's the cropping motif turned on the operator himself. And it complicates the withheld word: from Vee's side it read as strategy, a one-way valve; from inside here it reads as something he genuinely "can't" do, that costs him. I get to hold both now, and I'm not sure which is truer.

One line itches: the opening — "he would not bring her down today. Not this week, not this time of year. Whatever he'd carried in from the truck could keep." *Bring her down* where, and *what* did he carry in from the truck? I read it as choosing not to unload his own trouble onto Sheri at Christmas — considerate — but the phrasing sits ominous and unexplained, and I can't fully settle it.

The Chili's callback recolors nicely: from Vee's chapter it was "he can't hear you"; from here, the loud room literally came apart on him, "her voice down under all of it where he couldn't pull it clear... Here he could have heard her whisper. He wondered whether she'd let him." Tender, or a man who needs the room where he controls the acoustics — again, both.

Erotic charge is low and by-report — Sheri's ribbing ("Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you"), "you mean the sex is good / ...yes. It is." Charged, not hot.

**The title — "The Usual"** — is the waitress's "The usual?", "no question and never had been, and they told her yes like people saying yes to their own names." It's the ritual, the one stable known thing, and it lands as the anti-Vee: with Sheri nothing crackles and nothing costs, and *that's* the point — it's his rest, the un-trapped relationship, the control case. Against the book's tagline, "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap," this chapter shows a yes that's just a yes — small, warm, free, and *not* a trap. Which throws the Vee machinery into sharper relief. The **long spoon** is warmth borrowed across false distance; Sheri needs no spoon at all. This whole chapter is what Pace looks like when he isn't operating, and it makes the operating worse.

What I want next: Vee's side, term resuming, the collision — and I dread the Randi consolation arriving wearing a lover's face. I'm newly curious whether Sheri stays clean (I lean yes) and what came in on that truck.
