## Reader reaction

# Felt read

So this is Pace's Christmas week — the blank I couldn't see into from Vee's side. And it's a diner. A double-wide on a county road, griddle-smelling dark, and it's the first time in a while I've felt actually *fond* of Pace instead of wary of him. That's a real shift, and I want to be careful about it.

The opening line — "he would not bring her down today" — landed odd before I understood it. "Bring her down" reads at first like *bring her here*, then resolves into something heavier: he's carrying something in from the truck (grief, by the end — the Christmas song, "somebody always plays it"), and he's decided it can keep. "He had long practice at deciding a thing could keep." That's the same man who decides *love* can keep, who withholds the word. So even in a warm chapter the machinery of him is on display: he manages his own interior the way he manages everyone else's. I clocked it.

**Sheri** is a genuine pleasure. I've had her as a mentioned-only splinter — "she's alone," the name Vee weaponized into a fear — and meeting her in person completely defuses that fear and replaces it with something better. She's five feet of motion, two-tone permed hair, too much makeup worn like a dare, and she is *gay* — "There was somebody. Paige. Junior in the design school." Estranged from her family, working part-time now "that there was nobody behind her writing checks." The one person who wants nothing off Pace. "She didn't want him, she wasn't impressed by him, and she'd say so to his face." That's exactly why the scene works: she's the only mirror in the book that doesn't flatter him, and she calls him on the thing I've been calling him on.

Because here's the gift of this chapter — **Sheri says the quiet part.** Pace lays out his defense brick by brick ("every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something"), and Sheri knocks it flat: "that's a hell of a lot of reasons for a man who's already got the girl. She didn't ask you for reasons." And then the kill shot: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." *He didn't argue. There was nothing in it to argue with.* That's the most honest anyone's been about Pace's silence, including Pace. And it deepens my open question — WHY won't he say it — because even *he* can't produce the reason to Sheri, the one person he doesn't perform for. My standing suspicion is that the word belongs to Randi, that saying it to Vee breaks the game. Sheri, of course, has no idea there's a game. She thinks it's just a stubborn man.

The dramatic irony is thick and I felt it in my chest: Sheri asks about "the brunette that you brought dancing before Thanksgiving — the sorority one," and about "your redhead," and treats them as two separate girls in a rotating cast ("harem applicants"). She has no idea the redhead and the brunette are the *same* engine. Neither, really, does the reader get told outright — but I know Randi is the brunette-behind-Vee, so Sheri innocently sitting there ranking them lands as almost unbearable. "She find out about the brunette, then." "No. Not like that." The brunette is Randi. Sheri thinks it's an affair to be discovered. It's the co-author of Vee's whole life.

The erotic charge here is low and I think rightly — this isn't a heat scene, it's a confession scene. But when it comes it's *good*, because it's Pace trying to name why Vee: "The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles." Sheri deflates it instantly: "You mean the sex is good." And he holds both — "That's not what I meant. But, yes. It is." Then the thing that actually moved me: "She sees things nobody else does. I'll cook her something and she won't just tell me it tastes good — she gets what it's for. The whole idea under it. The philosophy of it." And Sheri: "The philosophy of your food. Lord almighty. Get over yourself." That's the best laugh in the book so far and I laughed with them. It also quietly confirms Vee's read on him is *real* — the thing she gives him is real, whatever the origin sin of how she was chosen.

**Trust vs. suspicion.** Nothing new feels *off* in Sheri — she reads completely clean, which is itself notable in a book where warmth keeps turning out to be method. She's the one warm person with no angle. And that makes Pace's problem starker, not softer: with the one person he can't manage, he still can't say the word, and can't say why.

**Friction.** Very little. The prose is doing that thing I like — the burger eaten in real sensory time, the fog on the glass keeping the cold where it belongs. If I reach: "she just liked him and gave him hell, and that was all" slightly over-explains a Sheri the dialogue had already made me feel. And "every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something" is a touch author-visible as a thesis line — but it's close enough to how this man actually thinks that I let it stand.

One ache: "he wanted very badly to know which" — whether Vee would love this place or never see it. That's him missing her, real and simple, in a chapter where she's a wound he decided could keep. I believe the missing. I still don't trust what he'll *do* with it.

**The title — "The Usual."** On the page it's the waitress's line: "The usual?... because there was no question and never had been, and they told her yes like people saying yes to their own names." Innocent, warm — the comfort of a standing order. But it colors on a second beat: the *usual* is exactly Pace's problem. He runs on the usual, the settled arrangement, the thing that can keep. Vee is asking him to break the usual and say something new, and he can't. And "saying yes to their own names" — in a book obsessed with withheld names — quietly stings. Also: Sheri says "Somebody always plays it" of the Christmas song. The usual grief, on schedule. The title's warmer than most in this book and I don't think it gave anything away; it sits nicely oblique.

**Book title / tagline.** No fresh pull on *With a Long Spoon* here — Sheri's the one person at the table not supping with any devil. "Every yes was freely given" — the waitress's yes, the standing order, yeses "like people saying yes to their own names." The tagline keeps whispering that the trouble is in what's freely and comfortably agreed to.

**What I want next.** I want the term to start and the worlds to touch — that's still the fuse. I want to know if Pace does anything with the missing, or lets it keep until it's too late. Guess from here: Sheri's "you're coming to the Brooks and Dunn thing, first Saturday in February, and I don't care what the redhead's got to say" is a planted date — I'd bet that dance, or the fact of Sheri at all, becomes a place two of these worlds brush. And I'm now *more* curious, not less, about why Pace can't say it, because this chapter proved it isn't cowardice-as-usual — even stripped of all performance, in front of the one person he can't fool, the word won't come. Something's holding it. My money's still on Randi.

**How I feel**
Vee: deep tenderness and fear — frightened for a girl walking toward Randi as refuge. Pace: **moved up a notch toward sympathy without dropping the distrust** — this chapter humanized him (missing her, laughing, flushing, unable to fool Sheri) while making his central withholding *worse*, because it's now shown to be reasonless even to himself. His silence isn't cowardice-as-usual; something is holding the word, and I still think it's Randi/the game. Randi: still tragic-and-dangerous; Sheri's innocent ranking of her as "the brunette" sharpened my dread. Sheri: warm relief — the one clean, unmanaged person in the book; I like her a lot and trust her completely. Overall mood: a warm, funny, honest chapter between two colder ones — but the fuse (term's start; maybe Sheri's Feb dance) is lit and the worlds still haven't touched. Braced and, for once, briefly charmed.
