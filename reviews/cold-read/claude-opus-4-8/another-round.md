# Cold read (grounded) — Another Round

*scene: scenes/another-round.md · model: claude-opus-4-8 · memory: ck-ch050 + raw ch051..ch051 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

FELT READ

Oh, this one just held me the whole way, and it held me warm. I came into it braced — that's the honest thing. The chapter opens with the exact domestic geometry of the one before it (Vee at the little table with the end of her wine, Pace at the sink, his back "listening"), and I settled into that rhythm gladly, and then he says "Sheri," and my stomach did the drop Vee's did. Because I know something Vee has half-decided she knows: I've been told, from the reader's seat, that Sheri is only a friend, that the "blonde model" rival is Vee's 3 a.m. invention. So I got to watch Vee walk into a fear I already knew was hollow, and instead of that making the chapter slack, it made it almost unbearably tender — I wasn't afraid *for* her, I was aching over how much armor she was going to strap on for nothing.

And the strapping-on is gorgeous and a little heartbreaking. "She dressed for the woman her jealousy had built, and she knew it while she was doing it, and she did it anyway." Meg's black keyhole shirt "that on Vee had stopped suggesting," the borrowed too-short skirt, and then — this landed hard — the four-inch induction heels Randi buckled onto her on a boutique floor, worn into a January taproom against a woman she invented. The heels being reached for "past" the sensible boots is the whole shame-and-armor engine of this book in one gesture. I felt the cold coming off that skirt. I felt how exposed she'd made herself, "armed sole to collar against a woman she had invented."

Then Sheri turns around and is five foot nothing, two-tone perm, "a round face under too much makeup, a little off, worn like she'd dare you to mention it," and "the blonde didn't die so much as fail to find anywhere to stand." That line delighted me. And the real relief of the chapter isn't that the rival is unthreatening — it's that "the heat in Vee's chest stayed exactly where it was. It had never needed the blonde, it turned out." The book is being honest that Vee's jealousy was never really about a face; it was about not having the word, not having the standing. Sheri's body was never the point.

The look. God, the look. Sheri's slow appraisal down Vee's body that she braces for as "the pricing look, the placing look" — the female ranking-glance she's been fluent in since high school — and it isn't that at all. "It was *enjoying* her." The mouth going to a "soundless, appreciative *oh*." And Vee's laugh coming out "before she'd voted on it," a month of dread leaving in one breath. That's the swoon of the chapter for me, and it's a swoon between women, and it's completely disarming because it's admiring and generous rather than predatory. Then Sheri to Pace: "And you get to motorboat them things?" — and *Pace blushes.* Pace, whom Vee has never once seen embarrassed, the color climbing past anything deniable. "The single most disarming thing she'd watched him do." I grinned. It humanizes him in a way the whole careful-courtly register had been keeping just out of reach, and it made me trust him a notch more, honestly — a man who can be caught out, who has a friend allowed to catch him out.

Sheri is the gift of this chapter. She reads instantly, wholly, as a real person — loud, crooked-lipsticked, "not one uncalibrated thing about what Sheri chose to say and when." The two disclosures she hands Vee did real work on me. First the dance-hall report, delivered "without heat, a woman reporting soil conditions": every man on that floor "making a down payment," and Pace the one who "dances because the dancing is the point... Hands stay where the dance puts them. Every time." That's the outside witness the book has been withholding — someone who isn't in love with Pace telling Vee (and me) that his restraint is real and consistent, not a trick aimed at her. I wanted that for Vee badly and I got it.

And then the coming-out story. "Whole tablecloth come off the table and nobody caught a dish." Marine Corps father, brother, church, Bible study, gone inside a month. Crying in the middle of a two-step. And Pace drove her home, no questions, showed up with coffee and donuts, fixed the lock, stopped the faucet, invited her cycling. "He never handed me one false word — no *they'll come around*, no *their loss*." That undid me a little. It rhymes so exactly with what Vee needs from him and can't get — the word he won't spend on her — that it recolors his withholding: here's a man who refuses cheap words on principle, who was "there" for someone when everyone who "owed her words weren't spending any." "Words are cheap. Believe what I do" is being shown to me from a second angle now, and from Sheri's it looks less like a man too proud to say it and more like a man who has watched cheap words fail people. I don't think that settles it — Vee still needs the four words, and the book knows she does — but it opened the question wider rather than closing it, and I appreciate that it left it ajar.

And the tucked-in line: "Went bad with his daddy. He hasn't been home since he was sixteen." Delivered flat, "a silence she didn't decorate." That's the estrangement I've been uneasy about, and Sheri hands it over without explaining it, which is exactly right — it stays a wound, not a diagnosis.

Trust and suspicion: here's where I have to be honest about the reader's double vision. Everything on this page is warm and I felt the warmth fully — but I also sat the whole time knowing Sheri knows things Vee doesn't. Sheri knows Pace is seeing two women. Sheri is the confidante pushing him to "say it." So when Sheri says "You've got him happy. That's the whole book, far as I'm concerned," I heard the second floor under it — *she means it, and she also knows the part of the book Vee hasn't read.* That's not the text putting a thumb on the scale; that's just my carried knowledge sitting quietly under a scene that plays, on its own terms, as pure kindness. And I want to be fair: Sheri herself never once tips it. There's no wink. Her generosity to Vee reads as real. If anything, Sheri liking Vee this much makes the coming reckoning worse, because it means one more genuine bond that was seeded on a rigged foundation.

The one small stinger the chapter allows itself is honest, too: Vee's flash on the mountain road, "he'd been shivering that whole time and hadn't said one word" — and here, "He knows how it goes, is the thing." The theme of Pace absorbing cold, going without, for the people he keeps, and never mentioning it. "A man standing his headlights on a door until the light came on... and had never once mentioned." I love that the chapter ends not on heat but on Vee watching his hands take them home, "ten and two, steady as a table — like something full he was carrying, and had no intention of spilling." That's the tenderest close the book's given me in a while.

Erotic charge: this chapter is low-heat by design and I didn't miss it — there's no sex, and there shouldn't be; the previous chapter spent its whole body on the truck. The charge here is social and it's the taproom look, the blush, the induction heels on the wrong floor. Simmering, clothed, exactly right.

Friction: almost none. The closest thing to a thumb on the scale is how thoroughly the chapter dismantles the blonde rival — it's a slightly tidy demolition, the "blonde didn't die so much as fail to find anywhere to stand" doing in one image what a jealous reader might've wanted stretched. But it earned it, because the book was honest months ago that Vee built the blonde herself, so watching her deflate isn't a cheat, it's a reckoning Vee owes herself. I bought it.

The titles: "Another Round" lands three ways for me now, and I like it. It's the literal round of drinks Sheri keeps commandeering; it's the second social introduction in two chapters ("Among Friends" was the roadhouse, this is the next round of meeting Vee's — no, *Pace's* — people); and there's the ghost of "A Round," the fitting chapter, in it, the sense of being measured — Sheri's slow look down Vee's body is a kind of fitting, a taking of measure that comes back approving. Innocuous on its surface, a bar phrase; charged underneath. As for the book: *A Polite Invitation*, *With a Long Spoon*, and the tagline "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — that spoon proverb ("sup with the devil, use a long spoon") keeps its cold hum under a chapter this warm, and that's the unease I carry into every scene now, not one this chapter planted. "A Polite Invitation" — Sheri's whole friendship with Pace began as one kind of invitation (the porch, the coffee, the bike on Sundays); Vee's began as another kind she doesn't know was extended. The jacket's "the invitation reached her long before she knew there was one" sat right under the butcher-paper VEE of last chapter and under Sheri's "I know who you are" here.

What I want next: I want the Brooks-and-Dunn dance hall, badly — Sheri booked it on the page ("Saturday, both of you, and leave them shoes home"), and I want to watch Pace dance, and watch Vee watch him. Guess from here, marked as a guess: the dance-hall floor, where Sheri says every man is "making a down payment," is exactly the kind of set this book uses to turn a sweet thing complicated, and Vee dancing in that room, or watching him dance a stranger "like she was the only thing happening in the state," could bring the standing-and-word ache right back up. And underneath all of it I'm still waiting, the way I've been waiting since page one, for Vee to learn what I know — Sheri is now one more person in the circle who knows more than Vee does, and the more people who love her inside the secret, the harder the floor's going to give when it gives.

Momentum holding, warmth accumulating, with the reader's dread quietly compounding under it.
