**Felt read**

This one ached. It's the mirror of "Fairytale" — the same two texts, the same midnight, but seen from the other twin bed — and reading them back to back did something to me that neither chapter would have done alone. In Gstaad I watched Randi build a fairytale out of lipstick and lies and feel nothing under it; here I watch Vee lie in the cold and *believe every word of it*, and want it, and measure her own life against a snowscape she knows is bought with fraud. "She'd been in the glass room when the name came up and Randi blanched; she knew whose money it was. And she envied the mountains anyway." That's the whole terrible engine of the book in one sentence — Vee sees the poison and reaches for the fruit anyway, because "even a poisoned Gstaad was more life than she had." I felt the dramatic irony land like a weight: I know the fairytale was a woman going cold on a stranger and lying to Vee's face by text, and Vee is envying it from the floor of her outgrown childhood.

Vee absolutely gutted me this chapter, and my feeling for her deepened. The room that "loves a girl who no longer lived inside her" — the ribbons, the frozen little gold swimmers, the clarinet — is the most precise image of outgrowing your own origin I've read in a while. She's not sneering at home; she's grieving that it no longer fits, and grieving that the thing that *does* fit her has no name and may already be over. The cropped photo returns and cuts deeper here: "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it." She scissored her own leg off before her parents could see what the dress does. That's the shame-voice doing its quiet work even in a chapter where Pace never appears.

The part that most swept me up — and hurt most — was the fantasy that betrays her. She reaches for her own best night (the fra diavolo, the falling sheet) "to be warm," and it *turns* on her mid-memory: the hair goes gold, the tan leg slides into her seat, and she can't keep her own night. "She could not keep her own night." God. That's jealousy rendered from the inside with no self-pity, and it's more erotic and more painful than any explicit scene could be, precisely because it's her own remembered pleasure being repossessed by fear.

Trust and suspicion: I have no *new* suspicion of anyone here — this is Vee alone — but the chapter sharpens the dread I already carry. She reaches for the reassurances (the bartender, Randi's "he leaves you the room") and they "went straight through her hands," because "they had only ever held her up while she was the one." And *I* know things she doesn't that would make it worse and better at once: that Randi's fairytale is hollow, that Randi is half in love with her. The line "Randi, the year's gift she had literally stumbled into" is a knife, because I know she didn't stumble into anything. And her invented rival — "blonde, tan, a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's" — is her mother's shame-voice building a woman out of spite. She's tormenting herself with a fiction while a truer, stranger threat (Randi) writes her a heart emoji.

Erotic charge is low and that's right — this is longing, not heat. Where it flickers is the kitchen: the garlic in the oil, "the same building, then giving, that his hands worked on her in the dark." The food-as-sex equivalence is now so established that "he was doing it for someone, and she didn't get to know who" reads as sexual jealousy even though it's a stove.

Friction: almost none. The chapter risks being static — a girl in a bed for forty minutes checking a phone — but the interior movement earns the stillness completely. If I reach, the paired repetition of the two identical texts is a device you *feel* as a device once you've read both chapters; but here it works for me rather than against, because the gap between sender's truth and receiver's reading is the point.

The titles: "Old Acquaintances" — after "Fairytale," the "Auld Lang Syne" echo is unmistakable, should old acquaintance be forgot. And it recolors sharply: the "old acquaintances" here are the *people Vee used to be* — Vivienne with her ribbons, the girls who "hadn't texted in a year" — being forgotten, and also the terror that *she* might be the acquaintance Pace forgets over a silent holiday. It points at loss and turning-over. The volume line, **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION**, keeps whispering "he who sups with the devil needs a long spoon" — and after Gstaad, where Randi supped with a devil of a different kind and Vee envies the meal, that proverb feels like it's tightening around both women. "A polite invitation" reads more ominous every chapter: everything in this book arrives as courtesy.

What I want next: I want the term to start. Vee handed me a calendar — "She'd have Randi back the day the term started" and "nothing at all for the other." I dread and crave the reunion with Randi (who is carrying a secret desire and a secret Vee saw), and I'm bracing for whether Pace's silence means it's over or means he's just "leaving the room" again. *Guess from here:* the reunion won't resolve the name question; it'll deepen the Vee–Randi pull instead, and Vee's invented blonde rival will turn out to be a projection while the real reconfiguration comes from Randi.

**Symbolism:** The bedroom that "loves a girl who no longer lived inside her" — outgrowing your origin, and the frozen gold swimmers "mid-lift" as the self preserved in amber. The self-cropped photo as self-censorship/shame made literal. Borrowed warmth (both girls warm only where they touch someone else) as the book's diagnosis of both women.

**Characterization:** Vee deepened powerfully — her jealousy, her clear-eyed envy of a fairytale she knows is rotten, her inability to keep her own memory, all consistent with the woman who can't name what she has. No one else is present, but the chapter retroactively deepens Randi through contrast: we alone hold both texts.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Slow and static by design; a single held breath of a chapter. It earns its length through interior escalation — the phone, the photos, the hijacked fantasy, the failed reassurances — never repeating a beat.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** Holding, deliberately — this is the trough after "Under the Rug"/"Believe Me," the low point of Vee's uncertainty, twinned against "Fairytale." Momentum is emotional rather than plot, and the paired-midnight structure justifies the stillness; but two near-eventless interior chapters (this and "Seconds") in the recent run means I'm now hungry for the term to start and the people to be in a room together.
