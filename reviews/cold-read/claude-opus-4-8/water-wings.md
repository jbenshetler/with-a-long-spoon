## Reader reaction

# Felt read

Oh, this one landed on me sideways. After six chapters of the golden hunt closing in, this chapter just... steps out of it. Pace isn't here. Randi isn't here. It's Vee and Cassie at the rec pool, and it's the first time in the whole book I've felt Vee alone with someone who wants *nothing* from her. And that absence of wanting is the whole point — "Nobody down here wanted anything." I could exhale for a full page. I didn't realize how braced I'd been until this let me down.

**Cassie.** God, I love her more now, and I already loved her. She's the anti-Randi in every line and the chapter knows it. Randi's currency is being looked at; Cassie "did not go to a pool to be admired... Nobody was going to look at Cassie in it. Cassie knew that, and it didn't bother her one bit." Randi listens to draw out shame and hand it back; Cassie "never asked — asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger." Where Randi would have *tended* the sad moment — leaned in, made it intimate, made herself necessary to it — Cassie flicks a low sheet of water across the gap and lets the splashing win. That's the tenderness that doesn't take anything. And the moment that got me hardest: the catcalls in the bleachers, "You won't need floaties with *those!*", and Cassie *could* have pitched a comeback up into the stands — "she had the lungs" — but "She'd kept it for Vee." "They will never know the love of a woman." She keeps her fire pointed at her friend, not the audience. After all the *performed* care I've been watching, this is the real article, and the contrast is deafening.

**Vee.** This is the most I've understood her body-shame from the inside. The green-and-white suit from synchronized swimming at sixteen, "when there'd been less of her to fill it. There was more of her now. That was the trouble with Vee's suit: it fit, and it gave her nowhere to hide." And then the walk down the bleachers — "she took it like an entrance," the game face, the catcalls "banked," the practiced long blink and eyeroll. She's *so good* at being looked at, at armoring it. And then the water: "She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem, and the water was the one part of it that had never been taken." That line broke something open for me. *Before she had a body to be a problem.* The pool is the one place the body isn't display, isn't currency, isn't a thing men shout about or mothers police — it's just buoyancy. "The body that was such a problem stayed up on the deck with the coiled towel, where it could wait." That's a heartbreaking image: her own body as a coat she gets to take off in the only room where nobody's watching.

And that little grief underwater — "grieved, a little, for something she didn't let herself name." I don't know what she's grieving and neither does she, and the chapter has the discipline not to tell me. Maybe it's the sixteen-year-old body. Maybe it's some dawning sense that the golden new life closing around her costs something. I can't name it either, and that's right.

**Trust vs. suspicion.** Nothing to distrust *here* — Cassie is the one clean well in the book and this chapter is her proof. But the very cleanliness of it made me *more* uneasy about everything else, because it's a control sample. This is what care with no agenda feels like. Now I have the reference point, and it makes Randi's warmth and Pace's "may I" look even more like instruments. The book handed me a real thing so I'd feel the counterfeit.

**Erotic charge.** Deliberately none — 0, and I don't hold it against the chapter. This is a breather, and the only sensuality is the water itself: weightlessness, the cold from below, light "in slow sheets," the slow scull "that keeps you down and turned and yours." It's sensual about *belonging to yourself*, which is the opposite of everything the hunters offer.

**Friction.** Almost none. The prose is doing the "unhurried" thing again — "close and unhurried," "lazy as a summer breeze," "unhurried" of the towel — and I've now clocked "unhurried" as a real tic across the book; it appears three-ish times in this short chapter alone. It's Pace's whole register too ("no-hurry house"), so I half-wonder if it's deliberate contamination, the calm creeping into the one safe place — but honestly it read to me as the author reaching for the same comfortable word. Tiny thumb. Everything else earned its keep.

**This chapter's title — "Water Wings."** Before reading, meaningless; a kiddie float. After: it's the catcall — "You won't need floaties with *those!*" — the crude joke about her breasts as flotation. But the chapter *takes the phrase back*: in the actual water she needs nothing to hold her, "nothing holding her and nothing to hold," she's the practiced synchronized swimmer sculling herself down. The title starts as a leer and ends as her mastery. That's a nice quiet judo — the men name her body as a joke; the water knows her as competent and free. It didn't give anything away; it recolored on the finish.

**The book's title and tagline.** And here's the jolt: the tagline text is *literally embedded* in this chapter. "it fit, and it gave her nowhere to hide" — "That was the trouble." The cadence of "That was the trap" is right there, transposed onto the swimsuit. The trap in the jacket is the freely-given yes; here the "trouble" is a suit that *fits*, that reveals. It made me feel the two traps rhyme — being seen as a body is the trap the world already set for her, long before Pace and Randi, and the pool is her one exemption from it. *With a Long Spoon* still sits oblique for me — the proverb about supping with the devil — but this chapter sharpened what's at stake: there's a self in Vee that predates the body-problem, and I now dread watching it get spent.

**What I want / dread next.** I want more Cassie, always. I dread the return to the golden machine, which now feels colder for this warm interval. Guess from here: this chapter is the "before" — the last clear look at Vee whole and unwatched — set right here so I'll feel the loss when the wanting closes back over her. And the unnamed grief underwater feels like a seed; I suspect I'll understand what she was grieving only much later, in retrospect.
