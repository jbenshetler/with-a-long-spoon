# Cold read (grounded) — Water Wings

## Reader reaction

### The felt read

After the long, patient machinery of the last few chapters — the bench, the pointing game, the froyo — this landed on me like a held breath let out. It's short, it's low-stakes on the surface, and it undid me more than any of the sex has. I mean that. I came out of it with a full chest.

This is a Vee-and-Cassie chapter, and it's the first time I've felt I was *inside* Vee's body rather than watching her be looked at. The whole chapter is organized around one brutal little sentence: "That was the trouble with Vee's suit: it fit, and it gave her nowhere to hide." Everything the seduction plot has been doing *to* her — being seen, being wanted, being the most of what a room is selling — gets stripped down here to its origin cost. She rolls the towel tight and carries it "down at her side — not up against her," puts her face on, walks the bleachers "like an entrance," and I understood exactly what that armor was and what it cost to hold. The catcalls ("You won't need floaties with *those*!"; "Legs for *days*") are the same gaze the men in this book keep aiming at her, only made ugly and public and stripped of the courtliness Pace dresses it in. Reading them right after Pace's froyo tenderness was clarifying: this is the water that gaze usually comes in.

And then the water — the actual water — and the chapter opens all the way up. The passage where she goes under and "the room switched off" is the most purely beautiful writing I've hit so far, and it's beautiful *because* it's the one place in the book where nobody wants anything from her. "Nobody up there could see her. Nobody down here wanted anything." "She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem, and the water was the one part of it that had never been taken." That last clause — *never been taken* — put a lump in my throat, because the whole book has been teaching me, gently, warmly, to enjoy watching things be taken from her. This chapter quietly names the thing under all of it: a body that became a problem, an appetite trimmed to nothing, a self that lives behind a face. I already knew this about Randi from the bench — the face, the not-crying, the portioning. Finding the same architecture in Vee, from the *inside*, and finding it tender instead of clinical, moved my whole sense of her. She isn't just the glad girl walking toward Pace. She's someone who grieves, underwater, where it's safe, "a little," and comes up "with her face on wrong."

Cassie. Oh, Cassie. I have been watching her from the corner of my eye for two chapters — the flat "Cassie," the "Goodbye, Vee" that landed like a stair that wasn't there, the way she reads the room's looks without a flicker — and this chapter is the first time I got to love her outright, and I do. What she does here is the opposite of everything Randi and Pace do: she *doesn't ask.* "Cassie never asked — asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger." She could pitch the comeback up into the bleachers — "She had the lungs" — and she keeps it low, "for Vee." And the splash: she reads that her friend "had gone off somewhere and come back with her face on wrong," and from ten feet off, without stepping closer, she shoots a low sheet of water to break the mood — a manufactured, out-of-proportion outrage designed to be fought, so Vee can't grieve and fight for a mouthful of pool at once. That is *love that wants nothing.* After a whole book of warmth that is an instrument — Randi's total, swinging warmth, Pace's listening that is a technique — Cassie's clumsy navy-suit refusal to be admired and her refusal to make anyone perform their sadness reads, right now, as the only clean warmth in the story. "They will never know the love of a woman," she says of the tossers, and the line does double duty I don't think Cassie means it to.

**Swoon / pull:** not erotic swoon — the chapter is basically sexless, and better for it — but the deepest emotional pull I've felt. The underwater passage, and the splash-fight that dissolves the grief ("the shriek-laugh, the ugly one"), had me rooting for these two with my whole chest. I want Vee to have *this* — the friend, the water, the belonging that was never taken — more than I want her to have Pace, and that's new.

**Trust vs. suspicion:** here's where the book has done its slow work on me, and I feel it. On its own this is a clean, warm chapter and I have no suspicion of Cassie at all — she reads as the realest, least instrumented person in the book. But I can't un-know what I know. I know Randi met Vee on purpose, steered by Pace, in a *game*; I know "See you later" was scheduled in a calendar Vee can't see; I know Vee is being drawn open by people with a plan. So the tenderness of this chapter has a shadow the *page itself* didn't put there but the book has earned across chapters: Cassie is the one person around Vee who wants nothing, and she's also the one being quietly moved out of frame. "Goodbye, Vee," last chapter's word "for a longer parting than a Wednesday," sits under this whole swim for me now. I read this chapter as showing me exactly what Vee has — the un-taken thing, the friend who doesn't ask — right before the book, I suspect, takes it. That's a *guess from here*, and I'll hold it as a guess. But the dread is real and I think the page earned it by making the good thing so vivid.

**Erotic charge:** essentially zero, deliberately. The only bodily heat is the *inverse* of erotic — the bleacher gaze made crude, the suit that won't let her hide. This is the book showing me the raw material the eroticism is built out of, before it's been made courtly. I didn't want heat here and didn't miss it.

**Friction:** almost none. It's tight and controlled. If I reach: "the water knew it" (of how far Cassie wades) is a hair precious, and "grieved, a little" flirts with telling me the feeling — but it's earned by everything around it, so I let it stand. The catcall dialogue could have been broader and wasn't; it's calibrated well.

**The title — "Water Wings":** before the chapter it meant nothing. After, it aches. Water wings are floaties — the thing a child wears to be held up when she can't hold herself up — and the catcall weaponizes exactly that ("You won't need floaties with *those*"), turning a child's safety device into a joke about her breasts. But the real charge is that the *water itself* is Vee's water wings: the one thing that holds her up, that she doesn't have to perform for, that was never taken. And under that, a colder read — she's being taught, this whole book, to float on other people's wanting, to let herself be buoyed by being desired. The title holds the child she was and the girl she's being made into at once. It didn't give anything away; it deepened on contact, which is the good kind.

**The book's titles:** *A Polite Invitation* / *With a Long Spoon* / the jacket's closing beat, "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." This chapter is the first that made me feel the *cost* side of that promise from the inside rather than the seduction side. "With a long spoon" — you sup with the devil with a long spoon — reads more clearly now as: someone is keeping their distance while feeding her, and the feeding is real and the distance is real. And "every yes was freely given, that was the problem" lands differently after watching Vee, underwater, be a person before she was a body-problem: her yeses are free *because* something was taken from her long ago that she doesn't even reach for anymore.

**What I want / dread next:** I want more Cassie, and I'm afraid of what wanting more Cassie means in a book like this. I expect we swing back to Pace and the froyo date's aftermath, or to the "gift" Randi is arranging — and I dread that Cassie is the price, or the witness, or the one who sees it coming and is set aside. *Guess from here:* Cassie's flat "Goodbye" + this chapter's insistence on what's "never been taken" makes me think the book is lining up the un-taken thing precisely so it can be taken, and Cassie is closer to the truth of the plan than anyone lets her be.
