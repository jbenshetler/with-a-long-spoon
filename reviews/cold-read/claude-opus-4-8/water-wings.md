## Reader reaction

FELT READ

This one landed on me differently than everything around it, and I mean that as praise. After the bench, the mirror, the pointing game — all that gorgeous, merciless watching-from-above — "Water Wings" drops down inside Vee with nobody managing her, and I got to just *love* her for a few pages. It's the first chapter in a while that isn't being run by someone. Nobody's steering Vee here. And the relief of that is the whole point of the chapter for me.

Cassie is who moved. She's been the flat, four-words-a-day one, the one who said "Goodbye, Vee" like a stair out of place, the one who watched Randi's cup without watching it. Here she gets a body and a heart, and both are wonderful. "Who designed this? Genuinely. What man?" — I laughed. "Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman." I laughed harder, and then I sat up, because that line does a small quiet thing: it's the first time in the book a woman's love gets spoken of as the real prize, dry and offhand, from the one person at the table who isn't performing anything. Cassie doesn't go past her ribs in the water. "Standing. Like a person." She keeps the joke *for* Vee instead of pitching it up into the bleachers — "She'd kept it for Vee" — and she never asks, because "asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger." That is the most loved-on Vee has been in the whole book, and it came free. No plan under it. No door being held open onto a further room. Just a friend with a good arm and better instincts. I trust Cassie completely, and this chapter is *why* — it earned the trust I'd only had a flicker of before.

The heartbreak in it is the body. That green-and-white one-piece she wore at sixteen for synchro, "when there'd been less of her to fill it. There was more of her now… it fit, and it gave her nowhere to hide." And the catcallers — "You won't need floaties with *those*!" — the applause-laughter of guys performing for each other. Vee "put her face on" and took the walk "like an entrance." That face again, the same instrument Randi runs on, the same one Vee borrowed for Pace's V.T. bit — here it's armor for fifty yards of bleachers. And then she goes under and the room switches off and "something in her chest that had stood at attention all afternoon quietly sat down." God, that landed. "She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem, and the water was the one part of it that had never been taken." That line is the ache of the whole book folded small — the sense, everywhere, that Vee's body has been *taken* from her, made into a thing other people read and grade and want, and here is the one place it was ever just hers. She grieves, a little, at the bottom of the pool. I grieved with her.

And then Cassie shoots the sheet of water across the gap — the trick calibrated so the outrage is out of proportion to the wet, "which was the point" — and the grief can't survive it. "You can't grieve and fight your best friend for a mouthful of pool at the same time." The shriek-laugh, "the ugly one," the one she hadn't made in a while. That's the warmest beat in the book so far for me, and it's not romantic and not hot — it's just two people who are actually good for each other, and the ugly laugh is the tell that it's real. "The body that was such a problem stayed up on the deck with the rolled towel, where it could wait." A perfect last line. The body as a problem set down, briefly, on a bench — same word as the other bench, and I felt the rhyme.

Trust vs. suspicion: this is the cleanest chapter in the book, and I mean it's clean of the thing that shadows everything else. There's no watcher here. No Pace reading her from a light pole, no Randi turning the wattage. Which — and here's the small cold thing the *arrangement* of the book put in me, not this chapter — makes me hold Cassie's warmth up against everyone else's and notice it's a different substance. Randi's "love you, girl" and Cassie keeping the joke for Vee are not the same act, and this chapter lets me feel the difference in my body. I don't distrust Cassie at all. If anything I'm braced *for* her — she's the one who says the true flat thing ("Goodbye, Vee," "he looked at her like somebody's great-aunt"), and I've started to feel she sees something about Randi and Pace that Vee won't. But that's my dread talking, carried in from before. On the page here, Cassie is nothing but good.

Erotic charge: basically none, and correctly so. This isn't a heat chapter. There's a flicker in how the body is rendered — the suit that fits, "legs for days" — but it's charged with shame and exposure, not desire. The chapter runs on tenderness and grief, not want. I wouldn't want a degree of heat in it; it would cheapen the one clean room in the house.

Friction: almost none. It's short and it knows exactly what it is. If I reach: "the walk of a girl who had *meant* to arrive" and "took it like an entrance" sit very close together and do the same work twice, but I didn't mind. The prose is plainer than the bench chapters and I was grateful for the plainness.

The title. "Water Wings" — the catcaller's line, "You won't need floaties with *those*," is the surface joke, crude, about her breasts. But by the end the title has turned all the way over: water wings are the thing that hold a child up who can't yet hold herself up, and the whole chapter is about Vee finding the one place she needs nothing to hold her — "nothing holding her and nothing to hold," weightless, "the slow scull… that keeps you down and turned and yours." She doesn't need water wings. She never did, down there. The floaties joke was a boy naming her body as a flotation device for *him* to notice; the chapter answers that the water was hers before her body was anyone's problem. That recoloring is lovely and I only got it on the last read-through of the title in my head. It gave nothing away going in.

The book's titles: *A Polite Invitation*, *With a Long Spoon*, and the tagline as printed — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." That tagline sits heavier and heavier on me the more chapters I read, and this chapter is the one place it doesn't apply, which is exactly why the chapter feels like air. Nobody invited Vee to the pool as a move. Nobody's spooning anything. It's the one hour of her the book has shown me that isn't inside someone's game. "With a long spoon" — you sup with the devil with a long spoon — keeps whispering that everyone getting close to Vee is keeping careful distance while they feed her. Cassie is the one person who got *in* the water, or would have if she could pass her own ribs. That she stops at her ribs — "This is as far as Cassie goes" — reads to me now, faintly, as its own held-open thing: even the good friend has a line she won't cross into the deep with Vee. I'll leave that ajar. It might just be that Cassie can't swim.

What I want next: I want more of Cassie, and I'm afraid for what the book will do to this friendship, because it's the one warm thing with no plan under it, and the book has taught me to distrust warm things with no plan under it right up until they turn out to have one. Guess from here, marked as a guess: Cassie is going to be the person who sees the Randi–Pace arrangement for what it is before Vee does, and this pool — this clean water — is being established now so I'll feel it when it gets taken too. I hope I'm wrong. I want Vee to keep the water.
