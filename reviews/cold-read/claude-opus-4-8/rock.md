## Reader reaction

# Felt read

This one undid me a little, and I need to be honest that it undid me *around* Pace, which is complicated, because I've spent chapters not trusting him.

The chapter is almost nothing but talking. Two people under a quilt on a cold glass porch, tea gone cold, a fire at their backs. And it's the first time I've felt like the machinery went quiet — no bench, no engineering I could point at, just a man answering the actual question and taking a second to find the actual answer. That's a phrase the chapter earns: "his quiet was not reluctance. It was that he answered the actual question." I believed it.

And then it gave me things I've been circling for chapters. **His name is Peter.** He handed it over flat — "My real name is Peter" — and took his mother out of the answer, and Vee let him, and I let him too. That landed. The whole evening is him underselling: the hall, the fifteen, the dormitory full of grown men. "Less than staying." "I was not a good fit where I was." The seventh-grade teacher who moved his desk into the hall for a *year* and made him do long-division because he taught himself trig — Vee's line "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment" is the best thing she's said in the book, and I felt the click of two damaged people recognizing each other.

But the thing that actually cracked the chapter open, and the reason I'm shaken, is the **tutor.** He was fifteen. She was nineteen. It went two years. He's calling it the best two years he'd had, naming it *luck*, setting himself between the memory and "the clean ugly grown-up word" Vee holds down under her tongue. The book does not say the word either, and it's exactly right not to. I said it in my own head. And here's what got me — the reader knows this man is currently steering a girl into his bed with a co-conspirator without telling her there's a game. So watching him have been, at fifteen, the *object* of an older person's engineered tenderness — handed to a nineteen-year-old as "help finding his feet" — I don't know what to do with that. It doesn't excuse the thing he's doing to Vee. But it made the machinery feel less like coldness and more like a shape someone poured him into. "He had handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck." That's the line I'll carry.

The scar. Finally touched again, and finally *addressed* — "Not tonight." No wall, only a door with a time on it. He keeps her hand there under his rather than moving it off, which is different from the first time (ch.18) when he moved her hand away. That's a small, real advance in the intimacy, and after this chapter I half-suspect the scar and the "closing at parents" are the same wound — the father in the chemical plant, the mother he keeps subtracting from every sentence.

**Vee** deepened for me too. She's not just receiving anymore; she's reading him, going first to make it a trade, holding the door open without pushing him through, choosing what to keep behind her teeth. "Sorry would have asked him to accept comfort for a thing he had not meant to open." She's become tactful, careful, *good* at love. Which — given the jacket's promise that every yes is a trap — makes me ache, because her tenderness here is completely genuine and completely aimed at a man who is lying to her by omission the whole time.

**Trust/suspicion:** the odd thing is my suspicion of Pace *softened* this chapter, and I clocked myself doing it and resented it a little. The chapter is designed — or at least I felt it — to make me warm to him via his own wound. And I keep the counterweight: Randi is out there, they picked Vee, this whole tender porch conversation is happening inside a deception he chose. So the sadness I feel is the trap doing its work on *me*. The tutor story is genuinely moving and also, some cold part of me notes, exactly the kind of thing that makes a person able to do to someone else what was done to him without feeling it as harm. He "did not hear himself doing it." That's the scariest sentence in the chapter.

**Erotic charge:** low and that's right — this is a 1, warmth not heat. The palm over her breast at the start, "as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm," is lovely and quiet. The hand on the bare hip, present as she'd just named it. It simmers under the whole thing but the chapter isn't after sex, it's after the thing that makes the sex mean what it means.

**Friction:** almost none. The "infinite paint / coastline / Newton's three sentences" riff ran a touch long, and I felt the author wanting me to find Pace beautiful there — "it's the language the world is written in" is close to the edge of too much. But Vee watching him more than listening, and the stars turning into thrown balls, pulled it back. I bought it.

**Titles.** "Rock" — I sat with it. The lift: "enough weight on it to bend the one-inch steel like dry spaghetti," nothing in his mind but the moment, and Vee's recognition that *that's how he is with her* — the lift is the same face. Rock as the thing under load that doesn't move; Pace as the steady weight-bearing presence; maybe rock-bottom, the child in the hall, the floor those careful sentences are held up off of. It's quiet and apt, doesn't give anything away. **The book title** — *With a Long Spoon* still sits opaque to me; "sup with the devil, use a long spoon," I keep assuming, someone dining with something dangerous at arm's length. And the **tagline** — "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap" — this chapter is the tagline in slow motion. Every yes Vee gives here (her name in his mouth, her palm on his scar) is free and true and offered into a frame she can't see.

**What I want/dread next:** I want the scar's door to open on its "time." I dread the two worlds touching — Randi and this porch are still overdue to collide, and after this chapter the collision will cost more. *Guess from here:* the tutor at nineteen is the template Pace and Randi are unconsciously re-running on Vee, and if Vee ever learns there was a game, this porch night — the most honest he's ever been — is what will hurt her worst, because it was real.
