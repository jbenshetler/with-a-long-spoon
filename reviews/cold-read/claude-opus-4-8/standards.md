# Cold read — Standards

*scene: scenes/standards.md · model: claude-opus-4-8 · read after: the-bench*

## Reader reaction

**The felt read**

This chapter unnerved me quietly and steadily, and it did it almost entirely through a four-minute conversation about a paper. After the heat and interiority of the founding scene, this pulls right back into daylight and dressed clothes and coffee — and it's just as tense, in a colder, more social register. What I felt in my body was the held-breath. Every "fraction of a second" that ran long, every smile that "arranged" a beat late. I was reading a woman being softly hunted and pretending, successfully, that she wasn't.

Brooke is the standout arrival and she scared me a little. "She had a voice the way other people had a perfume." The not-standing — "the not-standing was the move" — is such a precise read of power, and it's Randi who reads it, from the inside, because she sits on the same board. That detail (Randi *also* has this authority, just less of it than Brooke) is what makes the scene: this isn't a naïf being cornered, it's a professional being out-professionalled. The interrogation is masterful and awful — the paper, then "*where* did you end up working," then Pace's name, then the pickup, then "*engineering, isn't it*" landing him "a notch down without seeming to reach for it," then the real target: "you're not in one of his sections." That's what Brooke was actually walking toward the whole time. And when Randi clears it — "Not his section" — Brooke exhales "the *thank god* exhale" and releases her. The release "was the cleanest hit." I love and distrust that line. Randi is relieved and also stripped; she prepared three lines she never got to use because Brooke ended it when Brooke chose to.

So my feeling about Randi has deepened into something like ache plus admiration. Last chapter I worried for her; here I watched the machinery she runs on in full daylight, and it's extraordinary and exhausting. The matching of Kinsey's laugh pitch — "The matching was very fast. She had not had time to choose the pitch" — is the whole tragedy of her in one gesture. Even her involuntary reflexes are performances. And the "he's hard to say no to" callback wrecked me: the line she deployed to the girls as *what could I do*, coming back to her alone "in a different key … not a key she could sing in." That's the crack. She won't let it open.

The second half — the room alone — is where the chapter earns its slowness. She can't sit. She can't locate what she came to the closet to do, hand half-lifted toward the row of bone-to-camel-to-oatmeal clothes (that "one disciplined oxblood at the end" is a gorgeous, sad little flare of contained wanting). She runs water and doesn't put her hands under it. She lies down in last night's clothes with her shoes on. And the cold — "The room was cold. The house was lovely and old and did not hold its heat." After a whole first chapter of Pace's warm house, she comes home to a cold room and pulls up a duvet that is emphatically *not* something: "It was not — it was a duvet." That aposiopesis is doing everything. It's not the bench. It's not being held. It's adequate and synthetic and hers, and it's not enough, and she won't say what it's failing to be.

The last movement — the not-crying — is the best writing in the chapter and confirms everything the first chapter set up. "It did not feel like a decision … It felt like there being no door." Last chapter I read her sealing herself as a choice ("not ever"); here the book gently corrects me, or deepens me: it's past choosing now, so practiced it's stopped registering as an act, "the way a held breath stops feeling held." That's devastating and it rings true. She doesn't cry. She closes her eyes. No relief, no release, just the breath still held.

**Swoon / pull** — Almost no swoon here, and rightly — no Pace on the page, no touch. The pull is dread and tenderness, not heat. What swept me was craft: the surgical social scene, and then the ache of the empty cold room. I wanted to reach into the page and let her cry.

**Trust vs. suspicion** — My suspicion here isn't of Pace (absent) — it's of the *house*. Brooke's warmth is explicitly a tool ("Brooke's voice was always warm" sits right next to the not-standing power-move), and the interrogation was real, whatever the smiling. The sorority reads as a surveillance apparatus with excellent manners. Whether that's threat or just milieu, I can't tell yet — but the scrutiny of Pace specifically ("Pace. That's actually his name?" being filed, "I kept thinking you'd said Chase") felt like something being logged, not just gossip. I'll hold that ajar.

**Erotic charge** — Deliberately near zero, and I didn't miss it. This is the cold-daylight counterweight to the founding scene's heat. The one erotic residue is the callback and the duvet-that-isn't — desire present only as absence and cold.

**Friction** — Very little. The chapter is confident and controlled. If anything, the room-alone section risks over-repeating its own beat (she did not sit; she did not sit; did not put hands under water) — but I think the repetition *is* the paralysis, so it earned it for me. The one spot where I felt the thumb faintly: "a woman who would one day be some CEO's third wife" is a great line but it's the narrator editorializing on Brooke a touch more sharply than Randi's restraint elsewhere. Small.

**The title** — "Standards" lands as a clean double. It's literally the board Randi sits on — the sorority's standards committee, which polices exactly this kind of conduct (out all night, a grad-student boyfriend, "you're not in one of his sections"). So the whole interrogation is a Standards proceeding conducted as friendly chat, by the president, with Randi as both defendant and fellow-officer. But it also means her *standards* — the portioned appetite, the bone-to-oatmeal discipline, the face that never slips — the exacting self-surveillance that is eating her. The chapter title illuminates rather than gives away; nice.

**The book's titles** — *A Polite Invitation* and *With a Long Spoon* still hum ominously and this chapter feeds them. "Sup with the devil, use a long spoon" — keep your distance from what you're eating with. Randi keeps everyone at spoon's length, including herself; and there's a "polite invitation" in every one of Brooke's questions, each one an invitation you can't refuse politely. The tagline I don't have in front of me, so I'll leave it.

**What I want / dread next** — I want Vee, still — she hasn't appeared, and the jacket says Randi steers her to Pace, so I'm waiting for that machinery to start. *Guess from here:* the Standards scrutiny of Pace is going to matter — either the house is a threat to the secret, or Randi's position on that board is a tool she'll use. I dread watching Randi recruit someone else (Vee?) into Pace's orbit while she seals herself off from him. And I dread, gently, learning who the bench was built for.
