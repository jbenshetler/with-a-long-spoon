**Felt read**

Oh, this one hurt me, and it did it cleanly. For the first time in a long stretch I got a whole chapter of Pace and Vee with no Randi-and-Pace bedroom scene bracketing it to poison my view — just the dance lesson on a cold concrete pad under field lights, and it was *lovely*. The lead as a whole language: "Give me something to push back against. Otherwise you cannot feel my lead." "The space you're keeping. Close it — come all the way in — and my feet can't land anywhere but past yours." I melted at that, I'll be honest, because it's him all over — the thing that looks like a rule turns out to be the only place the next step can come from. And the moment it took her, "There it is," quiet — I was rooting so hard. That's the swoon, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't, even knowing what I know.

Which is exactly why the second half gutted me. The genius of this chapter is that Vee does the damage herself, and she does it with the *mother's voice* — the floozie voice — finally turned outward instead of inward. The blonde's line is a beautifully nasty little dose of venom: "Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to… He's so good about getting a girl home safe at the end of the night." It's engineered to curdle, and it curdles. And Vee — underslept, overfull on her own happiness, already counting the women who hugged him too long — takes the one true tender fact about him and throws it back as an accusation: "Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe from here, too?" I actually winced. "She heard it the way you hear a glass go past the point where you can still catch it." God, yes.

What moved my feeling: Pace went *up* for me here, and I didn't expect that, because I've spent the whole book holding him at arm's length as the man who engineered this girl's autumn as a game. But watch what he does — he answers straight ("Yes. Sometimes. When it's required"), doesn't defend, doesn't chase, sets the untouched beer down and *leaves*. And the bartender, who owes Vee nothing, delivers the correction: he leaves them at the door, doesn't take advantage, doesn't bring dates, and "Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey." That landed on me as hard as it landed on Vee. The horrible thing is that the con and the goodness are *both* real — the bartender is telling the truth, and so is the thing I know that Vee doesn't. He is a genuinely safe, decent man who is also the author of a hidden design. The chapter let me feel the first half of that fully for once.

Vee I love and ache for. Her cruelty here isn't out of character — it's the shame finally weaponized, "three dates, and she'd spread her legs like a slut," her mother arriving right on cue ("a man like that has girls, and you'd be one of them"). The self-punishment spiral on the bed — cataloguing every good thing about him and realizing "She'd punished him for being the man she wanted him to be" — is her at her most human and most heartbreaking. And the detail that wrecked me: the laundered shirt. She rationed his smell until it went stale, finally washed it, "It came back smelling of fabric softener and no one," and she put it on anyway. That's the whole ache of the chapter in one object.

Now the dread, and it's acute. The last line is a trap door opening under me: she's going to Randi's to fix it. *Randi.* I know Randi is Pace's lover. I know whatever Vee pours out, Pace may hear it tonight in bed. Vee thinks she's going to the one person who can name her feelings and hand them back clean; I'm watching her carry her freshest wound straight into the heart of the machine. That's the purest dramatic-irony dread the book has handed me yet, and I want to keep reading *right now* to see what Randi does with it — console her back toward Pace (because the game needs it), or something colder.

Trust/suspicion: no *new* suspicion earned on the page — the blonde is just a jealous girl, not a plant (I've no reason to read her as sent). My dread is all structural, from what I already know, not from a fresh tell. And one small honest note: Vee herself, mid-dance, reaches for the knife I keep holding — "how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd been building out of wanting him" — and lets it go. That she can even ask it kept her from feeling naïve to me.

Erotic charge: low and that's right — this chapter runs on intimacy and shame, not heat. The closest thing to charge is the frame itself, the hand at the shoulder blade, "come all the way in" — a simmer, and it's doing emotional work, not erotic.

Friction: almost none. The three dialogue-beat drumbeat ("word for word, huh") is light and funny and earns Meg's warmth. If anything I'll flag the triple "She thought:" stack at the Rusty Nail as a hair schematic, but it reads as her counting against her own will, so I bought it.

**The title.** "Lesson" — on the surface the dance lesson, obviously. But it recolors: the real lesson is the one the bartender teaches her, and the one she teaches *herself* with the shame-voice — and the dance itself is the thesis, *stop guessing, give your weight, trust the lead.* It points at Vee learning (too late tonight) what she's actually standing in. It didn't give anything away; it sharpened on finishing. The cover line **"WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION,"** volume title **"A Warm Reception"** — the long-spoon proverb (you sup with the devil with a long spoon) sits heavier every chapter: the warmth is real *and* it's the thing you need the long spoon for. "A Warm Reception" reads double now — the literal warmth Vee keeps being received with, and the reception waiting at Randi's.

What I want next: Randi's face when Vee arrives wounded. Whether Pace hears of this. Whether this is the first crack toward Vee discovering the two of them. I dread that her reaching for repair walks her deeper in.
