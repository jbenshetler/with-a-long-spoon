## Reader reaction

FELT READ

This one got me. I went in braced — I've been braced this whole book, because I know what Vee doesn't — and the chapter just quietly walked past my guard the same way Pace walks Vee up the mountain: at the speed of her not knowing how. By the end I wasn't watching for the con. I was on my knees on that truck bench with her, and I let myself be, and I only remembered to be afraid afterward, which is exactly what she does — "the body came back after the seeing, slow."

The heat is real and it's the best-earned heat in the book so far. What undoes me is that there's no seduction move in it at all — Pace doesn't lean in, doesn't lift a hand, doesn't touch her once during the striptease. He gives her his one dry shirt and then gets out of his own truck and stands in the rain with his back to her so she can be warm and private, and that gesture is what turns her over. The whole erotic engine of the scene is *her* deciding — rapping three times on the glass, crooking a finger, keeping his eyes on hers, going down the buttons by feel so she never has to look away, and then "She looked down anyway, and his eyes went with hers." I felt that line in my stomach. The swoon and the wanting-to-be-seen are one thing here, and the mother's voice running underneath it — "floozy," "three dates," "your mother would never" — isn't killing the heat, it's *feeding* it: "the voice was the heat and the heat was the voice." That's the interior life I read for. She's not overcoming the shame, she's found the wire where the shame and the wanting are the same current, and the chapter renders that with total nerve.

And then the gut-punch reversal: she reaches for his hand to bring it to her mouth, to start the next thing, and it's shaking. He's been shivering the whole time. He gave her the shirt and the warmth and took the cold, and he was so far gone into looking at her that he'd left the question of his own body entirely. "You're freezing." That she stops — that *she* is the one who says we should go, get warm, please — is what made the whole thing land as tender instead of predatory. He nods, leaves his hand on her belly a beat longer than he has to, and drives. Nobody gets fucked in this chapter and it's the most intimate thing yet.

How I feel about Pace: this is the problem, isn't it. On the page, in this chapter, he is nothing but good to her. The moleskin on the heel, laced tight-at-the-ankle-loose-at-the-top, "Should've done that at the lot. I'm sorry. I didn't think to ask about the boots" — that's care with no angle I can find. The cherry pit: she spits it, her face goes hot, the mother-voice has its "common" word ready, and he just picks up the pit, lays it on the wax paper, reaches for his own cherry and lays his pit beside hers and *doesn't look at her doing it* — teaches her without making her small. The russet dress: she talks herself out of it, sets it back on the shelf, and he doesn't push, doesn't offer to buy it, just "That sounds like a beautiful dress" and lets it stay set down. Every single one of these is the exact opposite of pressure. And I cannot fully rest in it, because I know this man selected her off a dining-hall doorway as a game with his lover, and the *same* attentiveness that's melting me is the instrument. But — being honest, the way I promised to be — the text this chapter is not winking at me. It's playing the tenderness straight, and I'm going to let it be as real as it plays, and hold the dread as a separate thing I carry, not something the page put in this scene. The jacket already told me it "got real" for the planners. This reads like the getting-real.

The suspicion I *do* have is structural and quiet, and it comes from one motif, not from Pace's face: the title. "We take nothing and leave nothing" — leave no trace. That's a hiker's ethic on the page, gentle, and it's the thing he teaches her with the cherry pit. But set against the jacket line "Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over," it goes cold. The chapter is a working demonstration of the method: he arranges the conditions — the hike makes her hungry so the food lands, the rain and the one dry shirt make the truck — and then he takes nothing. He *lets* her hand it over. "The hike had made her hungry first. That was the trick of it. That was what it was for." She thinks that's a nice thing about lunch. I read it as the whole design in a sentence, and the chapter handed it to me plainly enough that I don't feel I'm manufacturing it.

Vee: I love her more here than anywhere. The reaching for the russet and putting it back — "You put a thing back on the shelf and you keep walking" — is heartbreaking because it's so practiced, so young, so much her mother's economy in her own mouth. And the summit is the best writing in the chapter: the valley not caring that she's standing there with her heart going, "and that not-caring was somehow the most beautiful part of all of it." A girl who has been watched and displayed her whole life, standing in front of something enormous that will not look at her — of course that's the beautiful part. His blue shirt gets swallowed by the world and he lets her have the looking alone, and then she catches him watching her face instead of the view and he doesn't look away, "He let her catch him." That's the erotics of being seen again, but earned in open air.

Erotic charge: peaks in the truck, sustained and clothed and almost unbearable, a 2 that feels bigger than most 3s because it's all interiority and consent. Never goes clinical. The food writing (horseradish climbing into her sinuses, her eyes running and not caring) is its own sensual register and it's doing real work — appetite thawing, again, the book's throughline.

Friction: almost none. If I reach, the mother-voice interjections in the truck — "*This is what girls who. Three dates. Your mother would never. Your mother.*" — come thick and fragmented, and there's a hair's-breadth where the fragmentation risks tipping from felt into stylized. But it held for me because her body answers each one physically; it never became decoration. The cop at the end is a clean, slightly convenient cut — it takes her big unsaid thing away with a siren bark so she doesn't have to say it, which is neat, maybe a touch neat. But I bought it as the book refusing her the tidy declaration, which fits.

Titles: "Leave No Trace" is the sharpest title yet on reread — innocuous as trail ethics, and then it's the whole method named. *A Polite Invitation* keeps paying off: the invitation is the arrangement of conditions so the yes is hers. The tagline on my jacket — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — is basically the caption for this chapter. She freely gave every inch of it. That's the problem. *With a Long Spoon* still sits oblique to me — the proverb about supping with the devil needing a long spoon — and this chapter makes me feel the spoon: Pace never reaches, he lets her reach.

What I want / dread next: I want the hike afterglow to breathe before it curdles. I dread — guess from here — that Randi is going to hear about this truck scene the way she's heard everything, and that the "getting real" is going to start colliding. And I want to know what Pace felt sitting there shivering, having given away his shirt to watch her — because for one chapter the operator looked like a man who forgot he had a body.
