## Reader reaction

# Felt read

This is the chapter I've been waiting nine chapters for — Vee finally at Pace's world, well, at least out of the golden campus bubble and onto his trail — and it landed on me hard, more tenderly and more troublingly than I expected. Let me get the body-response out first: the truck scene worked on me. Fully. It's the first time the erotic charge in this book has peaked for me rather than simmered, and it did it with no one touching anyone. He gives her his only dry shirt and goes and stands in the rain with his back turned, and she reads the whole shape of it — "His shirt, and then the truck. Both." — and the wanting rises up in her *out of that*, out of the gift, and then she's the one who raps on the glass and crooks her finger and unbuttons her own shirt while holding his eyes. That's the thing the book keeps promising — "every step is hers" — and here it actually felt like hers. I believed the heat. The line that did it for me was the doubled undercurrent: "*I want him in here with me.* And under it, slower — *I want him to see me.*" That second one is the real one, and the book knows it.

And then — the turn I didn't see coming and loved — she reaches for his hand to start the next thing, "because her body was already going at the next thing and the next thing was more," and his hand is *shaking*. He's freezing. He's been out in the rain twice for her. And she stops. She stops her own momentum and mothers him — "You're freezing," "We should go. Get warm. Please." That reversal gutted me a little. For the first time Pace is the vulnerable body in the room and she's the one attending, and I couldn't tell whether I was watching the machine (jacket says both founders fall) or watching something genuine crack open in him. That ambiguity is exactly the ache the book runs on.

Here's where my suspicion sits, because I have to be honest about the double vision. Everything Pace did on this hike is *unimpeachably kind* — the moleskin on the heel, "Should've done that at the lot, I'm sorry, I didn't think to ask about the boots," the sandwiches made that morning with the lettuce against the bread, the water bottle shared without ceremony, the flannel. If I hadn't read the first eight chapters, this is a dream date with a thoughtful man. But I *have* read them, and I know this is the same man who ran a pointing game and picked her from a crowd, who is Randi's secret lover, who "engineers agency." So the horseradish sandwich made me flinch even as it charmed me: "The hike had made her hungry first. That was the trick of it. That was what it was for." That's Vee's own thought, but it's also the book's thesis said plainly — *the hunger made first, then met.* He builds the want and then satisfies it and calls it generosity. The cherry-pit moment — "We take nothing and leave nothing" — is beautiful surface and I felt her shame at not knowing, and I also clocked that he corrects her *without* making her small, laying his own pit beside hers, which is the exact texture of his method: kindness that leaves no fingerprint. Which is the chapter's title, isn't it. Leave no trace.

The russet dress. This is the quietest, most dangerous thing in the chapter and I don't fully trust it. She confesses the dress she can't afford, catches her own wanting, puts it back on the shelf — "You put a thing back on the shelf and you keep walking" — and he says only "That sounds like a beautiful dress" and *lets it lie there.* On the page that reads as restraint, as respect. But I've watched Randi draw out Vee's buried shames "and hand them back as gifts," and I've watched Pace file things away. He didn't buy the dress. He *learned* she wants it and where and what color and why she won't let herself have it. I would bet money — *guess from here* — that russet dress arrives before the induction. And when it does I won't know whether to swoon or scream, which is precisely where this book likes to put me.

The summit itself I'll flag as the one place the prose almost lost me — not because it's bad, it's gorgeous, but the paragraph is one enormous breathless sentence ("every yellow and orange and red the season had, banked and layered against the black of the rock and the silver of the water and the silver of the sky") and it goes on long enough that I felt the author's hand reaching for sublime. But it earned itself back with "it did not care that she was standing on the edge of it... and that not-caring was somehow the most beautiful part of all of it," and then "The world swallowed his blue whole." That's the thing I liked — the vista dwarfs *him*, shrinks the man who's been the whole frame of her attention, and for a moment she's just a body in weather. Good.

Randi is entirely absent, not even named, and I felt her absence as relief and as dread both — this was a clean two-hander, the first sustained Vee-and-Pace stretch, and it let the tenderness breathe. But I kept waiting for the other shoe. It never dropped. The cop showing up at the fire lane is the closest thing to consequence and it's almost comic — he parked illegal so she wouldn't cross the rain, and gets pulled over for the last kindness. It cut off her thank-you, "took the rest of her sentence with it," and I felt the small ache of the unfinished thing.

The tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap." — has never rung louder than it does over the truck scene. She initiated *all* of it. He didn't lean in, didn't lift a hand, "sat and looked at all of her and didn't move, and the gladness in him was the whole of the answer." That is the trap in its purest form: he made it so entirely hers that she can never later say he took anything. Which is either the most respectful thing a man can do or the most complete capture, and the book refuses to let me settle it. That's why I keep reading.

What I want next: the induction, the dress, and — badly — for Vee's two worlds to touch. She's now spent a whole day inside Pace's actual life (his gym bag, his only dry shirt, the "no house here") and still has no idea it connects to Randi. The floor under her feels thinner every chapter. I dread the moment she finds out there was a game, and I'm starting to dread it *for Pace and Randi too*, which I think is the point.
