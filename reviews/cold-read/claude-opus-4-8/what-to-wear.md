# Cold read (grounded) — What to Wear

## Reader reaction

FELT READ

After the mountain and the truck — which ran hot in a way I'm still carrying — this chapter is a deliberate exhale, and I let it be one, though not without a cold finger at the base of my spine the whole time. It's Vee in the gold-lit dining hall with two new girls, retelling everything I just watched, and the pleasure of it is real: I love watching her be *the one at the table*, loud and easy, "laughing with her whole body," the girl the next table wants in on. That's a woman I've only seen flinch and cover with cardigans, and here she's the sun of a little corner. It made me glad in a simple, warm way.

But I read the whole scene double, because I can't not. Vee narrates the shirt, the truck, the near-kiss, the siren, and to her it's a swoony highlight reel — "the story getting better in the telling and she did not care." And it IS the sweetest possible read of what happened. What snagged me is how much she's *performing the innocuousness of it*, the same trick this book keeps running: "It sounds like nothing. I can hear it sounding like nothing... climbing the walls over a man asking me what I want for dinner." She's half-defending Pace to the girls and half-defending him to herself, and the honesty of that — "He's different. I know how that sounds" — is exactly where the ache lives for me, because I know precisely how it sounds, and I know what I saw on the bench in chapter one.

Meg is the one who unsettled me, quietly and well. She's the calm, level girl who "let a sentence finish," and she's the only voice in the room that doesn't get swept: "Everybody's different at the start. I said it about Danny. Word for word... Sometimes they even are." That last clause is generous and devastating at once — it holds the door open both ways. And then: "Vee let it go by. It didn't catch on anything." That line landed like a small dropped stone. It's the closest thing to a warning Vee's going to get from inside her own glow, and it slides right off. I felt the author's hand there a little, but not heavily — it earned the chill because Meg is drawn as a real, unhurried person and not a plot-siren.

Then the phone. "His name" before she chose to look, and the whole table coming apart, and — the invitation. "Would you come to my house Saturday. Two o'clock." I felt my stomach drop, and I want to be honest that it dropped in *both* registers at once: the girlish thrill Vee feels ("her heart was doing something quick and light and stupid, and she let it"), and pure dread, because I have been to that house. I watched what the bench is for. I know the deadbolt goes first. Kayla, comic and oblivious, keeps saying the true thing without knowing it: "That's not a message, that's a ransom note." "He's not asking you over to eat." "He wants you for the afternoon. Bring a change of clothes." She thinks she's being racy. She's reading the situation more correctly than anyone at the table, and that irony is the coldest thing in the chapter. Two o'clock, no meal, no word about what it's for — and the girls fill the silence with hair and outfits while I fill it with the house I've seen.

Pace stays offstage and stays magnetic-by-report: the lifting, the six hours of writing then stopping, "My writing's no good after that." I still can't find the seam in him, and that's precisely what keeps my wariness pinned high. Every disclosed detail is another brick of a man too complete.

TRUST/SUSPICION: No new information, but the dramatic irony tightened hard. Nothing in *this* chapter is off — it's a warm, clean girl-talk scene. The dread is entirely mine, carried in from what I know that Vee doesn't: the house, the bench, Randi. The invitation is the plot's held breath.

EROTIC CHARGE: Mostly banked here — this is afterglow-by-retelling, not heat in the room. The charge lives in anticipation: the "least information a human being has ever sent me and I have read it a thousand times," and Kayla's "bring a change of clothes." A simmer, deliberately, after the truck's peak.

TITLES: "What to Wear" — on the surface it's just the girls' squeal, what does a person put on for an unmarked invitation. But it turned cold on me by the last page, because *he didn't say what to wear*, and I keep thinking about a house where, the one time I was in it, the whole point was the taking-off. "What to Wear" reads to me now like a question with a trick in it — as if the answer the house has in mind is *nothing*. It sits right on the innocuous-surface / reread-charge line. The book title *A Polite Invitation* and the series *With a Long Spoon* land square this chapter: the invitation just arrived, textbook-polite, "would you come," and I know it's not the first invitation and it's not innocent. "With a long spoon" — you sup with the devil using one — glosses everything: these lovely lunches, this warmth, the whole courteous distance being closed one date at a time. The tagline "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." hums under Kayla planning her hair while Vee's heart goes "quick and light and stupid, and she let it." She's letting it. That's the yes.

WANT/DREAD NEXT: I want Saturday, badly, and I dread it in equal measure — which is the exact grip this book has had on me since chapter one. Guess from here: the house scene is the threshold the whole volume has been walking toward, and I suspect Randi is nearer to it than Vee can imagine — that "for what" may not be a question only Vee gets to answer.
