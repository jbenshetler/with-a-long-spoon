## Reader reaction

A quiet chapter, and after last chapter's warmth-flood it lands like a breath held in a different key — no heat at all, just two friends at a cafeteria table, and yet it did something to me. This is the first time in a while I've had Cassie on the page, and I'd missed her. She's exactly as I carried her: warm, dry, exact. "Cassie didn't laugh, which was the right call." "I know how she does." She reads Vee at the millimeter and doesn't push — and the chapter closes on the smallest, sharpest thing: "Is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to?" "Both. Probably both." That's the whole chapter's spine. Vee cannot narrate herself cleanly, and Cassie is the one person who'd catch the seam, so Vee crops.

The title does its work in the open and then keeps working. She *cropped the photo* before sending it home — "The version they have stops at the waist." So the induction picture, the proud thing, is already edited down to a version that fits the frame her parents can hold. And then the whole telling is a crop: "I told Dad the parts that fit." The math PhD, the hiking, the dancing on concrete — the parts a father gets to be proud of. Cropped out: the house he owns, the cooking, and the one she names as the real unsayable — *he made the dress.* Sewed it himself because she mentioned a magazine she couldn't afford. That's a genuinely new fact and it deepened him for me all over again — the furniture-maker, the flannel with no tag, now a man who sews a dress on a sun porch. Care enacted in the made object, never the word. It fits everything I know of him and it's tender as hell on the page.

And here's where my dread hummed under the warmth: her father's instinct is the reader's instinct. "An expensive gift from a man means his daughter is in deeper than he'd pictured." "A man who cooks for you and owns his house, my father would really start looking for the catch. Too good is its own kind of suspicious." Her dad is doing exactly the reading I've been doing for chapters — looking for the catch — and he's looking in the wrong place (the cost of the dress) because he can't imagine the actual shape of it. The dress didn't cost money; it cost the thing I can't get the father to see and Vee can't either. "Too good is its own kind of suspicious" is the truest line in the book about the whole con, spoken by a man who has no idea what he's near.

What moved me most, quietly: "the strange thing is, I'm not ashamed of it. The opposite, if anything." She's proud of the made dress, proud of *being* the girl a man would sew for — and that pride is the thing the con feeds on. Her decency, her openness to being cherished, turned into the exact material. And she ends the scene grateful Cassie doesn't push and "also, a little, not." She wants to be caught. She wants someone to make her say it. Cassie is the one who could — and Vee crops herself before Cassie can.

Trust vs. suspicion: nothing new to suspect; this is consolidation. But it sharpened the ache — the truth-teller is right here, asking the right question, and gets "both." The geometry that keeps boxing Cassie out isn't only schedule; it's Vee's own cropping.

Friction: none, really. It's nearly all dialogue and telling, which after five nights and a pizza could have felt slack, but the compression works — each cropped detail is a knife. If anything I wanted one physical beat to break the table-talk, but the flatness is the point.

The book's title and *With a Long Spoon* — "too good is its own kind of suspicious" is the long-spoon proverb said plainly, from the mouth of the one person allowed to be wholly warm. And *A Polite Invitation* — everything here is invitation edited to be palatable: the photo cropped to the waist, the boyfriend with a question mark, the parts that fit. What I want next: I want Cassie to ask again, or to see the uncropped thing. I want to know whether "the version they have stops at the waist" is only about her parents — because I'm holding a whole thread about an uncropped nude already in the runners' hands, and the word *cropped* just got said out loud.
