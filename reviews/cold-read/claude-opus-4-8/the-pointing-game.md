# Cold read (grounded) — The Pointing Game

## Reader reaction

Oh. *Oh.* That last page landed like a trapdoor. I have been reading three chapters about Randi — her hunger, her face, the thing on the bench, the deadbolt in her ribs, the not-crying under the duvet — and I came into this chapter braced for more of her interior, and instead the book quietly walked me across a whole golden campus afternoon and delivered me the redhead by the dining hall doors, and named her *Vee*. And the jacket has been telling me from the first page that Vee is the girl this whole thing is happening *to*, the one who never knew there was an invitation. So the pointing game is the invitation. The "secret choice made about her" is this — a man's finger on a path, a woman saying *yes* with her whole broken body. I actually sat back from the screen. I had the pieces and the chapter still got the drop on me, because it let me feel it as a game before it let me feel it as a mechanism.

Let me start with the heat, because it opens hot and the opening is doing something I found genuinely thrilling and a little sinister at once. Pace comes up into her while she's still pulsing from his mouth, undefended, *he had decided this beforehand* — and then floats the question into "the warm disorder of her": *Would you like to taste another woman?* And her body answers before she does, she comes on the question, "far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it." That is a spectacular piece of erotic writing. The desire arriving ahead of the person, the want outrunning the self that would police it — that is exactly the interior-under-the-wanting thing I read for, and here it's rendered *as* the orgasm. I felt swept up and I also felt the cold little draft of how deliberately he's fishing. He's not asking to know her; he's asking to *catch* her, and he catches her. I loved it and I clocked the calculation in the same breath, and I think the chapter wants both from me.

Then the daylight game, which is where my admiration and my unease really braided together. The structure is beautiful — Pace pointing, Randi picking, and the parade of girls functioning as a slow striptease of Randi's own taste. Not the mirror-girls, not the matched pairs, not the closed camel-coat "project," not the girl who runs the room. Each *no* tells us who Randi is by telling us who she isn't, and I found myself leaning forward with her, "shopping," feeling the coil come up through her hand — "a follow gathering herself a half-beat before the lead moves." The dance metaphor is doing real work; it's the same trained instrument from the bench, aimed now at a person. And the *maybe* on the library-steps girl, where he stops and kisses her with her own taste still on his mouth — that's a filthy, gorgeous little escalation, the game and the sex folding into each other.

But here's my honest divided response: the whole afternoon is Pace *managing* her into wanting what he wants her to want, and telling himself the entire time that he's doing it *for* her — "let it come out laughing, dressed as play, before she had to look it full in the face. She deserved that gentleness." I don't fully trust that tenderness, and I don't think I'm supposed to. It's the same tenderness that put her on the bench and called it hers. He is very good, and being very good at reading a woman's helplessness and then curating a "gift" out of it is not the same as being safe. The jacket keeps insisting "nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over," and this chapter is the machinery of exactly that claim — a consent engineered so precisely that her *yes* is real and also produced. That's the merciless part the blurb promised, and it's working on me: I can't cleanly separate the swoon from the disquiet, because the text won't let me.

What most got me, emotionally, was the seam in Randi. Twice she gives him the bare thing and then reaches for the costume. The plain "Yes" with nothing around it, then "Uh — yeah. Yeah. I like her." The way she moves the wanting off herself — "not a woman who hungered for another woman, which was unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act. A different owner." That is the same woman from the mirror, from the duvet, who cannot let a thing *reach* her. The book is consistent with her all the way down: she is doing on the quad exactly what she did in the bathroom — taking the true thing and re-housing it somewhere it can't cost her. And Pace sees it and lets the camouflage stand. I ache for her a little. She's so armored that even her desire has to be laundered through him to be bearable.

And then Vee. The prose *changes* when she arrives — everything before her is graded, curated, "built to the proper scale," and Vee is "lush in a way that had nothing curated about it and nothing apologized for," laughing with her head thrown back "like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen." After three chapters steeped in the terror of being seen, that line is a gut-punch of contrast. Vee is everything Randi's grammar has no category for. And Randi *doesn't look away* — the one who releases every woman with a verdict just *stays*, helpless. That helplessness is the truest thing she's shown in daylight. I believe her wanting completely, and it frightens me for both of them, because I know from the jacket that Vee is the mark, and I've just watched two people select her like produce, and Randi's real feeling is now bound up inside a game she thinks is play.

The collision itself — the dance-clean shoulder-check engineered so *Vee* is the one apologizing — is a small masterpiece of dread dressed as charm. "Looked, if anything, like the redhead had walked into *her.*" That's the whole book in one image: the person who was chosen made to feel like the one who arrived. I admired it and it chilled me.

**Trust/suspicion:** I have real, earned unease now, not free-floating. Pace's courtliness reads increasingly like control that has learned to feel like care. Randi is sympathetic and self-deceiving in equal measure. Neither feels *evil* — that's what's unsettling; the warmth is genuine and it's also the instrument. And Vee, glowing "she's got a stats class together," has just been handed to two people who planned her, and doesn't know a plan exists. That's dread, and the page earned every ounce of it.

**Erotic charge:** peaks in the opening bed scene (the question-as-orgasm) and in the library-steps kiss with her own taste on his mouth. The parade stays warm-hot as sustained tension rather than act. It never went clinical for me.

**Titles:** "The Pointing Game" — before reading, innocuous, almost playful. After, it's chilling: the game is a person being pointed *at*, chosen, and the "game" framing is precisely the wrapper that lets Randi want without owning it, and lets Pace select a woman without it being what it is. It gave nothing away going in and recolors hard coming out. *A Polite Invitation* — now I feel the full weight: the invitation is this afternoon, polite because it never announces itself, extended to a girl who won't know she received it. *With a Long Spoon* — "he who sups with the devil should use a long spoon." That proverb is starting to hum. Who's the devil here, and whose spoon is long enough? The tagline — *"Every yes was freely given. That was the problem."* — this chapter is that sentence dramatized. Every yes in it *was* freely given, and every one was also produced. That's the problem, exactly.

**What I want/dread next:** I want to be inside Vee now — the jacket promised her POV and I'm hungry for the other side of that shoulder-check, the innocence against everything I now know. Guess from here: the next stretch shifts to Vee meeting "Randi," the glamorous new friend, none the wiser. I dread how much I'm going to like Vee while knowing what she's walking into. And I want to keep watching whether Randi's real feeling for Vee cracks the game open — the jacket hinted the thing gets "as true for the two who started it." That's the ache I'll be reading toward.
