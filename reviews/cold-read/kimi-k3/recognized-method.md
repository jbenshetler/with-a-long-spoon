## Reader reaction

I read this chapter with my shoulders up around my ears and then, somewhere around "Leave them on," they came down — and that is exactly the problem this book is building in me. I *know* things. And still, when he lifted the pie out of her hands "the way you take a glass from someone about to drop it," and set the scarf back with "a half-second longer over the silk than the errand needed," I felt the seduction work on me the way it's working on her. The tagline is no longer a premise; it's a sensation.

The apology itself broke my heart in the best way. The HUMBLE PIE label made at the stats-lab printer at three in the morning, trimmed "with the good scissors" — that's so exactly her, contrition as a manufactured object, pre-labeled so nobody has to be too naked. And then Randi's clean machine runs beautifully — and he simply declines it. "Okay," he said gently. "But what happened?" The script had no part for the why, because Randi built it precisely so Vee would never have to say the why. And here's the thing that undid me: the script's failure is the evening's success. What actually heals Thursday is the one thing Randi's design existed to avoid — Vee, out of road, telling the raw truth in the wrong order, the blonde's exact words falling out of her mouth. "She handed me a lie and I picked it up because some part of me was just standing there waiting for one." I wanted to put the book down and hold her. And the awful double exposure: that "lie" was false in its details and almost true in its shape. She *is* a girl being gotten somewhere by patient hands. Her instinct wasn't broken; the evidence was. She has no way to know that the thing standing there waiting wasn't a lie at all.

His response — "Thank you for telling me what she said" — I cannot settle. It's tender. It's also the response of a man receiving a file. He didn't say *she's wrong*, didn't defend himself; he took delivery. And I now carry the blonde's words in full — "Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to… all us girls love him… so good about getting a girl home safe" — and I cannot un-see Sheri. Bleached ponytail. Two years of Saturdays. At the Nail that Thursday. Who but a regular owns that exact proprietary knowledge, "said your name like she owned a piece of it"? And Sheri's report to Pace mentioned the glaring, the one drink, leaving alone — but no blonde conversation. The omission is loud. This is suspicion the text earned from me, and it is now live.

The kitchen scene is the hottest paragraph the book has given me, precisely because nobody planned it — or because I can't prove anybody planned it. Her lobbying about the soak, his unhurried hand turning off the tap, the attention settling "the way it had in the front hall," and then the twist that "was the wrong way, or it was exactly the wrong way." The wet flat hand. "Her body saying two or three things at once that she had not agreed to have it say." And him, gone still, watching her face — the same diagnostic stillness Randi had at the thumb behind the knee. Both of them have now watched her body answer before she could. Both times she covered — this time with a kiss she chose, walking him backward, the pan left "exactly where it wanted to be, soaking, while they went." That pan is her. The title is the whole method: don't scrub, let it sit, the water does the work. It's her own joke naming what he's been doing all along, and it recolors the chapter on second thought without giving anything away up front.

The quiet parts afterwards nearly hurt more. The undershirt folded on the bed while she slept. The sweatpants rolled three times. "She smelled of him head to foot, nothing of her own left anywhere on her skin, and she had not minded it. She had gone a little still over it." That stillness is the scariest sentence in the chapter. And "You're always beautiful" — not *you're beautiful*, "which she'd have argued" — *always*. He keeps finding the one version she can't deflect, and gathering her hair "the way you hold a thing you mean to keep." Keeping. I swooned and I took notes.

Friction: almost none. "The unbearable ordinary fact of him" is a touch lush. The narration spelling out his trick — "he said the true extravagant thing in the plain voice" — is a mild thumb on a scale I'd already weighed, but it's her voice, so I allow it. That's all.

What next: Monday, the dorm, the dress, Cassie's unasked questions — two nights gone, no bag, no explanation. Then the report to Randi, who will hear her script credited for work honesty did. And somewhere, Pace holding a description of a blonde girl who was at the Nail on Thursday — while his bleached-blonde riding partner asks how the redhead's doing. I dread that chapter and I need it.
