# Cold read (grounded) — Not Enough

## Reader reaction

This chapter broke me open a little. After chapters of watching Vee hold the word and Pace hold the word, here I finally get inside his silence — and the devastating part is that it's not coldness, not calculation, not the hidden structure with Randi. It's grief he never recovered from. Daphne. He said it once, to the only person he ever expected to, and losing her consumed his life — "There had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone. Daphne was the person he would have told." That sentence wrecked me. And the logic he built out of it — say it and loss destroys you; withhold it and loss stays survivable — is exactly the kind of false protection a lonely fifteen-year-old would devise and a grown man would keep wearing without noticing. He taught himself the wrong lesson and Vee is paying tuition on it.

The scene on the porch gets recast completely now. From her side it read as failure, maybe proof she was only sex. From his: "He had wanted to answer her. The true answer had been there, whole and ready... Then he had chosen the smaller truth." He knew. He knew exactly what she offered and exactly what his answer cost her, and he was afraid, and he was weak, and he admits it — "at the moment she had asked him to be brave he had been weak." That honesty, offered only to himself, makes me ache for both of them at once. Vee put her sentence in the drawer of unspeakable things, and Pace is sobbing over a cooling bowl of vacuum-sealed chicken because a truck on the highway didn't turn into his drive. Neither of them knows the other is drowning in the same water.

My feeling about Pace moved, a lot. Last chapters he was slipping into something suspect — the man who wouldn't warm in public, who said "words are cheap" with his arms folded. Here he's a hungover man brushing whiskey-sickness off his teeth, eating food that is "everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted," and the house he overheats for her is cold enough to chill his palm. The bleach smell in the hall, the mop and bucket left where they fell — he drank himself sick over her and cleaned it up alone. That's not a man playing games. That's a man whose one survival strategy has become the thing costing him the person he loves. I trust his tenderness more now, even as I trust his judgment less.

The detail work is doing quiet violence. "Prison food" — the ex who laughed at his bowl, kindly, and drifted away with "no part of the world altered by it." That's his whole romantic history in one clause: survivable, forgettable, nothing like this. And the engine on the highway — his body gathering itself toward the drive before his mind catches up, the fork stopping mid-air, then "The truck went past" and he breaks. That's the most naked we've ever seen him, and there's no one in the room to see it.

Erotically there's nothing here and it doesn't matter — this is the emotional inverse of the sex chapters, and the book earned the contrast. The heat of the whole book makes his grief legible: we know exactly what "watching her arrive" is worth because we've been him, waiting on that stoop, for forty chapters.

The title lands like a verdict. "Not Enough" — his answer on the porch was not enough; his cooking, his warmth, his presence, all the done-without-saying, not enough; and underneath, the fear that *he* is not enough, that saying it and still losing her is the wound he can't afford twice. And the cover-board title, *With a Long Spoon*, keeps echoing for me — the old saying about supping with the devil — though I'm still not sure who's holding the long spoon in this book. This volume's "A Polite Invitation" framing feels like it's quietly curdling as the concealed structures strain.

What I want next: I need Vee and Pace in a room again, and I dread it, because the fix is one sentence long and neither of them can say it. And Randi is coming back to campus with her own unsaid thing. (Guess from here: the silence won't break cleanly — someone will say the word at the wrong person or the wrong time.)
