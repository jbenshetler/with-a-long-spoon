## Reader reaction

**The felt read**

This one got under my guard in a way the last few hadn't. After the taproom chapter and its boozy, shoulder-warm wreckage, I wasn't expecting the morning after to be the one that actually cracks something open — but that's exactly what happens, and it happens at a breakfast table over cinnamon raisin toast, of all places.

The opening is all caretaking, and it landed on me like devotion rendered in miniature: the glass of ice water with the ice melted to slivers, "a folded square of paper towel he'd put down so it wouldn't mark the wood" — that paper towel square undid me. Who puts a paper towel under a glass he's leaving for a sick girl? Pace does. It's his whole love language in one move: the person anticipated, the wood protected, the mess pre-absorbed. The two brown pills administered "patient as a man watering something," the undershirt drawn over her head to the knees, "his mouth, once, on her forehead, in the dark, after he thought she was already gone" — a kiss spent only when it can't be witnessed. As always with him, the tenderness is maximal and the speech is zero, and I keep swooning at the first and aching at the second. The bread that "had not existed when the world ended last night" — scratch loaf, cooling rack, while she sleeps — had me grinning at "You made bread."

And then the little shift: "I'd love to meet your other friends," and the whole chapter pivots into one of the more quietly devastating confessions the book has given him. "Sheri's my friend. My one friend. There isn't anyone else." I heard the floor of Vee's world tilt with hers: "Nobody had one friend. One friend wasn't a number a person had." His sociology of it — women misunderstanding friendship into awkwardness; men only able to befriend by competing between thirteen and fifteen, inside "the pack," a door "on a timer" — is delivered with the flat, worn attention of a man handing over a handled fact, and it works on me both as sympathy and as that trademark Pace thing: the essay delivered where most people would just flinch. (I do note the book gives him a touch of lecture-voice even here, a small thumb-on-the-scale I felt for about two sentences before the truth of the thing took over.)

The doubling I couldn't help catching, though — and the page does earn it for readers carrying what I carry: "Women misunderstand it... the friendship. It gets awkward." The exact attentiveness that makes friendship impossible is also the instrument by which Vee was courted in the pointing game. And when he says "my one friend," he is, to Vee's ear, confessing loneliness — and to my ear, erasing Randi from his social map in speech, which he has to, because Vee doesn't know what Randi still is to him. The loneliness is plainly real; the erasure is also load-bearing. Both, at once. This book keeps handing me those.

The gut of it is "either weren't safe or weren't welcoming," said of the older boys and men around the fifteen-year-old, and Vee doing "the arithmetic she hadn't been asked to do." It's the closest the book has come to naming the father: *went bad with his daddy* (Sheri), *less than staying*, *I was not a good fit where I was* — and now *not safe*, slid under flatness, no name required. It moved me, genuinely; I felt the wound open a notch further, and it complicates rather than cancels what I know about how she entered his life. The two sit there like Vee's two stacked truths about Daphne — "the best two years he'd had" and "the wrong shape it had actually been... both true at once, and it hurt to hold them both." She inventories Meg, Kayla, Cassie, Randi — "One person couldn't be all of that" — and realizes he had the one (Daphne), then the none. I love that she doesn't say sorry; she "crossed the kitchen and put herself against him, both arms, all the way around," deliberately echoing Sheri's taproom hug, cheek flat, whole weight committed. And he receives it in his way: "he wasn't happy and he wasn't sad. He was somewhere else with it, thinking," chin on her hair, looking past her. The hug doesn't become catharsis; the bread cools; nothing is fixed. I trust that refusal.

My feeling for Pace moved toward the softest it's been in a while; for Vee, toward respect for how she handles him; the suspicion I carry stays where it was — a low hum under the tenderness, because I know the warmth is genuine and the concealment is structural. Sheri's remembered once-over ("she looked at me like—" / "That way too. She can be like that") gives the morning one tiny ember of charge, and Vee's "I liked it, honestly" keeps threading her comfort being enjoyed by women.

**Titles.** "Hangover" does double duty plainly and well: it's the literal morning after the taproom, and it's the residual truth that surfaces once the revelry wears off — the after-effects of a night, a party, and a boyhood. From the cover-board I have only the volume line: **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION** (no separate tagline shown in this packet). "A Warm Reception" keeps proving itself as the house's heat and the care as much as the roadhouse welcome, while "WITH A LONG SPOON" still reads as the warning about supping with dangerous company — every chapter of closeness a longer spoon that somehow never stays long enough. The remembered jacket line ("every yes was freely given") hangs over this one too: caretaking given freely, and the ache of what isn't said.

**What I want next:** the father's shape moving toward naming (marked as a guess from here); the Saturday dance hall Sheri demanded; and the triangle's reckoning, which the "one friend" omission only makes heavier. I dread the moment Vee has to do arithmetic she can't undo.
