## Reader reaction

I opened this book expecting to meet Vee, and instead the first person I'm inside is Randi — except I don't know her name until the very end, when Pace says "There you are, Randi," and it lands like a key turning (the book keeps using lock imagery, and it's doing something to me). For most of the chapter she's just "she," a sorority girl with startling blue eyes and a face she has "built her whole adult life around being looked at for," and by the time her name arrives I understand why the name had to wait: the whole chapter is about the difference between the face and the person.

The swoon is real and I want to say so plainly. Pace is — on the page, to me — enormously attractive: the sliced honeycrisp, cut sides up because she eats them faster that way; the henley; the furniture he built; "he backed off and let her decide." The undressing scene, kneeling, folding each garment, is one of the most tender things I've read, and the bench scene itself is patient, consensual, check-ins and safeword and water left on the nightstand in advance. The heat works on me — the long withholding, her begging arriving in a voice "almost ugly" and real, moved me as much as it aroused me. The orgasm being "hers in a way nothing he had done with her had ever been" — I felt that.

And then the chapter turns, and this is where it gets its teeth into me. Her flinch at "You're beautiful." The bathroom lock clicking — "not a thing she had ever done before in his house." The mirror passage, where she decides, "quiet as a deadbolt going over," that the self he found is "going back behind it, and she was not coming out again." That devastated me. He thinks this was a breakthrough; she has just experienced it as a breach she must never allow again. And he sits on the bed "quietly happy about her," still hard, setting it down because "that's not what this night was for" — decent, tender, and completely wrong about what just happened.

Do I trust Pace? Mostly, on the page — his care reads genuine, and the Daphne memory (he once went through a closed door and it cost him) explains his restraint without making it saintly. But the jacket whispered in my ear the whole time: two people planned the best year of *her* life, and she doesn't know there's a plan. Reading this chapter with that in my pocket, his attentiveness — the apple slices, the noticing — has a double exposure. I don't have page-earned suspicion of him yet; the text plays him straight. But I notice he "kept that off his face" when being directed pleased him, and I notice the bench was built *for her* before she knew she wanted it. Filed, not concluded.

The blurb says Randi steers Vee toward Pace — so Randi and Pace being lovers themselves, with this depth of history, complicates everything in a way I'm hungry to understand. Is what I just watched part of the "game between the two people now closest to her"? A guess from here, marked as a guess.

The title "The Bench" is exactly right — the object that is "not only a table," that sits in the room "full of what it was," waiting. The book's title, *A Polite Invitation*, now reads to me as the bench itself: an invitation extended before she knew there was one. The series title *With a Long Spoon* — supping with the devil, presumably — and the tagline "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" both feel aimed directly at what I just watched: her yes was real, and something is still wrong.

Friction: almost none. The chapter is long and earns its length. If anything, the interior summary near the end ("Nothing happened to her. That was what the face was for") edges toward over-explaining what the mirror scene had already shown me — but I forgave it because the prose kept finding fresh phrasing.

What I want next: Vee. And to find out what Randi and Pace are to each other beyond this — and whether her resolve in the bathroom holds.
