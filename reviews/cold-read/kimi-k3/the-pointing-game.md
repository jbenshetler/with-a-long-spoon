## Reader reaction

**The felt read.**

This chapter swept me up and left me uneasy in almost the same breath, and I think that's exactly where the book wants me. The opening in bed is gorgeous — "Would you like to taste another woman?" landing in her body before it lands in her, her coming *at the question* — and I felt it, the shock of a desire too fast to costume. Pace reading it, treasuring it, "He took the body's answer for the true one. He usually did." I swooned at that and also noticed myself noting it: *he usually did.* There's a man who trusts his readings of her more than her words, and the book keeps handing me both the tenderness of that and its danger at once.

The walk on the quad is where the chapter really got me. Randi coming alive by degrees — the scanning, the coil in her frame, "She would have told him she was being a good sport about his game; she had stopped being a good sport a quarter mile back. She was shopping." I loved watching her want things in daylight, loved the "Maybe" for the girl on the library steps, loved Pace kissing her with her own taste still on his mouth. And then Vee — the copper hair, the laugh with her head thrown all the way back, "laughing like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen." Of course that's the one. She's everything Randi's grammar forbids and everything Randi is starving for, and Randi's bare "Yes" with nothing around it — I felt that in my chest.

And here's where my trust snags. Because Vee is a real person who doesn't know she's in this. The staged collision — three seconds of execution so clean the *redhead* apologizes — is dazzling and faintly chilling: Randi's whole training folded into a predatory grace, deployed on someone who thinks she bumped into a stranger. Pace at his light pole, "the gift was moving toward her hands" — a gift with a face and a stats class and no idea she's been selected. Nobody has done anything cruel yet, and the desire is genuinely Randi's own — but the text earned my unease fair and square with that last exchange: "Do you think she'll like the bench?" God. That line reframes the entire game retroactively. The bench wasn't only for Randi. Or — it was, and now it's waiting, "full of what it was," and Randi is already imagining a third body on it. I don't know whether to be thrilled or alarmed, and I'm both.

Pace himself — I like him and I don't fully trust him, and I can't separate those anymore. "It was the best idea he'd had in a month" is so self-pleased; he chose the wall "for the angle," he orchestrated the entire afternoon while letting her believe the choosing was hers. Is it hers? The wanting is — the book is insistent and convincing on that — but the *shape* of everything is his. He's the kindest puppeteer I've ever read, and after the bench chapter and the bathroom lock-click and Randi's frozen morning-after in "Standards," I can't read his warmth as simple anymore. He asked her in bed, mid-act, when her defenses were down. He says he waited to re-ask until she was "gathered back into herself," and that's considerate — and it's also stage management.

The title is perfect and does double work: the pointing game is literal, but pointing is also what you do when you assign — to pick out, to indicate, to accuse. And it gave me nothing away; it only opened up once I was inside it. The cover board — WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION — clicks into place now in a way it didn't before: supping with the devil requires a long spoon, and someone is about to receive a polite invitation they don't understand the terms of. I'm guessing from here that Vee gets invited into something she consents to in form while missing the architecture — and I dread and want it equally.

What I want next: Vee as a person, not a sighting. I want to know if she has any gravity of her own against these two, because right now Randi and Pace together are a closed system of formidable skill — her aim, his patience — and Vee is outmatched before she's met. I'm completely pulled in. The erotic engine and the ethical quease are the same engine, and the book knows it.
