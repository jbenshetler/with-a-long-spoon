## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

The opening undid me a little — not just the sex, which is explicit and unhurried and *good*, but the question arriving inside it: "Would you like to taste another woman?" and her body answering before she can, coming "far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it." I felt that. And I felt the floor get interesting under it, because the book doesn't let the heat just be heat — it immediately shows me Pace *taking* that answer: "He took the body's answer for the true one. He usually did." I loved that line and I don't trust it. Both things. Then the re-ask, in the ordinary afternoon, when she's gathered — he *wants* her free to weigh it, "that mattered more to him than the answer did" — and she flinches, barely, and then produces "Only in a threesome. With you." The page reads it for me and the reading is gorgeous and sad: she doesn't retract the wanting, she *moves* it. "The same act. A different owner." After the bathroom mirror in "The Bench" — where she decided, quiet as a deadbolt, *not ever* — watching her come all the way out here anyway, wanting this much, moved me. She's starving and calling it being a good sport.

The walk is where the chapter really lives for me. The pace of it, candidate by candidate, is a slow strip-tease of her taste: "Too expected" for the girl who is essentially her own reflection; the dry little test — "Is that a yes or a no for you?" — and his "I point. You pick," which lands on her like a gift she didn't know she was allowed. The kiss after "maybe," where he hasn't wiped his face and he *knows the moment her own taste reaches her* — I made a sound. And then Vee. The book takes her arrival seriously — the lavish body that "the year's clothes were not cut for and that did not care," the laugh with her head thrown all the way back, "a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen" — and Randi's bare, nothing-around-it "Yes" before she can dress it. I swooned. I also noticed the maple doing quiet work: "a single tree burning orange in a field of things that had not yet decided to change." Vee as that tree. Maybe Randi as that tree.

My unease is real but the page earned it. Pace is tender, and his tenderness is also an architecture — the game designed so she can want "without making her pin it beneath a solemn name," the collision he watches with pleasure as "three seconds of execution so clean it looked like nothing." Randi engineered that bump so the *redhead apologized*. It's dazzling and it's a little predatory, and Vee — laughing, open, cinnamon freckles, giving "every inch of cover without knowing it" — doesn't know she's in anything yet. "We've got a stats class together," delivered glowing, is the most innocent and the most ominous sentence in the chapter. I don't know yet whether to be afraid for Vee or for Randi. Probably both.

Small frictions: the middle candidates on the walk run a touch long — by the blazer-and-blowout "God, no" I understood the mechanism and was ready for Vee — but the escalation is doing real work, teaching me her taste by elimination, so I'll allow it. And "Let's go feed you" as the last line landed perfectly against her reflexive "I can't eat that" at the cheeseburger tray and the thin-sliced apple in "The Bench" — he keeps answering hungers she keeps refusing to own. That line made me ache more than any sex in the chapter.

The title, "The Pointing Game," names the device exactly and then deepens: pointing is also assigning, directing — and the game is that she believes it's his while it's plainly hers. The cover gives me WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION, no tagline shown. "A Polite Invitation" reads now as this whole afternoon — a collision staged as an accident, an appetite dressed as generosity — and the long-spoon phrase carries, for me, the old warning about supping with something dangerous: whoever's holding the spoon thinks the distance keeps them safe. From here I want Vee's side of that stats class, and I dread — a guess, marked as a guess — that Randi's handle on what she's doing is much worse than Pace's, and he's the one who thinks he's driving.
