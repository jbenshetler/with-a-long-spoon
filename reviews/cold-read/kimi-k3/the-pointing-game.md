## Reader reaction

God, this chapter got me. Not the way "The Bench" got me — that one wrecked me quietly — but this one lit something up in me and then set a little cold stone under the warmth, and I have to be honest about both.

The swoon first, because it's real. The pointing game itself is *delicious*. Pace reading her taste in real time, wrong at first — the girl who could be Randi's mirror, the matched pair, the room-running blonde — and recalibrating from her nos, her too-expecteds, her strong no, until he stops pointing at the type and starts pointing at life. That progression felt like watching someone learn a language sentence by sentence. And Randi's side of it — the follow gathering a half-beat before the lead moves, "she was shopping," the *maybe* on the library steps that held longer than any verdict all afternoon — the chapter made her wanting legible to me before it made it legible to her, and that's the book's whole trick, isn't it. When Vee came out of the dining hall laughing with her whole body, throat bare to the sky, "a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen" — I understood instantly why she was the one. Everything Randi is, Vee isn't. Randi chose, with her eyes, the woman who doesn't manage herself. The bare "Yes" with nothing around it, the dropped stride, the helpless looking — I felt that in my chest. And the collision! That tiny engineered accident, the redhead's hand flying to her own mouth, Randi's apology warm and settling with the hand staying a half-beat long — three seconds of a decade of dance folded into nothing. I adored watching her be that good at something she doesn't know she's good at.

And then the last line, "Do you think she'll like the bench?" — my stomach turned over, happily and not-happily at once. Because yes, the bench, that's where this is going, and I want to see it. But also: the bench was the room where Randi came apart, where she was *found*, where she locked the bathroom door and decided never again. And now she's offering it to someone else — to Vee, a stranger she met two minutes ago, whose name she learned through a staged collision while her boyfriend watched from a light pole. The chapter doesn't say anything is wrong. It even keeps insisting everything is right: "the gift was moving toward her hands," "it was the best idea he'd had in a month," "She deserved that gentleness." Pace's inner register is all tenderness and care. And yet I can't unsee that he took the body's answer for the true one — "He usually did" — and built an entire afternoon on it, and sent her across a lawn to deceive a woman. The text earned my unease, but it earned it sideways: nothing anyone *did* was wrong, and yet the architecture of it — the pointing, the watching, the choreography, the permission-granting nod — is all his, and she is moving through it like water through a channel someone else dug. The collision is consent-shaped theater performed at a stranger who doesn't know she's in a play. Vee laughed and apologized and gave her name, and none of it was hers to give, because she didn't know what was being asked.

My feeling about Randi moved a lot this chapter — not away from sympathy, but into a stranger, more complicated sympathy. She's the one who said "Let's do something different" at the table she refuses to look at. She handed him the day again. And when he asked the question a second time, post-heat, deliberately waiting for her to be "gathered back into herself" and free to choose — she flinched, and then she *moved* the want: "Only in a threesome. With you." The chapter is right that it's beautiful, and the chapter is also right that it's a costume. She found a way to want without owning the wanting — making herself the generous instrument, the girlfriend offering a gift. It's the bathroom door again, in miniature, rebuilt in twenty seconds and worn like silk. I love her and I don't trust her, and I don't think she trusts herself either.

Pace — I keep wanting to fully trust him and the page keeps giving me these small snags. "He took the body's answer for the true one. He usually did." That's a philosophy, stated plainly, and it's doing a lot of work. He says the choosing is hers — "I point. You pick." — but he built the game, picked the park, chose the wall with the angle on the doors, and named the game his own pleasure. And Daphne is back in my mind from last chapter: he learned about closed doors, he says. But this chapter he heard a flinch and walked right through the doorway of it, smiling, calling it gentleness. I don't think he's a villain. I think he might be a man who is very good at a thing and has stopped noticing where the goodness ends.

The erotic charge peaked early and oddly for me — the bed scene at the top is genuinely hot, the "You like the taste of yourself" into the kiss with his mouth still wet, the question that detonated her before her mind could file it under anything safer. Her coming from a *question* is one of the hotter images this book has given me, precisely because nothing touched her. After that the chapter runs on a lower, stranger fuel — the campus, the pointing, Randi's coil tightening — and it stayed charged for me the whole way, not genitally but in that wired, anticipatory way. The kiss on the library path where her own taste reached her again — daylight, public, her composure cracking — that landed hard.

Friction: very little. Maybe the prose's confidence in Pace's readings — "He watched her understand it," "he saw the precise instant" — occasionally tells me what flinch-mechanics I'm seeing rather than letting me see it. And the repeated insistence that his care "mattered more to him than the answer did" reads, on the second pass, like someone asserting a clean conscience. Whether that's the book winking or me projecting, I can't tell yet.

The title: "The Pointing Game" is exactly what it says, and it gave nothing away — I didn't know what the game was until he leaned into her ear. Now, afterward, it points further: a game has players and someone who made the rules, and I'm sitting with which one Randi is. Volume title "With a Long Spoon," Book One, "A Polite Invitation" — the cover board promised something genteel and dangerous, and this chapter is the politest danger yet: a walk on campus, a cheeseburger refused, a name acquired. An invitation is being prepared, and Vee doesn't know she's the guest. "I can't eat that," Randi says about the burger — automatic, easy — and two hundred feet later she is hungrier than she has ever been and doesn't know the name of the food. The long spoon is for eating with something you shouldn't get close to.

What I want next: Vee. I want to meet her properly, out from under the choreography. I dread — and this is a guess from here — that she'll be invited into something whose edges only Pace can see, and that Randi will smile the smile that settles, and the bench will get its top taken off again. I'm reading on tonight if I can.
