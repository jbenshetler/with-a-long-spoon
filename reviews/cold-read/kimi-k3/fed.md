## Reader reaction

This was the scene I'd been both wanting and dreading since the lunch — Randi getting Friday — and it was worse than I feared, in exactly the way this book has trained me to fear: nothing in it is wrong. That's what's wrong. Randi is warm, funny, self-deprecating; she pays first with her "sad little story," she mirrors the glow, she asks only what any best friend would ask, and when Vee's one real flicker of fear surfaces, Randi catches it and hands back the kindest possible reframe. To Vee it's "the easiest thing in Vee's week." To me it read as procedure, start to finish, and the gap between those two readings is now the book's whole engine.

The beats that got me, in order:

The money. Quiet for two chapters, and it roars back in three strokes — Vee reading the right side of the menu "the way she read a road in fog," the arithmetic "that ended in Thursday," Randi ordering "without looking at the card at all." Then the second mimosa, "and the second one she did not price at all." Tiny, and it chilled me. The budget dissolving inside the hospitality. *Fed* indeed.

Randi's old-boyfriend story. On its face, charming. But I know whose lover Randi is, so a beautiful ex who's "in such a hurry, like the building's on fire" reads as invention or cover, and either way its function is naked: her whole weekend disposed of in one paragraph, clearing the table — and buying the right to ask. "I'm running on fumes here. How was he?" That's not confession; that's priming the pump. And Vee thinks Randi is the one who makes "everything that had cost her into a story that had cost her nothing." Gorgeous line. I wanted to shake her.

The plum. "Why'd you pick it?" Vee can't answer — the answer is the silk, the dress, his sun porch — so her face answers instead. But Randi already extracted the silk account at the lunch. She may well know exactly what that color is. She asked anyway and watched the face arrive: "That face isn't about a color, is it." Every question Randi asks might be one she already holds the answer to; the brunch is an audit dressed as a girlfriend session. Even the venue — a sun porch, glass, light that "lay on everything without asking" — rhymes with the room where the measuring happened. Randi picked it. Randi picks all of them.

The fear. The one true moment: "how much of herself she'd handed over without ever once deciding to, and a thread of fear went through her at it and showed before she could catch it." Vee fails to catch it. Randi catches it. And says — I had to stop reading for a second — "You're not caught, sweetheart. You're lucky." *Caught.* The trap's own word, spoken by its architect, to dissolve the mark's single clear look at the trap. The coldest warm line in the book so far.

And then the climax: "I wanted it. And I took it. For once." "Good." — and the word settles into her and stays. The tagline, verbatim, as the scene's emotional peak. The horror is that it's also true — she did want it, she did take it — and the truth is the mechanism. The curb kiss, met this time, no scanning the street. "See you soon, gorgeous." The liturgy's closing cadence, and Vee now sings her part unprompted.

Friction: almost none; the chapter glides. One small snag — Vee has wanted to tell this "all week," which quietly re-times the book; I'd had the overnight as Friday and the Cassie morning as Saturday, so a week slid past me. Minor.

The title means everything after the fact: everyone in this chapter feeds — Randi on the story, Vee on the blessing, the mimosas on Vee's Thursday — and the one being fattened does all the chewing herself. The book's title: the brunch is the polite invitation; so is every question Randi asks. The tagline has graduated from warning to procedure. Next (guessing from here): Pace and Randi on one page — the sharpest pull in the book, now sharper, because I suspect today's file has a destination. The dress is confirmed still being made, and the phrase is staggering: "the dress he was still making her out of it." Not for her. *Out of her.*
