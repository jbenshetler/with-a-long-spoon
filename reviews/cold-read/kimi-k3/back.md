## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

After the full orchestration of "Missed a Spot," this chapter played in a chamber register and it absolutely worked on me — quietly, then all at once. The scene is nothing: Vee naked on a lacquered chair Pace made, wine in hand, talking at his back while he does the dishes. "His back was a good listener. All of him was, but the back made it easy." That one sentence tells me she's built a whole confessional practice into their domestic shape, and the fact that Pace listens turned away — no face to manage, no performance demanded of him — is exactly why she runs on past what she meant to say. I believed every inch of it.

The swoon came in two beats. First the banter: "You'd have worn the sweater out of spite. It was lavender." I laughed out loud. Their post-reconciliation couple-ease is so well earned; she dallies with a hypothetical him, and he deadpans into the sink. Second, and deeper: "Your heel came up out of the right one when you pushed off." And: "And Kayla's the one who can talk the paint off a wall?" I felt what Vee felt — something "she would have been embarrassed to describe out loud." He has kept *everything*, all the floor-news and half-sentences she's run on at his back all fall. This is the book's recurring form of Pace's inarticulate devotion made literal: the man who can't say it keeps her friends' names on a shelf. And it's handed back to her in his "flat certain voice" while scrubbing. I melted the same way she did.

But the reader-knowledge hums under the warmth, and the book leans into it: "It arrived as *Randi*. Of course she did." My stomach did something complicated there — Vee's delight at "Properly, I mean. You two said about nine words at the coffee shop" is the dramatic irony at its cruelest and sweetest. She wants her two favorite people to finally find out about each other, and I know they've been lovers all along. Her picture of the dinner — "Randi in the middle of them all" — is a live wire. I want this gathering to happen and I dread it in equal measure: either something cracks at that table, or Randi performs strangerhood, and Randi is a flawless performer.

The Cassie complication stopped me in the best way. Vee runs the seating chart forward and can't find a restaurant where neither woman has to be "a good sport" — Randi's real-napkin world would seat Cassie "in yesterday's shirt under a pendant light," Cassie's world can't hold "Randi's hands on the menu." That's such tender, precise class-consciousness in a girl who's been carried into sorority-glamour all year. And then: "She'd watched somebody be fine about it before. She wasn't going to look at when." The book dangles the averted memory for a page and then lets it surface anyway as a picture: the bar with games on three walls, Pace squaring his fork to the table edge, *"I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you."* The failed Chili's outing. She won't look at it, and then it stands there until she's seen it — involuntary memory rendered exactly right. What this plan is, underneath: a second attempt at the one public thing that died in December. And this time she hands him the venue: "Will you pick the place?" "Let me handle it."

Which prickles, beautifully. "Let me handle it" lands on a reader who watched Randi handle the coat plan, handle the whole reconciliation. On the page itself Pace's offer is pure care; my unease is imported from what I carry, and I can feel the difference. Still — his lacquered-chair hospitality, "Tables have ends. Somebody ends up at one," the man refusing literal and social furniture that assigns somebody the low seat — this is the isolation-is-his-door axis starting to move. He's bidding to bring her whole world in: Meg, Meg's Danny, Kayla, Cassie planted at the edge with arms crossed, even Theo, whose existence she fumbles an unnecessary explanation for while Pace just reaches for the next pan. "Bring Theo" with zero interrogation; the question she braced for never comes. His non-jealousy is consistent to the point of architecture.

And Randi-in-absentia is perfectly characterized by the sweater story: "She just keeps on being delighted with him… She let him feel like it was his idea." Vee narrates Randi's exact method — the one being used on *her*, the one that dressed her and sent her to his door — as charming anecdote, admiringly, to Randi's partner. She can see the mechanism anywhere but in her own mirror. That ache is the book's signature, and it landed.

**Titles.** "Back" gave nothing away; reading it, I take it as the scene's governing image — his turned back as the condition of her openness — but also the rupture's picture coming back to her unbidden, and maybe deliberately her people coming back into a life that had narrowed to one house. The volume title "A Warm Reception" (series: "With a Long Spoon") now reads as pointing at a literal reception: a gathering, a welcome — and I hear the proverb's echo, supping with the devil wants a long spoon, and everyone here keeps supping together warmly. The plan of a party makes that title feel like it might be the sprint-line of this book-from-here.

**Next:** my guess-from-here is Pace hosts rather than rents — "tables have ends" suggests he solves it by not having a table, cooking for them all, ending the her-people/his-door divide. I want the party. I dread Randi at it with Cassie's watchful flat eyes present. I want Randi's face-off-page reaction when Vee tells her "he wants to meet you properly." Pull to keep reading: high.
