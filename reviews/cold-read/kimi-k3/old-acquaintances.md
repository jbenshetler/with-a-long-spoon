## Reader reaction

This one hurt in the quiet way — no confrontation, nothing even *happens*, and I put it down feeling hollowed out. It's the same night I just spent inside Randi's head, seen now from the other cold bed, and the rhyme is brutal: both of them lying awake, both of them holding the phone like it's the only warm object in the room, both of them sending the other a cover story polished to pass inspection.

**Vee** has never been more sympathetic to me. The childhood room is devastating — the synchro trophies, "the little gold girls frozen mid-lift," the ribbon bunting, the room that "kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed." She's outgrown the bed, the house, Vivienne, and she hasn't grown *into* anywhere yet: "She had no answer that would fit in this house. She was no longer sure she had one that fit anywhere." That line is the chapter. And the detail that broke me: the induction photo, the one where she's "all of her... handed to the frame at once" — she sent it to Pace whole, and for her mother's phone *she cut the leg off herself*. "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it." Nobody asked her to, this time. She did the cropping. That's the whole tragedy of this book in one gesture.

**Pace**, absent entirely, somehow aches more than present-Pace. "He left you the room. He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now." The silence has stretched the whole break and she has three maybes and no standing to ask even one question. And then she *invents* the blonde — "built her from spite," tan, fashion-model body, "where Vee's was a stripper's" — and hands her the fra diavolo night, the sheet, the garlic blooming, her own sacred memory evicting her. "She could not keep her own night." That's the cruelest beat in the book so far, and she does it to herself. I want to be clear with myself: there is not one line of evidence for the blonde. The page even tells me so — "what you believe at midnight." The Thanksgiving Sheri dinner is real but she folded it away as pity and I have no reason to unfold it; the Christmas week is a blank her dread filled. My suspicion dial didn't move. My heart just broke.

**Randi**, seen from the outside this time, is flawless — which is to say, her cover is flawless, and I alone know what it cost. The feed is "another country," and Vee *knows it's poisoned* and wants it anyway: "even a poisoned Gstaad was more life than she had." And the reply text landing at 11:52 — I watched Randi write that fairytale from beside a spent stranger with her phone turned facedown, and now I watch Vee receive it as proof of a wonderful life, feel *grateful* — "Randi had taken the time" — and look forward to "a face that would light up and call her gorgeous and mean it." Mean it. The doubled irony there is almost physically painful, because Randi *does* mean it, in the way Vee can't hear.

The pull of the chapter isn't swoon, it's ache — the deleted "*are you*—" taken back letter by letter; the scroll through old messages "the way you worry a sore tooth"; midnight arriving as "a few small poppers... a dog bark," the most anti-fairytale New Year imaginable against Randi's choreographed one. The near-warmth: her reaching deliberately for the best night to be warm, and losing it from inside. No erotic charge to speak of — the fra diavolo memory carries sensual heat (the slipping sheet, "the evening having used her well") but it's retrospective, and the fear steals it before it can even work on her.

No friction. If anything the chapter earns its length completely; it's one woman in one bed for forty minutes and I wanted it longer. The parents' beige love — casseroles that "had never once been the thing Vee was hungry for, it was love... she just hadn't had the word for it then" — reframes her whole childhood in the book's hunger-vocabulary without saying so.

The title, **Old Acquaintances** — it took me a second and then it spread everywhere: it's the Auld Lang Syne question, *should old acquaintance be forgot*, playing under a chapter where she's deciding nothing and dreading the answer. Old acquaintances: the room and Vivienne ("the girl who was gone"), the corkboard friends who never text, the bartender's and Randi's old reassurances that "went straight through her hands" now that she doesn't know she's anything, and Pace himself — the year's acquaintance the song asks about at midnight. It didn't give anything away; it waited until I'd read it and then deepened. As for *A Polite Invitation* — her text to Randi is one, engineered to demand nothing, and it gets back exactly what politeness buys. *With a Long Spoon* keeps feeling like the shape of all of it: everyone in this book reaching toward someone across a distance they're keeping deliberately.

Next: term starts, Randi comes back — "the one sure thing the new year held" — and I both want and dread that brunch, two women each freshly wrecked, each congratulating the other on her cover. And the silence with Pace: someone has to break first, and she already deleted her one attempt. *Guess from here:* the reunion chapter, and Randi's cover will be so good it makes Vee lonelier.
