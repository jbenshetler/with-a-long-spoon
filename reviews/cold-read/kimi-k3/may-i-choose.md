## Reader reaction

This is the chapter where the book's tagline stopped being marketing and started being a voice in my ear. On the page, taken straight, this is the best date I have ever read. Showered and shaved "for this," boots "worn but polished," street-side without making anything of it, a hidden block "he just knew," a waitress whose face eases at him. And then — "May I choose?" Three words, and I felt her relief in my own shoulders. He asks permission to order for her and treats the permission as weighty: weighs the page, asks the single question that matters ("was there anything she wouldn't eat"), builds the meal unhurried. The kiss-rule beat is even better. She says no — half-meant, the reflex talking — and he does nothing. No lean, no lobby. "He just smiled, like she'd told him something true and fine about herself, and let it close." And *that* is what breaks the rule: not pressure, its absence. She has to pull him taut on her own hand to make the exception. "I'm making an exception" — the chapter ends there, before the kiss, and I made a sound out loud.

But I have the jacket. So every free yes rang double. He asks may-I-choose over a *menu*, having already chosen her. The place, the block, the dishes in a language she can't read, the route home, the long way — every element of the evening is his design, and the text hands her the exact wrong lesson to draw from it: "she was already learning she could trust where he was taking her." And then, the line that I think is the whole trap stated in her own voice, delivered as a delight: "content for once not to know." Not-knowing rendered delicious, chapter after chapter — the froyo system, now the "beautiful nonsense" of the menu, "one more thing he knew that she didn't." The book keeps making ignorance taste good. That's the author's thumb, if anything is, and I don't even mind; I think it's the point. The hand-hold wrecked me: "less like something beginning than something resolving — like her hand had been wrong all evening, all its life maybe, for want of his exactly." A first hand-hold written as destiny. And I sat there thinking: *that's what a choreographed environment feels like from inside. It feels like fate.*

Pace: still zero interiority, but we got the closest thing yet — "He'd been somewhere in himself; when she came out, he came back." He has a somewhere. Also the right-hand rule, "always the right, never the other," a law he keeps without seeming to think about it — visible lawfulness, unreadable law. I find him more attractive this chapter and I trust him less, and neither has anything to do with anything he did wrong, because he did nothing wrong. That's the vertigo this book is building in me.

Vee deepened again. The brace is finally named as a whole system: "that low background readiness she carried everywhere, the part of her always braced for the bill of a thing to come due" — and it went quiet at his table, without her noticing, exactly as the watcher went quiet at the froyo shop. He doesn't fight her defenses; he renders them unemployed. The drip-lick moment — the quick unthinking tongue, "the food was meant for hands" — was the erotic peak of the chapter for me, more than the almost-kiss. She lost the thread and *let it stay lost*, and the book quietly rehearses the ending in it: "let the thread go," twice, once over dinner and once under the tree.

Cassie is the warm floor of the book. "That's a face. That's a whole face." She teases Vee about everything; "she didn't tease her about this." And her verdict — "That's not a boy. That's a man." — lands as both true and, to me, faintly alarming: the audit-voice, charmed. Also, one small seam I won't lose: Vee grinned at the dark screen "in a way she'd have died to have anyone see. Anyone but Cassie." She hides the depth of the wanting from the person who knows her best. Why? The page doesn't say, and I noticed.

Randi: absent for the second straight chapter, unmentioned this time. Vee processed the biggest night of her romantic life — the text, the dress verdicts, the debrief-to-come — without the jacket's "best friend" anywhere in it, and I don't believe that silence is accidental. Somewhere offstage there is a woman who will hear how Saturday went, and the man she shares a bed with is the one who made it go that way. My dread has a shape now and it's the debrief.

The tea glasses did me in — "small ones, set into little cages of worked brass... so you could hold them while they were still too hot to hold." I put the book down at that. A structure whose whole purpose is to let you hold heat you couldn't otherwise hold. That's him. That's the whole design. I don't think the page made me reach for that; I think it placed it in my hand, warm, just bearable.

Titles: "May I Choose" illuminates the chapter directly, and then recolors — by the end it's also *her* question, may *she* choose, and she does, twice (yes-please, and the exception). It didn't give anything away. *A Polite Invitation* — this dinner is the title object: "Saturday. Dinner. He didn't say where, and he didn't ask her to answer that night." Politeness as engineering, freedom with the quiet built in. *With a Long Spoon* keeps whispering its old proverb at me — supping with the devil — and this is the book's first full meal, eaten with bare hands, no spoon at all, long or otherwise. Tagline: three yeses this chapter, all free. The trap-clause still has no visible teeth, and I've stopped expecting to see them — I'm starting to think that's what the trap is.

Next: the kiss, presumably, where the chapter so deliberately cut. Then Randi. I want and dread the "how was it?" scene in equal measure. Guesses from here: Pace tells her everything, or tells her nothing and she reads it anyway; and Cassie is going to watch Vee come home lit from inside and say nothing at all.
