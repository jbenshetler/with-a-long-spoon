## Reader reaction

This chapter undid me, and it undid me *knowing what I know* — which is the whole strange ache of it. From inside Vee, this is the best first date I have ever read. The text that doesn't need an answer that night; the phone pressed to her chest; "he just left it with me. Like it could keep." The lobby, where she gets one second of him to himself — the damp hair, the jaw still blushing from the razor, boots *kept* rather than bought. "May I choose?" and the relief of handing it over. The bright dish and the smoke-dark one, the bread torn against the heat, mint tea in brass cages. And then the hand — "it felt less like something beginning than something resolving — like her hand had been wrong all evening, all its life maybe, for want of his exactly." I melted. Fully. I want her to have this. "I'm making an exception" is the kind of line that makes me put a hand over my own mouth.

And I cannot have it cleanly, because I'm the only one at this table who knows everything. I know Pace went home from courting Vee — or came from — a house where Randi begs on a bench he built for her. I know Randi staged the collision on the quad, picked Vee with a bare helpless *yes*, and then sat two tables away in the coffee shop performing strangerhood while Pace performed it back — "the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt," Cassie said, and Cassie was describing a lie neither of them knew she was catching. So when he says "May I choose?" over the menu, I hear it twice: the tender version Vee hears, and the version where the choosing already happened — Randi chose her, Pace pointed, the geometry was arranged — and Vee is being gently handed the small choices while the large one was made around her and without her. That is *earned* unease; the text gave me every piece of it. And yet I hold it ajar, because I genuinely don't know what Pace is doing. His attentiveness with Randi was real *and* instrumental at once; there's no reason it can't be both here too. The man who gave Vee back the pencil is the man who gave Randi back herself on the bench. He means it. That might be the most dangerous version.

Vee deepened enormously this chapter. The hunger work is extraordinary — noon "coming and going somewhere in" the dress-and-other-dress afternoon, the hollow that "had been empty a lot longer than since noon," and then the quiet miracle: "She wasn't watching herself eat, as some part of her almost always was, and she didn't notice she'd stopped." That's Randi's apple-slice portioning seen from the other side — Pace feeds *both* women, finds the trimmed appetite in each and answers it. And the moment she lets the thread of his sentence go and nothing bad happens — "the not-needing-to was the most restful thing she had felt in as long as she could remember" — that's the same surrender he engineered in Randi, arriving here as peace instead of devastation. Vee is earnest where Randi is managed; the same man, the same patience, two completely different women coming apart in his care. The right-hand rule, Christine's ease at him walking in — he's embedded in a real life, and that makes him more trustworthy and somehow worse.

Cassie stays the truest instrument in the book. "That's not a boy. That's a man." — she means it as blessing and I received it as warning, and I don't think the text lets me settle which. Her not teasing Vee about this one thing is its own kind of love.

The title does heavy work. "May I Choose" is the three softest words in the chapter — consent as courtship, the "may" he carried in from "May I call you?" — and it's also, from where I sit, quietly sinister: a book in which everyone is being offered choices inside a frame they didn't choose. And the cover board reads different now than it would have cold: WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION. I hear the old saying under it — supping with the devil, you want a long spoon — and "a polite invitation" is exactly what this whole chapter was: immaculate manners as the instrument of something I can't yet name. No tagline was supplied beyond that line. The politeness is the promise and the threat at once.

Friction: almost none. The soft-edged middle of the dinner runs long and I let it, because the dissolution *is* the event — her vigilance has to have time to actually set. The kiss-rule reversal is quick but earned; she'd already practiced dropping the thread over the bread and liked it. What I want next is the exception's consequence — the kiss, obviously — and what I dread is the collision: Vee learning that the girl who hugged her under the gold sign and said *love you, girl* shares the man who just took her hand. That reveal is going to hurt somebody I now love twice over. (A guess from here, nothing more: I don't think Randi has told Vee anything, and I don't think Pace plans to.)
