## Reader reaction

This is the chapter where she says it, and she says it to no one. "She loved him. There. She'd said it, or not said it, but let it be true in her own chest where she couldn't take it back." I had to stop. After last chapter — the bathroom, the curdled wanting, the kiss with a beat too much in it — the book hands me this: a door already open, a spin, "her feet swinging out, the porch light wheeling once behind his head," and "a bright stupid sound she'd have died of anywhere else." I felt the relief of it physically, like being handed back to someone careful. And I don't trust my own relief, because I know what she doesn't, and the book has taught me that relief is the texture of the trap.

The drawer got me. I want to say that plainly before I get suspicious of it. The razor squared to the edge, her kit flung across the one clean surface, the week of "Had he minded. She couldn't tell. He never said" — and then the top drawer, her scrunchie form, her pins in a little dish, "a clear run of liner with room to spare, room for more of her, laid out like something prepared and waiting." And his whole ordered self folded down into the drawer below, "pressed in and fitted too tight together." No note. "There was never a note." As a reader I was moved — genuinely, throat-tight moved. And then the double vision arrived, because I carry the jacket and she doesn't: a drawer is a measured space. You give someone a drawer. It's the politest invitation in the book so far, and it is an allotment as much as a welcome — room for more of her, a fixed amount of room, in a house that has another woman with a prior claim she knows nothing about. She read the drawer as a declaration. I read it as a declaration AND as a filing. Both readings are on the page only if you've read the earlier chapters, and I hate that I can't unread them.

Pace himself: this is the most attractive he's ever been to me — the slowness she still can't get used to, "usually more than once before he took a single thing for himself," the cooking after, the coming back to her an hour later "like she was something he'd been turning over the whole time the salmon cooked." And he has never once said it. "He felt it in everything he did and said it in nothing, and she found, tonight, that she could live clear inside the everything-he-did and not go hungry for the saying." That's the sentence that frightened me. She's building an entire love out of interpreting a silent man's arrangements — the same silence I've been trained to read as cost or method, both. Her fluency in his acts is the most dangerous thing she owns, because acts are the one thing that can be performed.

And then "Peter." Breathed at the sleeping shape of him, meant unheard — "She hadn't meant him to." The first time she's used his true name on the page, and she can only say it to a man who can't hear it. That's the pattern completing itself: she couldn't finish in the blue bathroom under a fond imagined face; she kept the costume night's core back from Randi; now the name, the love, the unasked scar she "meant to keep" — everything true in her happens off the record. Secrecy has become her only mode of truth, and she learned that somewhere. She's started keeping files, reading acts, never asking — she's becoming fluent in the house style without knowing there's a house style.

The body detail that won't leave me: she's losing weight, "her body quietly burning down whole weekends of him, and she'd never asked it to," the bras riding higher — and the only clothes that fit her are the ones that were bought for her. Her own clothes never fit ("the racks had never once believed a build like hers"). Her body is being refitted to the gifts, unasked, and the page states it as happiness. Also the appetite: she "ate like a field hand," everything, after years of pushing plates away half done — set against Randi's one neat bite, "Delicious," the taster who never eats. One of them devours; the other samples and closes the subject.

Randi is not in this chapter. Not once — not a mention, not the fond face, nothing. The absence is loud. This is the first sealed room Vee has had, the first stretch of the book no one else has witnessed, and I kept waiting for the phone to buzz over the drawer. It didn't. The chapter stays clean — which is exactly why I'm braced, because I know what a brunch does to a clean thing.

**"Above Him"**: she is lifted and spun; she watches him sleep from beside-above him; she whispers down into his ear; and then — the quiet click — her things go in the TOP drawer, the one your hand goes to first, his pressed below. She's placed above him in the order of the house, and above him in knowledge (she loves; he doesn't know; she watches him sleep). It's the one chapter where she holds the watcher's seat, fondly — she knows now how the fond watching feels from the other side. The book's title hums under this one: the drawer is a polite invitation. The tagline hums too: every yes freely given — a whole chapter of yes, nothing taken, everything given. That was the trap, presumably.

**What I want/dread next**: I dread the next brunch with real force. She now holds four things — the reunion, the love, the whispered name, the drawer — and I know the mechanism: a wrong word ("domestic," "settling," some lever) and she'll pay the true file to correct it. If the drawer goes into Randi's keeping I'll grieve. If she keeps it, it'll be her third kept thing and I'll want to cheer. Also: the scar, still kept unasked. And who calls him Peter — whether that name is hers alone or shared. I don't know, and neither does she.
