# Cold read (grounded) — Above Him

## Reader reaction

**The felt read.**

This one undid me quietly, which is worse than being undone loudly. The whole chapter is built out of smallness — a week apart, coats dropped inside the door, salmon with dill, a man asleep with one arm flung up — and it lands like a vow. Vee setting the word down in her own chest, "She loved him. There," and then *counting all the ways he'd said it without saying it* — the door, the spin, the slowness, the salmon — I felt that arithmetic in my sternum, because I've been keeping the same ledger as a reader. And then "Peter," barely a breath, said to a sleeping man precisely because he can't hear it. She's practicing the word in the safest room there is. It's the mirror image of what she did in ch 25 — saying his name into the dark to keep it — except now the thing she's keeping is bigger. I swooned. Plainly.

And the drawer. God, the drawer. The scrunchies gone from the counter and her stomach dropping — *he'd minded after all, and quietly* — and then the top drawer opened and it's hers, prepared, waiting, "room for more of her," while his whole ordered self is folded down and pressed too tight into the drawer below. "There was no note. There was never a note." That's this book's whole love language in one image and it got me completely. He doesn't reassure; he makes a place. The title clicked for me right there — her things above his, the easy drawer, the one your hand goes to first, given to her. Though it also hangs over the sleep scene, her awake and watching him, holding a knowledge he doesn't have. Both readings feel true and I don't need to pick.

The retrospective sex — coats off inside the door, "she never knew which it would be, only that it always came around to her first" — runs warm rather than burning, deliberately. The heat here is domestic: a man who comes back to her an hour later like she's something he's been turning over while the salmon cooked. That line alone is worth a chapter of acrobatics. And the detail that her clothes have gone *looser*, the bras keeping the secret — her body changing under the feeding instead of the starving, her mother's whole regime quietly losing a war nobody declared. That's the book's central thesis sneaking in through a waistband.

My unease, such as it is, is the kind the book has taught me to carry: the happier she is, the more the reader's private knowledge weighs. She's whispering love to a sleeping man who shares a bed with her best friend and has never said so. The drawer scene is *real* — I believe the tenderness completely, the checkpoint's "both poles true at once" has never felt truer — and that's exactly what makes it ache. I trust his love and don't trust his silence, simultaneously, in the same paragraph. Also: the bathroom counter moment, "He never said. He never says" — she carries the smallest tail of that worry out of the room, and the chapter lets it stand even after the drawer answers it. He resolves things by gesture rather than words, and gestures can be misread, and someday one will be. That feels planted but earned.

Friction: almost none. The bras-and-sizes paragraph runs a touch long, and I'll admit I wanted one live scene instead of the whole reunion told in retrospect — but the chapter's about what settles *after* wanting, so the summary is the right texture. Nothing felt like a thumb on the scale.

What I want next: I dread and crave the Randi collision in equal measure now. She loves him, she's been *given a drawer* — belonging has become furniture — and every stitch of it makes the eventual discovery more catastrophic. And the scar got another quiet pass ("still meant to keep"). Somewhere soon this book has to start opening its sealed doors, and I'm braced. (Guesses from here: the drawer becomes evidence she'll point to, later, as proof — which is what will hurt most.)
