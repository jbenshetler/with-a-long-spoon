## Reader reaction

The quietest chapter in the book, and it undid me. After Friday's heat we get cereal and cold air and a girl who cannot stop smiling — and it turns out the morning after is where this book keeps its sharpest instrument: Cassie's eyes.

The face first. "For half a second something crossed her face and was gone before it found a name." That's the second time Cassie has had an unreadable flicker at the sight of Vee lit up over this man — there was a folded look once before — and twice is a pattern. Cassie is the best reader in the book; she reads Vee "fast and whole." So when her face does something no sentence can name, I put a pin in it. Worry? Recognition? I don't know, and the text earns my suspicion only by repetition — everything she *says* is warm: "That good?", "I have eyes," "Don't wait up, lover."

Then the book shows me the edit from inside the blade, and it's the most honest the narration has ever been about what Vee does: "She lifted it out clean as she went, the way a ladle takes soup from the pot and the surface closes without a hole, and told herself it was a kindness. It was a kindness. She believed it the way you believe the thing that lets you keep what you want to keep." The cut isn't only protection anymore — it's possession. She keeps the middle because it's hers. "A man made you dinner," Cassie says, and lets it stand, and "whether she felt it there, Vee couldn't have said." I felt the seam even if Cassie didn't. I suspect Cassie felt it and chose not to pull.

The zipper moment is the chapter's center and it's perfect: she starts to undress three feet from her best friend and realizes the next layer down isn't clothing, it's evidence. Nothing under the skirt. *Trade*, driven home bare. "It would be on the floor, and there would be nothing left to say." So she rezips the story and tells the smallest lie in the book: "I forgot my." After a whole night of her body testifying before she could speak, here she overrules it with one second to spare. And Cassie's exit line — "You need to get your brain back from your boyfriend," "kindly, certainly, wrong" — that flat "wrong" carrying everything: wrong word, wrong diagnosis; she has never been more present.

The no-bag paragraph is the most I've ever understood her: she'll prepare her body down to the toenails but won't pack the bag, because the bag is the admission said plainly to herself. She engineers her own spontaneity. That's the whole girl in one move.

And then "She had never in her life been so happy." I believe it — the grin at every red light, the smile that won't come down — and it scared me more than anything in the book, because I know what floor that happiness stands on. Nothing is wrong yet. That's the horror. This is what freely-given yeses buy: one cold bright morning of total joy, already being edited for its first audience. Pace is absent and everywhere — the missing underwear, the second channel, a house with no brush that would go through her hair. Randi's absence now has a new edge: Cassie got the story first. The extractor is two files behind, and she said "See you soon, gorgeous." The mother's word came and was welcomed and unfelt. The title is the tell: the smallest, most private preparation — painted toes in October, hidden in flats — is the first thing seen. What the body shows that the story hides.

What I want next and dread next are the same scene: Vee and Randi. (Guess from here: Randi gets a different cut than Cassie's, and the two files will diverge on some detail that later matters.) Still humming underneath: she drove home bare, and the book keeps touching contraception by not touching it.
