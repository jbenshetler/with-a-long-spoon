## Reader reaction

This one got me in the kitchen, not the bed. The sex was good — fast, honest, her body "publishing the number" of those five nights — but the chapter's real charge is domestic, and the book knows it. The shirt scene undid me. She washes the flannel because "it didn't smell like anything at all," and when she hands it over he thinks she's *returning* it — and there it is, the first thing I'd call need from him on the page: "the start of a hurt," seen for a second, then "He decided not to ask. He was good at not asking things." I've watched him care for her in a dozen registers — food, patience, the drawer, the lamp — but this is the first time I've watched him *fear losing* something. His face "came open," that rare smile "that seemed to cost him something, that he didn't hand around," and then he just pulls the shirt on over the one he's wearing, two shirts in his own warm kitchen, "All weekend." I believed all of it. My feeling for him moved — warmer, and for the first time I feel like I've seen him want, not just tend.

And Vee moved too, twice. First: "some unreasonable corner of her had wanted him to fight" — the first dissatisfaction she's ever consciously named about him. His grace cost her more than a fight would have. That's a real ache and it complicates her old conclusion that she could live inside the everything-he-did without the saying. Second: she *asked* for something — nakedly, badly, "It came out small and she couldn't help it" — the girl whose mouth never reads out the tally just handed him her most embarrassing want, and he received it without one flicker of embarrassment on her behalf. The book keeps doing this: shame carried out, handed back as a gift. Randi does it with absolution-words; Pace does it wordlessly, by putting the shirt on.

Trust vs. suspicion: on this page, nothing is off. Nothing. And that itself is the dissonance I carry — the jacket says this began as a game between him and Randi, and yet "He'd have made the pizza exactly like this for an empty house." The care isn't for an audience; she watches him not performing and can't tell whether she or the dough got the larger share, "and found she didn't mind." Either the game became real — the jacket does promise it gets real for the two who started it — or he's the most complete performer I've ever read. The hurt at the returned shirt felt unperformable. I hold both and I can't resolve it, and I'm noticing the not-resolving is becoming the pleasure of the book.

Erotic charge: the doorway kiss — "the cold of her cheeks against the warm of his palms" — and the speed of her, "shamefully fast," five nights coming out of her "in one long undignified rush." Hot because it's honest. The intercourse itself is compressed, almost perfunctory next to the bet chapter or the photo night — and rightly; this chapter's eros is the shirt, "Smelling like you." / "Smelling like me." That's the most intimate exchange in the book so far, and nobody's undressed for it.

Friction: almost none. If I squint, the watching-him-cook beat is now a ritual with a fixed structure — the going-down-into-the-work, the tasting off the spoon, the watching of her first bite — and this is the fourth or fifth elaborate meal; the variety is in the dish, not the shape. But the pizza scene earns its length with the single best line about him yet, the empty-house line, so I can't call it slack. And "He was good at not asking things" landed with a hum I can't fully source from the page alone — she means it as praise; the jacket in my hands makes it sound like the load-bearing wall of the whole house.

The title, "The New Ordinary": after reading, it means her baseline has been replaced. No bet, no costume, no camera — just a Friday, a pizza, a shirt, and the bag that "rode light" because half her life already lives in his top drawer. "Her space." The old ordinary — dorm, textbooks — is what she tried for five nights and couldn't sustain. Ordinary, here, is what the trap feels like from the inside: not a cage, a kitchen. The book's title keeps meaning what it's meant — this whole chapter is one long polite invitation she keeps accepting — and *With a Long Spoon* keeps feeding her at his table; she ate two slices too hot and held her plate out for more. Every yes in this chapter was hers, freely — the five nights, the return, the ask. That's the tagline working exactly as promised.

What I want next: Thanksgiving — Ohio, the parents, his "I don't go back" standing against her asking, and Sheri's pie at his table. What I dread: the uncropped photograph sitting in Randi's keeping like an unexploded thing, and the fact that Randi and Pace have still never shared a page in my reading — two worlds Vee believes are separate, and I was told on the jacket they are one. A guess from here: the next chapter swings back to school and Randi, and this weekend's shirt story gets paid out at the next brunch like everything else does.
