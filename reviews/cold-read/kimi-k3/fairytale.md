# Cold read (grounded) — Fairytale

## Reader reaction

**The felt read.**

This one hurt in a quiet, precise way. After chapters of watching Randi through Vee's dazzled eyes — the glamour, the command, the woman who always knows — the page turns her inside out, and what's underneath is a person freezing to death in a room full of gold. The breakfast alone did it: "eating her revulsion" at her father's hug (and I can't not feel the MIRANDA record sitting in that revulsion, unspoken), her mother "all edges and points," the invented boys because "the invented ones were safer than the real ones; they always had been, at this table." She's been performing at that table since childhood. The sorority smile is just the family smile with better lighting.

John is the cruelest thing the book has done to her, and he does it by being perfect. The ski run told me everything before the bedroom did — "he had the run to spare and spent none of it proving it" — and I believed her falling. The dress bought *for* the story, "the year I met your father," the daughter she doesn't have yet: I swooned, honestly, the way she swooned. The white tie, "She walks in beauty," the sleigh with its bells. The book let me have the whole fairytale at full wattage and then, in the after, took it apart seam by seam. "A good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon." I gasped at that. And "She was warm only where she touched him... the cold did not belong to the room. It was hers." That may be the loneliest sentence in the book so far.

The comparison passages are where it cuts deepest, because they're so fair. John the better dancer, the easier lead — "Pace's lead was the lightest pressure, a thing she'd had to be listening for" — and she decides easier is better, and then discovers easier is exactly the problem. "Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best. She wished it wasn't true." And then the line that recontextualizes everything: "Pace went straight to her. That was the whole terror of him, that he found the person she kept hidden and did not look away — and he would never once belong in the life she had built to keep her hidden." She loves the man who sees her and cannot have the life that hides her, and she can't give either one up. That's the whole tragedy of her, named.

And Vee. God. "Something came off Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday that this room could not counterfeit one thread of, and her whole body knew it, and she knew exactly what it was." She can imagine telling her — over eggs, plainly — and cannot run the scenario of Vee's face to the end, "and she had always been able to run a scenario to the end." That's the detail that breaks me: the manager of everything, stopped cold by one face she can't simulate. "Vee was the best friend she had ever had. She loved Vee and she had told her so, more than once. She had never once before said that to anyone without meaning it as currency." So every "Love you, girl" Vee received as performance was, on Randi's side, the only true sentence she owns. And the resolution — don't tell her, keep the cover, "let Vee arrive at it on her own" — is tender and cowardly and self-protective all at once, and it's also, I realize, the exact shape of the whole plan: engineering arrival instead of asking. She's doing to herself what she and Pace did to Vee. I don't know whether to ache or be afraid.

The ending gutted me twice. Vee's text — "i'm here if you want to talk. or not." — that "or not" is so exactly Vee, so unguarded, so free. And Randi, lying in the borrowed warmth of a stranger she feels nothing for, "gave her the fairytale": every word true, none of it the thing. Then "miss your face most though, gorgeous 💋" — the one line she meant, smuggled inside the lie. That's Randi's entire life in one text message. And "she had wanted the night to be a story, and here he was writing another page of it" turned poisonous: she got her story, and the story was hollow.

Trust vs. suspicion: my suspicion of Randi has almost entirely converted to grief. She's still deceiving Vee — the text proves she'll keep doing it — but I no longer read the deception as predatory from the inside. It reads as a starving woman at a feast she can't taste, protecting the one table where she can. The erotic charge here is all in the negation: the wedding-night-perfect sex rendered as cold arithmetic, and the heat located entirely offstage, in Ohio, in a warm kitchen, in a text from a girl in a dorm. The hottest thing in the chapter is "or not."

The title "Fairytale" — it's what Randi texts Vee, and it's the life her parents pointed her at, and it's what she tried to buy in the second boutique. The chapter lives inside the word and then shows the word is the problem: fairytales are stories you tell a daughter you don't have, not lives you can actually be warm in. Against the volume title "With a Long Spoon" — sup with a long spoon — this chapter shows Randi dining with the devil she's supposed to want, and finding out the devil is bland. No tagline was supplied beyond the cover board.

What I dread and want next: Randi coming home to Vee carrying this — the knowledge that the approved life is a dead room, and the decision to keep the cover. And I keep thinking about the bracelet "locked on her wrist," kept out of the light like the ring. Something in this book locks jewelry onto Randi, and nobody has told me why yet.

**Symbolism:** The bracelet locked on and kept out of the light — the hidden bond she neither removes nor displays. The dress bought for a story rather than a night — a life lived as anecdote. The sleigh: the fairytale literally arriving on command, and therefore worthless. Warmth along one seam only: intimacy as contact patch, not climate.

**Characterization:** Randi deepens enormously — the family table explains the performance, and the John's-night autopsy gives her interiority a spine of grief. Her father sharpens into something faintly sinister post-MIRANDA; her mother is one precise paragraph of damage. John is intentionally a beautiful blank. No one goes thin.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Long but earned; the ski day and ball need their full sweep so the hollow aftermath detonates properly. The final pages — mirror, thoughts of Pace and Vee, the text — are perfectly weighted.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** A hard POV swerve after Vee's run of anguish ("Believe Me" ended with her walking out into the cold) — and it rhymes: Vee crying in the doorway for a word Pace won't say; Randi in a grand hotel for a warmth John can't make. Both women starving at opposite tables. Momentum holds; I'm desperate for the collision.
