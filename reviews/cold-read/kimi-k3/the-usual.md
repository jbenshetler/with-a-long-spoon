## Reader reaction

First time inside his head, and the book knows exactly what it's withholding even there: the opening line gives me a decision with no object — "he would not bring her down today" — and an un-antecedented *her* I had to chase. My strongest read, helped by the thought he "could not stop" three paragraphs later ("Vee would either love this place or never see it at all"), is that he considered bringing Vee down here, into this county, this week, and decided she "could keep." And then the sentence that iced me: "he had long practice at deciding a thing could keep." That is the whole silence from the inside. Vee is drowning in it; he experiences it as a lifelong competence. Things keep. People keep. She keeps. I wanted to shake the page: things *expire*.

Sheri. So *that's* Sheri. The name Vee flickered at, and she's a five-foot gay girl in a chambray shirt who eats the double, drinks his shake "like something owed," steals his fries, and wants absolutely nothing off him — which is precisely why the diner is the one rest he gets: "Everyone he knew wanted something off him — to be wanted, or to have a problem solved — and he paid it out without ever letting the cost show." That's the key to every restraint scene I've watched from outside. Vee's flicker was wasted on the wrong threat, and something about that — the real intimacy being this warm, harmless, invisible-to-her friendship — hurts more than a rival would.

The fight, from his side, rearranged me. "I told her she was the most important thing in my life. I said it, out loud, in words. And she looked at me like I'd handed her half of something." Her account kept "warm and level and wrong"; his keeps a line hers dropped. Both true, both wounded, and the gap between them is exactly one word wide — he experiences that sentence as whole, she as half, and the missing half is *the word*. "Every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something" genuinely hurt to read. And then Sheri, small and level, lands the verdict he doesn't argue with: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her… from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." "There was nothing in it to argue with." He *knows*. My coldness from last chapter thawed — it's not cold in his booth, it's boarded up — but knowing and still not reaching is its own kind of awful. "I can't" is doing enormous, unexplained work, and the only light under the door is "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice." He has *lost* something. The parents-door is grief, not stubbornness, and the word is jammed behind it.

New dissonance: the brunette. A sorority girl he brought dancing before Thanksgiving, who looked at Sheri "like I was one more of your harem applicants" and "never did learn my name." So there *is* a recent other-woman shape in his trail — and Vee, building a blonde from spite at midnight, has aimed her jealousy at the wrong phantom entirely. Meanwhile "two years I've known you, and you've never once been a hit-'em-and-quit-'em man. But I have never seen you gone on a girl like you're gone on this redhead" — so there has been a parade, and she is not of it. Reassuring and unsettling in the same breath.

The quiet is now explained, and it re-reads everything: the Chili's where "something in him had come apart very quietly," her voice "down under all of it where he couldn't pull it clear" — the dead TV, the playlists, the sleep-giving quiet weren't taste, they're need. "Here he could have heard her whisper. He wondered whether she'd let him." He's imagining a future with her in it, in his places, while she drafts and deletes "are you—" ninety miles of silence away. Two people aching in the same direction and neither reaching. And "she gets what it's for. The whole idea under it. The philosophy of it" — her hunger was for meaning, and he *knows she receives it*. Being seen is his crackle; being said is hers. The tragedy is geometrical.

Erotically it's a low-heat chapter and the right one: Sheri's coarse love of Vee's body — "long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you" — puts Vee in the room, and his hot face going on anyway is more intimate than most sex scenes. Two quiet chapters in a row, though; the reunion is going to detonate.

"The Usual" — the order that needs no asking, "yes like people saying yes to their own names," a diner that became a diner "by long consent." That's his whole theory of love: true by long consent, no declaration required. And Vee needs the damn paperwork. Even the kid misassigns the double by "the grave confidence of arithmetic" — the world's wrong math about who gets what portion. As for the tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the trap." — it no longer reads like a predator's trap. It reads structural: a man this good in every room but one, and everyone keeps saying yes to him, and the yeses are all real, and it still starves her. Next: term resumes, Randi's circled day, and Sheri's commanded dance the first Saturday in February — which I am now certain will be a set-piece and dread. Guesses from here: Vee meets Sheri and misreads her; the keep expires before anyone reaches; whatever he lost has a name the parents-door is keeping.
