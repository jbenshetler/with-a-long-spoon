# Cold read (grounded) — The Usual

## Reader reaction

**Felt read.**

A whole chapter inside Pace's head, and the first thing he does is shelve himself: "he would not bring her down today... he had long practice at deciding a thing could keep." That landed in my chest like a diagnosis. This is the man Vee keeps hitting her fists against — not cold, not calculating in the moment, but *compartmentalized to the bone*, a man whose whole survival skill is deciding what can keep. His love can't keep, apparently, only his silence can. And yet — I believed him. I believed every brick. "When I've got her, there's nothing else in the room." "I'm there." He's not performing devotion for Sheri; there's no one to perform for here. That's what makes it ache instead of frighten, in this chapter at least.

The Chili's scene from his side undid me a little. In "Under the Rug" I watched Vee read his drifting eyes as contempt — *he doesn't want to be seen with me* — and now I learn "something in him had come apart very quietly over the hour," that he literally could not pull her voice clear, and that he *still* thinks about it: "Here he could have heard her whisper. He wondered whether she'd let him." Both of them were telling the truth that night and both of them went home bleeding. That's the cruelest kind of dramatic irony — no villain, just two people divided by the exact thing neither can say.

Sheri owns this chapter and I let her. She walked in as "the cycling buddy" and walks out a full person: sallow makeup worn as a dare, the shake drawn over "like something owed," and then Paige — the bird prints, the laugh too big for her, the hollering house of seven — gone because Sheri won't call her mama. "Call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas," done in sing-song and then killed flat. When she says "Ironic. Isn't it." and Pace doesn't touch it, I realized the book just quietly twinned them: two estranged people in a diner on the county road, both fluent in keeping, both paying for it. "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice" is the best line anyone's said in this book, and her "Don't waste the good stuff on me" broke my heart a little.

And then she turns and takes Pace apart: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her... from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." I wanted to stand up. Because yes — Vee has said exactly what she needs, plainly, twice now, and Pace's answer in this chapter is "I can't," and the chapter *never tells me why*. He won't lie — "I'm not them" — which I believe, and which makes the silence stranger, not simpler. Is the wall Randi? Is it Daphne, the door he pushed through once and paid for? Is it something older, the parents he won't go back to? The text holds it open and so will I, but I'll say this: knowing what I know — that there's a whole other woman, a whole other plan — "She wants me to say it" reads differently than Pace seems to hear it. The word he's withholding isn't just a word. Saying it would make Vee *real* in a way the arrangement can't survive. I don't know if he knows that. I suspect Sheri senses it, the way she keeps circling "two years I've known you and I've never seen you gone on a girl like this."

The swoon in this chapter is all yearning-in-absence, and it worked on me: "The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles." And the thing about the food — "she won't just tell me it tastes good, she gets what it's for" — which Sheri instantly deflates ("Get over yourself"), and his laugh is the first unguarded thing he's done in chapters. I want them to have each other. I also cannot forget that this same man pointed at her across a quad and sent Randi in. Both true. The book keeps making me hold both, and this chapter is the first time I've felt Pace *paying* for the hold.

The Randi beat curdled quietly: "She looked at me like I was one more of your harem applicants... Never did learn my name. But I got to see her." Sheri's one read of Randi is sharper than anything Randi's polish has allowed anyone, and "harem applicants" is funny and awful and not entirely wrong. My suspicion isn't new, but it's newly *specific*: Randi marks territory, and the territory now includes a woman she's also falling for. The title does quiet work — "The Usual" is the standing order, the waitress writing before anyone speaks, "yes like people saying yes to their own names" — and it's also, bitterly, the pattern: the usual silence, the usual keeping, somebody always playing "Blue Christmas" and pressing the bruise. The volume title — WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION — keeps humming underneath everything: you sup with dangerous company using a long spoon, and everything Pace serves is supper. From here I want February — that cover band, Sheri and Vee in the same room, Randi's name in the same air as Pace's truck. That's my guess, and my dread: the collision this book has been parking in plain sight.

Tea rings, amber photographs sealed under glass — the past preserved and untouchable. Sheri stealing his fries — the only hands in the book that take from him without want.

**Symbolism:** The diner itself — the one room in three counties without piped Christmas, a refuge for people with no home to go back to; "the usual" as identity for the unmoored ("yes to their own names"). The photos ambered under the tabletop: a young Elvis, a laughing family, a car nobody builds anymore — everything frozen mid-happiness, exactly what Pace does with feelings that "can keep."

**Characterization:** Pace deepens considerably — sincere, compartmentalized, paying a cost, "I can't" left unexplained but no longer unreadable. Sheri transforms from function (cycling buddy, blunt chorus) to person: Paige, the family wound, the grief-is-a-choice line; the chapter's moral spine. Randi, seen once through Sheri's eyes, flattens into her polish — pointedly.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Unhurried and earned; the food-talk buys the confession, the Paige detour deepens rather than delays; nothing wasted.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** A necessary exhale and reversal after the heavy run (Bare, Believe Me, Fairytale, Old Acquaintances) — same wound, other side of the glass. Momentum holds and sharpens: the question "why can't he say it" is now explicitly on the table, and February's dance is a loaded date on the calendar.
