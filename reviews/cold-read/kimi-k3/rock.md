## Reader reaction

This chapter broke my heart in the simplest possible way: it let two people talk in the warm, and it made me *want* to forget what I know. Coming straight out of the last chapter — Randi astride him, the two of them pricing Vee's readiness — I braced for this one. And then the book handed me the tenderest night in it so far, and every line landed double.

**Pace.** I loved him this chapter, against my own judgment, and I know it, and I can't stop it. The flat two-sentence answer about his parents — "My mother stays home. My father works in a chemical plant" — set next to the warm sprawl of hers; and then, asked what his mother calls him, he takes his mother *out* of the answer: "My real name is Peter." That substitution is the saddest thing in the chapter to me, sadder than the hall. "Less than staying." The desk in the hall for a whole year, doing long-division while the thing he loved got used as the punishment. And then the tutor. I did the same arithmetic Vee did — nineteen and fifteen, two years — and I held down the same word she held down, and then I caught myself doing something worse: rhyming. The boy who was had too early by a patient older woman now runs a patience of his own — "I want her to get there on her own" — on a girl barely older than he was then. Repetition or correction? The page put nineteen-and-fifteen in my hands; last chapter put "She'll get there" in his mouth. I didn't invent the rhyme. I don't know what it means yet. I believe the story completely — the good order was the tell, "the careful way a person carries a full glass" — and Vee's read on it undid me: "he had handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck." Also new and quietly enormous: he's twenty-two. The man with the house and the doctorate and the game is twenty-two.

**Vee.** Her best chapter as a person. She defends the casseroles before she accuses them ("It had been made with love"); the saffron "old enough to vote"; she doesn't say sorry; she goes first with the trumpet player "to make it a trade"; she swallows the ugly word and lets out only "You were so young." And the robe — the girl of the cardigans now "had tied it badly, it had fallen open, and she didn't mind." Nobody comments. The shame arc just *moved*, on its own, in the warm. I was so happy for her. That's the worst of it.

**Randi.** Absent — not invoked once — and her absence is a presence for me. Vee narrates her entire life to Pace and Randi appears nowhere in it, while in Randi's company it's "Pace this, Pace that" the whole hour. That asymmetry startled me. And I caught myself wondering, with real unease: does Randi know the name Peter? The tutor? If tonight ever gets carried to the other bed the way Saturday did — "That was kind of hot" — it will feel like a desecration of a different order.

**Trust vs. suspicion.** Nothing in this chapter is false on its face; I believe every fact in it. My suspicion is structural now: the tenderness is real *and* it is the medium the game runs in. Nights like this are how "she gets there on her own." The tagline has fully stopped being marketing.

**Erotic charge.** Almost no act, and the chapter is drenched anyway — the held breast "without pressing, without asking," his hand answering on her bare hip when she names his method, palms together over the scar. The sexiest sentence in it is "He picked it up. He turned it over. He gave it back warmer."

**Friction.** Minimal. The math set-piece (the paint, the coastline, Newton's three sentences) is the one place the author's pleasure shows; it survives because it pays off in her seeing the stars as bodies — "he had turned the understanding toward her like light." If there's a thumb on the scale it's the stacking: hall + tutor + flat parents + scar-tease is a lot of wound for one night. Vee's restraint kept the temperature low enough that I took it all.

**The titles.** "Rock" meant nothing to me until "My real name is Peter," and then it clicked — Peter, the rock; I'm a church kid's reader and the chapter put church all over her childhood, I can't not hear it. It also sits on the steadiness: steel bends "like dry spaghetti," rock doesn't; "No future. No past." It gave nothing away beforehand — it waited, and then it recolored everything. And the book's title and tagline: tonight is a night of freely given everything — name, wound, body, the word kept — and I am the only one at the fire who can see the spoon.

**What I want / expect / dread.** The scar's story is now promised — "a door with a time on it" — and I want it and dread what it will explain (guesses from here: the dormitory years, or whatever made "Less than staying" true at fifteen). The induction looms. I dread the next Randi chapter precisely because tonight was beautiful — I know what this cargo would be worth in that bed.
