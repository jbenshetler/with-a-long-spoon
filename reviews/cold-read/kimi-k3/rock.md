## Reader reaction

This chapter undid me quietly. After the sprint of the last four — the dress, the shoes, and then *Gone*, where I had to watch Randi and Pace discuss Vee over his own spent body — this one slows everything to a quilt, a fire, cold stars behind glass, and two people trading their actual lives. And it is, perversely, the most intimate chapter in the book so far, more intimate than any of the sex: because what gets bared here isn't skin, it's him.

The Daphne reveal hit me low and heavy, exactly as it hits Vee. The checkpoint memory of Daphne was a closed door he'd once pushed through and "it cost him" — I'd filed her as an ex he'd wronged, a lesson in restraint. This chapter rewrites her entirely: a nineteen-year-old tutor assigned to a fifteen-year-old boy, two years, and then the ordinary world pulling her away into rooms he wasn't allowed to stand in. Vee does the arithmetic and holds "the clean ugly grown-up word" down under her tongue, and I did the same thing at the same moment — the page made us complicit in the same restraint. And then the turn that actually broke me: "It was the best two years I'd had." He hands her the thing that hurt him and names it luck, and doesn't hear himself defending it. Suddenly the whole man reassembled for me — the patience that verges on liturgical, "May I call you," "Would you be bothered if I was too hard," the closed-door discipline with Daphne — a boy who was handled before he was grown, now handling a twenty-one-year-old woman with a care he was never owed and never got. I trust his tenderness *more* after this chapter, not less. And that's exactly what makes the frame ache: this is his most honest scene in the book, he gives her his name, his wound, his hallway, his scar — everything except the one true thing. The deception has never felt heavier than it does here, precisely because everything else was real.

"Peter" undid me too. He takes his mother out of the answer — flat, "My mother stays home. My father works in a chemical plant," nothing more — and then gives Vee the name instead, as if the name is the mother-sized hole. And Vee, who has been trained by this man in the etiquette of the unasked question, just says it once, "to feel how it sat in her own mouth," and lets him have his silence. Her family sketch is perfect in the other direction — warm, sprawling, unopened saffron "old enough to vote," the dad who calls her Vee and reserves *Vivienne* for trouble, so that "the name that means love in one mouth means trouble in the other." That's the whole shame-architecture of her life in one line, and he just lays it where she can see it. No wonder she loves him. I love him, in this scene, and I hold the arrangement against him in the same breath, and the book is making me live inside that double exposure.

The math speech could have been a writer showing off and instead it's the emotional center: the desk in the hallway for a year because he asked questions his teacher couldn't answer — "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment" — and then college at fifteen as "the first year of my life I wasn't in the hall." The finite paint, the infinite surface, the coastline as long as your patience, three sentences that hold the ball and the leaf and the stars. "Most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it, and I got to read some." And then Vee's connection — "That's how you are with me. It's the lift. It's the same face" — landing the squat bar, the bended steel, the nothing-but-this-moment, onto the quality everyone in this book feels around him. That presence is the thing Randi lost behind the face, the thing Vee keeps almost naming. The chapter quietly tells us what it's *for*, and it's for attention itself.

The scar scene is the most erotically charged moment in the chapter without any sex at all: her fingers finding "more of it than she'd known," his hand coming over hers — not fast, not to move it — pressing her palm down onto the thing he won't narrate yet. "Not tonight" with "no wall in it, only a door with a time on it." Doors again. I want to know what's under that scar and I trust the book to make it matter.

The title, *Rock*: I read it as the weight and the steadiness at once — the bar loaded until the steel bends "like dry spaghetti," the ridge that sits black and unmoving against the moving stars, and Pace himself, the fixed thing Vee's whole spring is leaning on. Which is the book's quiet cruelty, because I know the rock has a seam in it she can't see. The volume title and cover line — WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION — hang over this chapter like frost: all this warmth offered with such courtesy, and I still remember she never knew there was an invitation. Nothing this chapter did softened that; it sharpened it.

What I dread now is specific: the induction, the mother arriving, the dress walking into that room — and Vee building her selfhood on a foundation with a room in it she hasn't been shown. What I want, greedily, is more of *this* — and the scar, eventually, on its own time.
