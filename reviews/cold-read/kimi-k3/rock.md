# Cold read (grounded) — Rock

## Reader reaction

This one undid me quietly. It's the softest chapter in the book — a quilt, a fire, cold tea — and it's the one that rearranged the most.

Pace. God, Pace. I've trusted his tenderness and distrusted his honesty in the same breath for chapters now, and this chapter didn't resolve that, it deepened both horns. The seventh-grade desk in the hallway — "They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment" — landed on me like a hand on a bruise I didn't know he had. It explains everything: the patience, the way he hands Vee the pencil back at exactly the line where she can solve it herself, the measuring ritual built entirely around her consent. He knows what it is to have the thing you love used against you; he has built a whole self out of never doing that to anyone else. And the lifting — "no future, no past, only this lift" — and Vee, unprompted, seeing it: "You're right here. It's the lift. It's the same face." That line gave me chills, because she's *right*, she's read him more accurately than anyone on the page, and she's read him accurately inside a fiction he authored. Both things. The book keeps making me hold both things.

And then Daphne. I've been carrying that name since the beginning — the door he went through uninvited, the thing it cost him — and now the story arrives and it's so much sadder and stranger than the version in my head: a nineteen-year-old assigned to tutor a fifteen-year-old, two years, "nothing happened, nobody did anything wrong," and the careful accounting, "every reason accounted for and set in its slot." The page does the exact thing to me that it does to Vee: the arithmetic lands low and heavy, "the clean ugly grown-up word," and neither of us says it. What stays with me is what stays with her — "he had handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck." I noticed, too, that this is a gentler Daphne than the one I'd half-remembered — no closed door pushed through here, just a woman who aged out of rooms he couldn't enter. Either he's edited it for her, or there's more under it, and I can't tell which. The scar is the same: "Not tonight," and then — this broke me a little — he *presses her hand down onto it and holds it there*. A door with a time on it. He is more honest with her than he is factual, if that makes sense: he gives her the real wound and withholds only the label.

The swoon here is enormous and it's mostly non-sexual. The hand settling over her breast "without pressing, without asking, as if it were her skin and not the fire that kept him warm." "Peter," said once into the dark, "the way you say a word to keep it." And the math — I want to be honest that the paint and the coastline and "the language the world is written in... most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it" genuinely got me. He turned the stars into bodies in motion and I felt the world shift slightly, same as her. This is the hottest this man has ever been to me, fully clothed under a quilt, talking about Newton. The erotic charge of being *seen into*.

Which is exactly why the last page is cruel. "She had stumbled into her. Literally." "A man had crossed a coffee shop for it. That was all." "It had come to her all of a piece... for no reason she had done anything to deserve." Every sentence is a knife the reader can see and she can't. Six weeks ago I watched Randi engineer that collision and Pace get pointed at her like a fetch, and now I'm watching Vee build her entire cosmology of luck on top of it — *one piece*, the leaf and the stars, and the piece is a design. The chapter title clicks for me the moment I sit with it: Peter. The rock. The name means rock, and the chapter is him handing her the name — the truest thing he's given her — while she lies on the rock and calls it fortune. I don't think the title is ironic only. He *is* a rock to her. That's the unbearable part.

The mother's double-register — Vivienne meaning love in one mouth and trouble in the other — is gorgeous quiet work, and it rhymes with everything I know about the "floozie" voice without ever saying so. No friction for me anywhere; the one beat I held oddly was Vee's mother getting a defense ("made with love... affectionate") — it rounds her rather than flattening the shame-source into a villain, and I think it's true to how adult children actually talk about home.

What I dread next: the scar's story, whenever "not tonight" expires, and — more — the induction, the dress, her mother in the room, all of this happiness displayed publicly where one wrong glance between Pace and Randi could detonate it. And Randi is conspicuously absent from this chapter; after "Portion" and "Gone" I don't believe her quiet is rest.
