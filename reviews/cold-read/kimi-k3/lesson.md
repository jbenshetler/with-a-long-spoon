## Reader reaction

The dance lesson undid me before the bar did. "Give me something to push back against. Otherwise you cannot feel my lead." I read that and felt the whole book tilt — because it's the consent grammar of this relationship turned inside out. Pace has always asked; here he teaches her that the lead only works if she *resists*, if she gives him weight to move. And it works — "The pattern came through his body before it arrived in her feet" — and I swooned at it, genuinely, her feet landing where they should, "There it is," quiet. And in the same breath I couldn't unsee that I was watching a man teach a woman to stop anticipating, stop guessing, stop keeping space — to trust the frame completely — while I know the frame she's inside is rigged wall to wall. "That's what gets you stepped on — the space you're keeping." That line is going to live in me.

And then the blonde. God. "Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to." In-world it's a jealous woman salting the earth, and Vee receives it exactly that way, eventually — but I sat there with my mouth open because *it's true*, truer than the blonde knows, and Vee can't hear the truth in it, only the poison. That's the cruelest thing this book has done to me yet: the actual secret arriving disguised as cattiness, so it does its damage and teaches her nothing. And Vee's own thought on the floor — "how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she'd been building out of wanting him, wish laid over man so neatly she couldn't find the join" — I wanted to grab her shoulders. There is no join, sweetheart. That's the problem. There isn't one.

What happened to Vee after that genuinely hurt to read, and I want to be honest that my sympathy dipped — deliberately, I think. The venom in "Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe" was ugly and she knew it mid-air ("the way you hear a glass go past the point where you can still catch it"). And then the mother's voice did something it's never done before: it turned *outward*. "Floozies. She watched them." The shame-word she's been slowly surviving, aimed now at bare midriffs and October tans. That's a real darkening — the shame isn't shrinking, it's metastasizing into contempt for other women, and it took a jealous spike to show it. I didn't excuse her and the book didn't ask me to.

The bartender's reversal earned every word: "Leaves her at the door. Doesn't take advantage — I'd have heard otherwise. Not that plenty haven't tried." And "Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey." I felt the floor of the chapter come back up under me. Pace's own behavior through the rupture was exactly the man the Daphne story made: he answered straight — "Yes. Sometimes. When it's required" — no defense, no pursuit, set the beer down untouched and went out into the cold. He will not push through a closed door, even a door slammed at him unfairly. I believe that's integrity and I also notice it's a discipline that conveniently never risks the design.

The shirt scene gutted me most of all. The flannel she'd rationed the smell of, finally washed — "It came back smelling of fabric softener and no one. She took it down and put it on anyway." That's the whole chapter in one image: she laundered him out and then put on the absence. And underneath the shame, the *sensation* keeps returning — the lead arriving ahead of her feet — her body refusing the verdict her mouth delivered. That both-at-once is the truest thing in the chapter.

And then the ending turned my stomach in the best way: "go to Randi's." Of course. She's carrying the rupture to the co-author. Randi will receive this, comfort it, and steer it — and I dread and crave that scene in equal measure, because Randi now gets to play surgeon on a wound in her own design, and some part of her, I believe, will feel it as a wound in something she loves.

The title, *Lesson*: the dance lesson first, but it recolors everything — Vee's lesson about her own jealousy, the blonde's false lesson that was secretly true, and the quiet horror that everything in Vee's spring has been a lesson someone planned for her. The volume title — WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION — hums underneath: the frame holds her at exactly a long spoon's length, close enough to be moved, far enough not to be touched. No tagline beyond that on my cover board. Next: Randi's door. I'm braced.
