## Reader reaction

I need to confess first: the dress got me. Not the kiss — the bolt of silk. When she realized he hadn't matched the magazine shade, that "he hadn't matched the dress she'd described. He'd matched *her*" — my chest did the thing her chest did, and I was holding the jacket the whole time. I *know* what this is supposed to be. And I stood in that glass porch anyway and let a man who sews his own shirts and cares that scared kids spend less time in the MRI tube hand a girl back the wish she'd thrown away at the summit. This book is doing to me exactly what the tagline says it's doing to her, and I can feel it happening, and I keep reading. That's the report from the field.

The kiss landed — hers, taken, decided "somewhere back on the paved road," consistent with the girl who backed him into a tree. And then *he* stopped it. "No boy she'd ever been with had once been the one to stop. That had always been her job." I felt that drop too — because I'll admit it, Kayla had gotten to me: I walked into this chapter half-expecting the bedroom. Instead the bedroom is the only closed door in the house, and he says "my bedroom" with no weight on it at all, and she leans toward it in the satin she wore *on purpose* and his hand never goes to the handle. "Something in her dropped" — mine dropped with it, and then I caught myself: I have the jacket, I should know better than to be disappointed on schedule. The want stays lit with nowhere to put it — hers, and a little bit mine. The most erotic object in this chapter is a bolt of cloth, and the measuring hasn't even happened yet. That's where the heat lives now: "down to what you'd wear under it, in good light." She wore the satin hoping he'd hand her a reason. He declined the reason and built her a better one out of silk and consent — "If you'll let me," "If you want it" — and she said "Yes — of course, yes," three yesses, redundant, and I'd just read him explaining error-correcting codes: "Say it more than once so the noise can't kill it." I don't know what to do with how neatly that rhymes.

The tour is the seduction, and it worked on me as a tour — the shoes paired and squared ("Depends how many things he does"), the KitchenAid worn to a shine, the Sanskrit joke he didn't explain down ("she was glad he stayed exactly as far past her as he was"), the dovetail joint "cut to lock, and glued, so it stays," no screws, which I cannot stop holding next to the tagline. And then the one wrong note in the whole house, the one thing she didn't read: "a bathroom, plain, clean, two towels on the rod." That's the chapter's *title*. He lives alone. The closet holds one man's shoes, the kitchen one man's pans — and two towels. Maybe it's nothing, a bath towel and a hand towel. Maybe he set a second one out for her, more of the same care that pre-warmed the house. But I know from the jacket that there is a woman who is his lover, and the house is otherwise scrubbed clean of her, and the title plants a flag on the one detail that doesn't add up, and Vee — who can read half of him off a closet floor — walks past it without a ripple. She does that twice this chapter, actually: the heat she files "under the small mysteries of him," "the way you walk past a wrapped box on the counter without asking who it's for" — and I wanted to grab her, because the *narration itself* just said there's a wrapped box in this story. She registered the anomaly and declined to pursue it, same as the tagless shirt she once turned inside out and then stopped thinking about. Filing is her tell. Cassie audits; Vee files.

The parents-flinch is the second wrong note: "Something crossed him at *parents* — a small closing, there and gone, a door easing shut two rooms away." He showed her every room in his house except two — the bedroom and that one. First crack in the showroom, and I trust him more for it, which may itself be the trap's texture.

Friction: honestly, almost none. If I squint, the chapter risks making him *too* good — furniture-maker, patent-holder, dinosaur-light, "Patience. Paying attention" — a showroom of a man, and without the jacket I might have found it faintly implausible; with the jacket, the showroom quality is itself the most suspicious thing on the page. The chapter also stops exactly at the yes, before the measuring, which isn't a flaw but is definitely a cliff I'm standing on.

The titles: "Two Towels" meant nothing until the bathroom line, then lit up retroactively, and by the end — bedroom shut, no other woman in evidence — it's the one loose thread humming. It didn't give anything away; it taught me where to look, and what I saw when I looked is ambiguous, which is worse. And the book's title keeps literalizing: the invitation text last chapter, and now "'Come in,' he invited" at the door, and "I'd like to make it for you... If you'll let me" — every threshold in this book is a polite invitation, and every one of them is walked through by her, freely. The tagline is playing as a long, slow proof.

What I want next: the measuring. I want it and I dread it in exactly the proportions this book intends. Guesses from here: the fittings become the recurring structure from here to the induction; the bedroom stays shut until she opens it herself; and the towels are Randi's, and some day Vee will be in that bathroom long enough to count them.
