# Cold read (grounded) — hills-and-valleys

## Reader reaction

This chapter is a breather, and it caught me off guard how much I *needed* it. After the bar fight, the cab, the pie, the spank, the practice room — all that heat compressed into Vee's skin — the book takes me out onto a cold road with two people who have nothing to prove to each other, and just lets them pedal. And the strange thing is, it isn't empty. It might be one of the most quietly revealing chapters about Pace yet, precisely because Vee isn't in it and he doesn't know anyone is performing for anyone.

The moment that landed on me hardest is small: he starts talking about Vee — the thumb down the walnut grain, the joint he spent a weekend on, the math book she "not put back like it had burned her" — and "He heard himself going and didn't stop." That's the tell. This is a man who meters everything, who chooses his silences like tools, and with Sheri he just... runs. And then, devastatingly: "He didn't know what there was to listen to." He cannot hear his own smittenness. Sheri can. The book keeps insisting his tenderness is real — and here it is again, unguarded, witnessed by the one person with no stake in the game — and yet the design with Randi is still humming underneath, so I'm back in that vertigo: the love is real, the trap is real, both at once. My feeling about Pace didn't move so much as *settle*: whatever else is true, he is gone on her in a way he hasn't noticed himself.

Sheri, I like enormously and trust mostly. "She couldn't take your heat" — and Sheri laughs, *pleased*, and lets it go — that's a whole friendship in one exchange. Her acid is real but it's the kind that burns off to loyalty ("seen what it was worth on the other side, which was everything"). The "Hell of a backside on her" weather-report crassness keeps her from sainthood, which I appreciate; she's not a plot device in a ponytail, she's a person who remarks on women's bodies the way she remarks on headwinds. And her "That must be better than your dreams" is genuinely funny and a little wistful — there's something unowned in her there, maybe, or maybe just an honest athlete's envy of anyone whose life is richer than the road. I hold that loosely.

Trust vs. suspicion: the one cold seam in the chapter is the exchange about knowledge. "She know about the redhead?" — "She knows. She likes her." — "And the redhead?" — "Not yet." Two words, *not yet*, and my stomach tightened, because it's the entire book in two words: Vee's ignorance has an *expiry date* in Pace's mind, and he says it flat, without guilt, the way you'd say the milk hasn't turned *yet*. The text earns my unease there — that's not me being on guard, that's the page handing me the asymmetry in plain daylight. And Sheri laughing "the real one" at "Two of them at once" stung a little on Vee's behalf: Vee's life is, to everyone but Vee, a funny story told at handlebar distance.

Erotic charge: low, deliberately. There's a flicker — the small engine of Sheri standing on the pedals, the ponytail swinging, his eyes on her back as she pulls away — and the book lets me wonder for half a beat whether Sheri is another of his women. But the prose declines to heat it; it's admiration of work, not want. The chapter is about warmth without desire, which after ch 29–30 feels almost like a palate cleanser — and maybe that's its function on me: it re-grounds Pace as a man with a whole life, friendships, loyalties, rather than only the axis of the game. If anything, the charge is retrospective: "She was here last night" about Randi reminds me the parallel bed is still warm, and "not yet" is foreplay for a disaster.

Friction: almost none. If I have a quibble, it's that the chapter is *slight* — one ride, one conversation, done — and coming after two very eventful chapters it risks feeling like a held note. But the held note is doing work: it confirms the bar aftermath from outside ("all she did was sit by herself" — Vee's night corroborated, the blonde's poison further falsified), and it plants Sheri as a witness who knows *both* women exist. That last part is the loaded gun on the mantel, I think. Sheri knows about Randi *and* Vee now. She's exactly the type to say the wrong true thing in the wrong room someday. That's a guess from here, not knowledge — but "If that's not already complicated, it will be" reads like the book agreeing with me.

The title: **hills-and-valleys**. On its face it's the ride — they talk on the flats and descents, go quiet on the climbs, "which took the breath for themselves" (a line I loved; effort as something that *takes* rather than something you give). But it recolors the chapter on second thought: Sheri is faster on the climbs, always has been; the hard grades are where she pulls away and where he watches her go. And isn't that the whole arrangement — Vee is being ridden up a grade she doesn't know is a grade, by two people who've trained for the climb, while she thinks they're on a flat together? Talk where it lets you, silence where the hill takes your breath. The valleys are where the truth gets spoken. The volume title — **With a Long Spoon, Book Two: A Warm Reception** — still sits ominous over everything: you sup with the devil with a long spoon, and the warmth of the reception keeps proving itself to be both genuine hospitality and a way of keeping someone seated at the table. No tagline shown in my packet beyond those.

What I want next: the induction is still out there somewhere, the dress debut, the room turning — and now I want it *and* dread it, because "not yet" means the reckoning with Randi has a scheduled date even if Pace hasn't circled one. And I want, stupidly, for Sheri and Vee to meet. Or maybe I dread that more.
