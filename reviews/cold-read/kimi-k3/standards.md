The chapter after the bench, and it lands exactly where it has to: the walk home in last night's clothes, the machine she lives inside. And the first line told me everything — "Randi closed it behind her quietly, which was already wrong." Last night at his house she locked the door behind her, "first thing," and it read as safety, as ritual. Here the quiet close is a tell, a woman hoping not to be registered. The same gesture, inverted. I felt the drop in my stomach right there.

Brooke terrifies me, and the text earns it completely. She never stands. "The not-standing was the move" — I believed that instantly, and Randi believing it too, from inside, sitting on Standards herself, is what makes it hurt: she knows exactly what's being done to her and can only answer it with smiles. The interrogation is all warmth — "Oh — *hey*, you," the voice "the way other people had a perfume" — and every warm question is a probe: the paper, the study group, the place on Wilson, and then, seamless, "*Pace.* That's actually his name?" She'd misfiled him as Chase. She refiles him correctly. Then engineering — "the guess landing him a notch down without seeming to reach for it" — and math absorbing the notch "without conceding" it. The teaching question with its rehearsed *thank god* exhale. This is a woman mapping a threat. I don't trust Brooke at all, and I think I'm right not to; the text gave me her spreadsheet, her seat, her timing. She spotted the truck through Sloane, she spotted the lie about the paper — or at least logged the fractions of seconds. Randi got out in under four minutes, "most of it smiling," and it felt like a narrow escape, not a conversation.

Kinsey's "short-cute" gutted me worse than Brooke. Because Randi matched her pitch — "She had not had time to choose the pitch" — and said "He's cute. Yeah." After last night. After *there you are, Randi.* She stood on a bench's worth of having been truly seen, walked out into Saturday morning, and filed him as *cute* at someone else's pitch. That's not hypocrisy; it's the whole tragedy of the bathroom scene playing out in public. The face is lying. The face is good at lying. And now I understand what the face is *for* in a way the bedroom couldn't show me: this house is the environment the face evolved in, and he has no camouflage here — white pickup, math PhD, wrong register, "a notch down."

My feeling for Randi moved — not colder, exactly, but sadder and more frightened for her. Last chapter I watched her decide, quietly, "not ever," and this chapter shows what that decision costs to maintain: nothing, apparently. That's the horror of the final pages. The room cold where his house was warm; the duvet "light and synthetic and adequate" against his cashmere blanket; the closet she opens and can't remember why; the shoes still on. And then the killer: "*He's hard to say no to,* she had said" — the line that "landed perfectly" in the kitchen, now arriving "in a different key," one she can't sing in. She lied to the girls about *how* she came to him, and the lie is truer than she meant it. The last paragraph undid me: the not-crying that doesn't feel like holding a door shut because "it felt like there being no door... the way a held breath stops feeling held. The breath was held." Twenty-one years of a held breath she no longer feels. I don't blame her. I ache for her. And I'm quietly afraid of what "she was going to have to figure out what she was going to do about this" becomes, now that I've seen the machinery she'd be doing it inside.
