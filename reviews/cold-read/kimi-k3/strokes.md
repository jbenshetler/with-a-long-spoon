# Cold read (grounded) — Strokes

## Reader reaction

**Felt read.**

This chapter undid me quietly, which is its own trick after the run I've just been through — the induction walk, the bet, "We find out," the brunch bathroom, the drawer. I came in braced for more heat and instead got Vee alone in a lane of water at four on a Tuesday, and it turned out to be exactly what I needed and exactly what she needed, and the book knew it before I did. The pool was already her one unshamed room from back in chapter seven, and here it's confirmed and deepened: "her body, which had opinions all day long every day of her life, had none at all down here. It only worked." That line landed on me like a small grief. Everywhere else in this book her body is a site of verdicts — her mother's, the bleachers', the mirror's, his worshipful ones, Randi's measuring ones, her own shame tagging along after every pleasure. Underwater she's just an engine that goes and goes and asks for nothing. I love her most in this chapter, and I've loved her a lot.

The ache of it is what she carries *in* with her. Cassie's flat "Do you love him" gets in off the third wall and won't get out. And I know — I *know*, from "Above Him," one chapter ago — that she has the answer, that she lay against his sleeping chest and said "Peter" into the dark and let "she loved him" be true where she couldn't take it back. So the "maybe" she gave Cassie reads to me as pure self-protection, and the chapter knows it: the answer was "sitting plainly in her, easy to reach, easy to say, and no idea in the world why it had come out of her mouth as *maybe.*" That precision about her own self-deception is the thing this book does better than anything. She can say it to a sleeping man. She cannot say it to the person who'd hear her.

And then the four strokes. Around the seventh piece she gets up into the quiet and turns over the question of "whether he was that good or she had made him that good, out of wanting, out of two months of being looked at like that" — and I stopped breathing, because *I* know. I know about Randi, the game, the debriefs, the pointing. Her one moment of clear-eyed doubt in the whole chapter, four strokes long, is the truest suspicion available to her and she lets the wall come and rolls and it's gone. The text doesn't punish her for it or underline it; it just lets it pass the way real doubt passes when you're loved well and tired and the water is doing its work. "Four strokes was all it got." As a reader I'm holding both things: the tenderness is real, the trap is real, and she almost saw the seam. My dread is entirely made of love, still.

The "did he mind" thread got me too, because I'd watched that drawer scene from the outside and read it as the most eloquent devotion in the book — him folding his ordered self down into one cramped drawer to give her the top one — and here's the turn: even *after* the drawer, she carries "the smallest tail" of it. The swimming is where she puts it down. "It never answered anything, the swimming. That was the whole use of it." I believed every word. The chapter earns its quiet.

Friction: almost none. If anything, the risk is slack — a chapter this still after five chapters of escalating charge could sag — but it's short, the prose is exact ("her arms feeling like they belonged to somebody generous" — that "generous" is perfect), and the four-stroke doubt gives it a spine. No thumb on the scale anywhere.

The title: "Strokes." It means the swim strokes first, plainly — the whole chapter is built on them, the count, breathe every third. But after "We find out" and the brunch, I cannot read "Strokes" without also hearing his hand coming down on her, the strokes she paid her bet with, the strokes of the hairbrush Saturday morning, even his stroking her hair while he said wonderful, wonderful. The title holds all the kinds of touch in the book — the ones in water that ask nothing of her, and the ones in his house that ask everything — and lets the chapter be about the difference without saying so. It recolors the whole thing on second thought; the unerotic swim is defined against all the strokes she isn't naming. The volume title, *With a Long Spoon* — Book One, *A Polite Invitation* — keeps humming underneath: "polite invitation" is still the book's great double meaning, courtly "may I" laid over a design she can't see, and this chapter, where she nearly asks herself the one question that matters and swims it off instead, fits that exactly. The dread of the long spoon is that she still doesn't know whose table she's supping at.

What I want next: I want her to say it to his face, and I'm dreading what his face does when she does. I want to know whether the four strokes come back. And I keep waiting — *guessing from here* — for the collision: Randi's name said in Pace's bedroom, or his in hers, one slip. This chapter bought her quiet. I don't trust the book to let it last.
