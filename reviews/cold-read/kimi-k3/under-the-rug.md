## Reader reaction

This one hurt in a new key. The opening line — "the word was right there, simple, wanting out. She didn't say it" — made me actually groan, because I've watched her build and retire the surrogate sentence already, and now the fear is conscious: "that she'd say it and he wouldn't... a room that didn't say it back." And then the book gave her the thought with teeth: is what she feels true, "or just what a man could do to a woman when he was this good at it"? Whether his attention is "showing her the truth or keeping her from it." Given what the jacket told me about how this started, that question lands like a tuning fork. She is groping toward the shape of the game without knowing there is one. It's the most chilling passage in the book so far, and it's also the first time I've felt her doubt the thing itself rather than just its name.

"Is this what we are now?" — I winced with her, and loved that the book lets her hear herself do it and not pretend she didn't. His "Okay. Where do you want to go tonight?" is such a good answer and such a non-answer at once: instant compliance that somehow widens the gap. She wanted a fight about what they *are*; he handed her logistics.

Chili's broke my heart in both directions. His overload is rendered with total precision — the eyes that slip and get brought back "with a cost in it," the fork squared and re-squared, the irritation flicker he drops from view so she won't have to carry it. I believed it completely, and it re-lit the whole house for me: the dead TV, the always-going playlists, the sleep-giving quiet — it all re-reads as an environment he *needs*, not a taste he has. And she can't see it, and reads it as shame of her: "The just-sex of it, proven in fluorescent light." I wanted to shake her and hold her at once. The dead nachos — "It was to food what a billboard is to a painting," "She had liked this once," "He had ruined it for her... Not just this" — that's Randi's ghost sitting in the booth uninvited. The old palate is gone. There's no going back to fine.

Here's where my sympathy took its first real dent: the Rusty Nail accusation. She *knows* which of them went cold that night — the pie, the porch steps, her own back room knows — and she says it anyway, "because she needed the line of it to hold." And Pace, handed a false charge, answers with "You know what happened after the dance" — doesn't correct her, doesn't spend the truth as a weapon. Grace or control? I genuinely can't tell, and neither can she, and then she converts his restraint into "she had forgiven him" — a clean shape laid over a reversed ledger — and the book lets her feel it "sat well in her." That's the most human and the most alarming thing she's done yet. She's now editing in both directions: inflating against him, then forgiving him for the inflation, anything to make a shape she can rest in.

"I'm listening now" undid me a little. It's the only vow he has the parts for. And the narration's flat "It wasn't the word. It would never be the word" chilled me — whose certainty is that?

The sex is the book's erotic summit and its saddest scene at once. His mouth at her breast "as though he were taking something there for himself" — new, tender, almost childlike, the first time I've seen him *need* — and then the control the book has been edging for chapters finally goes: "took her like a man who'd run clean out of the strength to wait." It's hot. And then the cruelest true thing: it doesn't hold. "She came with his name in her mouth and not the other thing," and ten minutes later she's lying in cooling sweat doing the proof-math of what she can't hold up to anyone. He sleeps easy; she grinds. "It had no name" is where the chapter leaves her, and me.

Friction: "You know what happened after the dance" is aimed past me — I don't know. A fight at the dance, an aftermath, a cold night at the Rusty Nail, a pie on porch steps: this chapter leans hard on backstory I've only seen as file-names. I could run on the emotional shape, but I felt the gap.

The title, "Under the Rug," meant nothing until the end and then it meant everything: the fight is resolved by sex, the word is drowned by orgasm, her false accusation is swept by his non-correction, her forgiveness is a rug laid over a reversed ledger, and the dance aftermath — the load-bearing fact — stays swept under, unshown. The rug is the great sex itself. The book's title and tagline keep acquiring teeth: her yes to the forgiveness-shape, her yes to the drowning, her yes to not turning over the wrong note — "she didn't stop." Every one freely given.

Next: I dread and want the next brunch. Randi's been gone three chapters; if this fight gets extracted, Randi will hear that Vee nearly said the word again and that Pace's nearest vow is "I'm listening now" — and she'll hear what neither of them can. And I want, without expecting, to be shown what happened after the dance.
