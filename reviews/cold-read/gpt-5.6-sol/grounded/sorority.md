I’m warm and laughing with them before I’m worried, and that matters. That makes them feel more mutual, and it deepens my affection for both of them.

The dress scene pulls hard too. Vee wants Randi to see her in it, not merely see the dress, and Randi understands almost instantly: “He didn’t make this *for* you. He made it *to* you.” That lands because it names exactly what Vee felt in the previous chapter. Randi’s response is intensely intimate without quite becoming overtly sexual—the stillness, the hand following the seam over Vee’s body, “Turn for me—slow,” the breathless “Oh, *baby*.” I feel Vee wanting Randi’s gaze in a way she still does not name, and I want her to recognize that want because it is already so alive.

The erotic peak is Randi kneeling to put the shoe on her. Her holding Vee’s foot, noticing the plum polish, moving her thumb once over the big toenail, and Vee feeling it “go all the way up the length of her” absolutely works on me. It is small, specific, and electrically charged. Randi looks up, smiles, and leaves it unspoken. That silence makes it hotter, but it also preserves Vee’s ability to keep not knowing what she knows. Pace is absent, yet the dress he made is almost a third presence between them: his intimate knowledge of Vee becomes the occasion for Randi to touch, admire, and complete the image.

But my warmth turns into real unease in the boutique. Randi never directly orders Vee to buy the shoes, and Vee makes the purchase herself, but Randi chooses a store where Vee cannot afford anything, declares “A dress your *lover* made you? It goes on these,” and supplies the emotional logic that makes extravagance feel mandatory. Vee then transforms a want she cannot afford into a debt she supposedly owes the dress: “The shoes were not a want she had to own as a want. They were what the dress was owed.” That is self-deception happening in front of me. She knows she lacks “the paying-it-back part” and uses her emergency credit card anyway. Her yes is real, but the desire around it has been shaped until saying no feels like betraying Pace, the dress, and the bolder self she wants to become.

Randi’s effortless purchase of shoes costing several times more sharpens the discomfort. I don’t think she deliberately humiliates Vee; the page gives me no sign that she even notices what the credit card means. That may be the problem. Her money is so frictionless that she can lead Vee into her world without feeling the cost Vee must absorb to enter it. I still believe Randi’s admiration, delight, and desire are genuine. I also see how easily her warmth reorganizes a dorm room, a day, a definition of necessity, and Vee herself.

The chapter lets the purchase stay pleasurable—the right shoes really do hold her, complete the line, and make her feel capable of walking into that imagined room—but it does not let pleasure erase the bill. At the same time, the title carries the expensive social world Randi belongs to and Vee wants admission into—the world where the correct shoe matters and money need not be looked at.
