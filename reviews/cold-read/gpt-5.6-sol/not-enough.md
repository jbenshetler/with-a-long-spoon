This hurts, and it also changes Pace for me. I have been waiting to know whether his refusal was uncertainty, evasion, or some colder limit in what he feels. Now I know the central thing Vee cannot: “He knew he loved her.” The relief of that lands hard, but it cannot undo what he did. He understood her cracker-and-meal speech perfectly, knew his answer would fail her, and “chose the smaller truth.” That admission makes me trust his self-knowledge more and his conduct less. He did not merely miss her meaning. At the exact moment she risked herself, he protected himself knowingly and left her to absorb the wound.

Still, I ache for him here. The bleakest turn is the contrast between the meals he makes for Vee and the “prison food” he makes for himself: nutritionally complete, joyless, eaten by command. His care for her has always been one of the book’s great seductions, and now her absence reveals that she fed something in him too. He does not only miss her body or their sex. He misses “watching her arrive,” her unguarded walk to the bathroom, her sleeping face, and the instant pleasure of her first bite. That specificity sweeps me up. It makes his love feel lived rather than declared for the chapter’s convenience.

The Daphne history clarifies Pace without settling everything about that relationship. She was the person who taught an isolated fifteen-year-old how to inhabit college life, then became best friend, lover, and the only person he had told he loved. His belief that saying the words caused the devastation is painfully irrational and emotionally convincing: after Daphne, he kept love unspoken and later losses stayed survivable. I feel enormous sympathy for the abandoned boy inside that rule. I also feel frustrated with the adult man who turned a private superstition into a cost Vee had to pay. “He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him” is exactly the truth I needed from him, and it does not excuse him.

There is no erotic heat for me here, despite the intimate memories of Vee naked or in his shirt. Those images are charged with domestic absence, not lust. The chapter’s strongest physical blow is the passing truck: his body rises toward the possibility of her before thought catches up, and when it does not turn in, he sobs. That broke me. The book has made his restraint so foundational that seeing it fail in a cold kitchen feels almost shockingly naked. I do not suspect the grief; it is fully earned. My larger suspicion of him remains because he is still concealing Randi and the plan from Vee, and this chapter does not touch that moral breach at all.

The repeated eating at the end might have felt over-insistent elsewhere, but here it lands: he feeds himself because survival is what he knows how to do, even while finally admitting that making loss survivable is not the same as making love smaller.
