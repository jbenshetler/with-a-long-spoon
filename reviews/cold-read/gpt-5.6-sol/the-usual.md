## Reader reaction

This chapter makes me ache for Pace more than it absolves him. I feel the relief in the diner immediately: “the warm griddle-smelling dark,” the dead jukebox, the tiny sounds he can separate from one another. After Chili’s, his gratitude for quiet lands bodily. So does the first sentence: he has brought something painful in from the truck and instantly decides not to burden Sheri with it. That is recognizably his tenderness and also recognizably his problem—“he had long practice at deciding a thing could keep.” He keeps pain, history, declarations, and apparently entire relationships until keeping them begins to hurt everyone around him.

I believe him when he says, “I want Vee. I miss her.” I believe “The air’s different when she’s in the room. It—crackles.” His embarrassment over her understanding “the philosophy” of his food is more convincing to me than “most important person” was, because here he is not constructing an argument for Vee; he is trying to name her effect on him. It is painfully clear he is “gone on” her. My attraction and tenderness toward him rise again because I can finally see his longing during the silence. But my frustration rises too. Sheri gets exactly to the point: “Then not saying it matters more to you than having her.” He cannot refute her. Whatever stops him from saying it is not merely care, honesty, or disdain for cheap words. It is a governing refusal strong enough that he will risk losing Vee.

The opening also confirms that his silence has not meant indifference: he is bruised and missing her. But he still has not contacted her, and he still describes the conflict to Sheri without mentioning the most decisive fact—that there is a brunette, and Vee does not know about her. When Sheri asks, “She find out about the brunette, then,” his “No. Not like that” is technically true and evasive. The deception remains very much alive.

Sheri deepens beautifully for me. She is funny, abrasive, observant, and genuinely safe for Pace because “she just liked him and gave him hell.” Her appetite and theft of his fries make me like her; her talk about Paige and estrangement from her family gives the roughness an unadvertised wound. “People who’ve never lost anything think grief is a choice” feels shared between them. It also makes Pace’s estrangement from his own parents feel less isolated, though I still do not know either history. Sheri’s sexual comments about Vee’s body are crude enough to make me wince, but they feel like her deliberately needling Pace rather than threatening Vee. I trust her more after she tells him plainly that his choice “feels pretty shitty.”

Paige interests me despite appearing only in Sheri’s account. Sheri really liked her—the too-big laugh, the loud family, the little bird prints—but Paige kept treating estrangement as a correctable failure. I sympathize with both of them, though more with Sheri in the breakup as told.

There is little conventional erotic heat here, but there is a low flare when Sheri talks about Vee’s backside, breasts, and long legs around Pace, especially because Pace blushes and answers despite himself. It peaks less in the anatomy than in “The air’s different when she’s in the room. It—crackles.” That feels erotic and romantic at once. The food description is sensuous but comforting rather than sexual.

My main friction is Pace’s self-accounting: “When I’ve got her, there’s nothing else in the room.” It is emotionally persuasive in the moment, but I cannot forget that Randi exists and that Pace has arranged Vee’s experience around what she does not know. Likewise, “every brick was true” sharply echoes Randi’s truthful-but-incomplete fairytale. Their statements can be individually true and still build the wrong house around Vee. That is not a line I disbelieve; it is the line that makes me most uneasy.

“The Usual” begins as the diner order, something so established that saying yes is “like people saying yes to their own names.” By the end it also seems to mean Pace and Sheri’s habitual refuge: plain food, insults, honesty without performance, and pain handled indirectly until one of them forces it into speech. More darkly, Pace’s “usual” is deciding that difficult things can keep. The title did not give anything away; it widened as I read.

*With a Long Spoon* still has no single settled meaning for me. This chapter’s table, food, shake, fries, and “philosophy” of cooking keep the title’s eating imagery alive, while the emotional distance across the booth matters too. The cover’s “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap” presses especially hard against “they told her yes like people saying yes to their own names”: an easy, familiar yes can still rest on unexamined circumstances. My guess from here is that Pace’s inability to declare himself is tied to whatever he has “long practice” keeping, perhaps his family history or the tutor relationship—but that remains only a guess. What I want next is for him to contact Vee, not merely miss her safely in conversation with Sheri. What I dread is that he will return to Randi, let the usual arrangement resume, and continue believing true bricks necessarily make an honest whole.
