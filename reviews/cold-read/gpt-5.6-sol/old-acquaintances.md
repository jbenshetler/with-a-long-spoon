## Reader reaction

This chapter hurts in a quiet, airless way. I feel fiercely protective of Vee. Her loneliness is bodily before it is romantic: knees bent because she outgrew the bed, covers to her chin, the ceiling seeming lower after she has “been breathing bigger air for months.” The sharpest line for me is that her room “had known Vivienne” but “had no idea who was in the bed.” Home loves her, but only in an old shape; Pace helped awaken the new one and then left her without a word.

My feeling toward Pace worsens here, although he never appears. I still want the warm, attentive man Vee remembers at the stove, but his silence across the whole break feels punitive whether he intends it that way or not. “He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now.” Space without reassurance has become abandonment. Vee’s imagined blonde is plainly fear rather than evidence, and I do not suspect Pace of actually spending Christmas with such a woman. But the book has given me every reason to know Vee’s larger fear is justified: she cannot know whose he is because he has withheld both a name and the truth about Randi.

Randi’s reply is devastating because I have just seen her compose it. Vee receives the curated fairytale exactly as intended and feels chosen by “miss your face most though, gorgeous,” never knowing that Randi is in John’s bed, genuinely loves her, and is consciously withholding that love’s second meaning. I remain tender toward Randi, but more uncomfortable with her choice. Vee thinks Randi is “the only girl she could talk to about real things,” while the real things are mostly Pace—whose secret lover Randi is. That is the trap in miniature.

There is little conventional erotic heat, but Vee’s memory of Pace cooking after their first night is sensuous: the falling sheet, wine light, garlic and pepper blooming, “her body quiet and the evening having used her well.” It works until jealousy replaces Vee with the blonde. That corruption of her best sexual memory is the chapter’s emotional peak. The charge does not go clinical; it curdles into grief.

I did briefly resist Vee’s certainty that Randi must be with “a devastatingly handsome billionaire,” because the message itself says nothing about a man. But it reads as Vee filling in the glamorous mythology Randi has long supplied, not the narration forcing a fact. Likewise, “a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's” is painfully cruel, but recognizably Vee using inherited shame against herself.

“Old Acquaintances” points first to the bedroom and the preserved Vivienne whom it knows better than the changed Vee. It also feels like the return of old companions she thought she had outgrown: smallness, bodily shame, jealousy, loneliness, and making herself believe she can live without what she wants. The title did not give the chapter away; it became sadder as the room kept watch over “the girl who was gone.”

*With a Long Spoon* still has no single settled meaning for me, though distance and reaching feel stronger now: Vee is reaching toward both lovers while both remain beyond what she can know. The cover line lands brutally here. Her trust, her nude photograph, her intimacy, her silence about Miranda Holdings, and her loving message to Randi were all freely given—but with information cropped away by both Pace and Randi. I dread how completely she believes Randi is the one safe certainty of the new year.

I want Pace to contact her and finally answer, but I no longer want mere warmth or sex to repair this. I want Vee to tell Cassie or confront Randi with what she knows about Miranda Holdings. My guess from here is that returning to school will bring Vee and Randi together first, with Vee leaning harder on Randi because Pace remains uncertain. That closeness may become openly romantic before Vee learns why it was never an innocent refuge.

#### Running memory — story so far

Randi and Pace are secret lovers who selected Vee for a sexual game and approached her separately without telling her. Randi became Vee’s best friend while Pace courted her through attention, mathematics, cooking, making, dancing, domestic care, dominance and sex. Both conspirators developed real feelings.

Pace disclosed the name Peter, his early-college history, and his sexual relationship at fifteen with a nineteen-year-old tutor, which he says was not wrong. His childhood, parents, finances, scar, rules, closed rooms and preparations remain obscure.

He made Vee a burgundy gown, gave her clothes and domestic space, and built intense sexual and domestic intimacy. Vee realized she loved him, slept at his house most school nights, received a bathroom drawer, and used his flannel during absence. At induction she wore his gown bare beneath it, chose public exposure, and sent him the uncropped photograph. Later she offered a full nude after securing his privacy promise; unknown to her, Randi saw it. Vee set a five-night academic boundary and Pace accepted it.

At brunch Vee told Randi intimate details about Pace and showed her the nude. Randi celebrated Vee’s appetite but lost composure when Vee described Pace wanting her whole. Vee proposed an Anthro elective for “just us” and prolonged their kiss.

Cassie, Vee and Randi analyzed pandemic payroll loans. Miranda Holdings, Randi’s family company, was the worst outlier. Randi blanched and said, “Cheating bastards.” Vee recognized the family connection and silently protected Randi from Cassie.

Over Thanksgiving Vee sent her parents a cropped induction photograph and offered only acceptable fragments of Pace. She tentatively called him her boyfriend while hiding his age, house, sewing, cooking and their sexual life. Cassie later asked what Pace was to her; Vee could not answer.

On Pace’s porch Vee compared her old deprivation to crackers and his care to a meal. Pace replied that he loved how good they were together, not that he loved her. Later she asked whether they were just sex and complained they never went out. Pace took her immediately to Chili’s, where sensory overload made him seem angry until he explained that he could not hear her. His “I’m listening now” repaired the moment.

After attentive sex Pace lost control and entered her urgently. Vee briefly read this as possession, but once he slept she still could not name them. In the dorm she realized his shirt offered scent and deeds without a name, dropped it, and lay naked in the cold.

After two sleepless nights she drove to Pace’s. He had heated the house, poured wine and begun chicken. She left her bag in the car, stayed on the mat, kept her coat shut and refused the usual kiss. When she asked what they were, Pace called her his most important person and said words were cheap. At the same New Year period, Randi vacationed in Gstaad with wealthy parents who welcome only an acceptable version of her. Her father asked about eligible men; she supplied invented conquests. Her controlled mother and locked bracelet reflected the life attached to her.

Randi met John skiing. They invented a marriage and children, skied together, drank champagne, and attended a white-tie ball after Randi bought a metallic-blue dress. John’s firm dance lead was easier than Pace’s responsive one. Randi briefly imagined John as “the one.” After midnight he arranged a sleigh and assumed she would go to his room; she knowingly accepted. Sex was physically good but emotionally empty. John slept while she waited for intimacy. Restoring her face in the mirror, she saw that nothing had happened to her eyes.

Randi admitted Pace reaches her hidden self more deeply on his worst night than John at his best. She also recognized that Vee gives her something no perfect setting can counterfeit. She knows she loves Vee romantically as well as platonically, but decides not to disclose it because she fears losing their unguarded friendship. She tells herself she will let Vee arrive at the second meaning.

Meanwhile, at her parents’ Ohio house, Vee spends New Year’s Eve alone in the cold childhood bedroom she has physically and inwardly outgrown. The room preserves swimming trophies, ribbons, a clarinet and old friends—the high-resolution Vivienne who no longer exists. Her parents repeatedly ask about her young man, but she has no answer.

Pace has sent nothing across the entire break. Vee scrolls old messages, starts “are you—,” deletes it, and knows no small message can return the needed answer. She studies photographs: laughing with Pace, the dance outfit, and the whole induction image she sent Pace versus the version she cropped at the waist for her mother.

Vee sees Randi’s glamorous Gstaad feed, including the bracelet and black mountains, and envies it despite knowing Miranda money may be poisoned. She sends the gentle message offering to talk without revealing that she recognized Miranda Holdings. Randi replies with the true but incomplete fairytale message. Vee believes Randi is thriving in a glamorous public life and feels cherished that Randi answered.

Vee sees Randi as the one secure person waiting in the new term and wishes she could discuss Pace with her, not knowing Randi is his lover. Uncertainty about Pace and Sheri makes Vee invent a blonde model-bodied rival. She tries to warm herself with the memory of Pace cooking fra diavolo after their first night, but the imagined blonde replaces her at the table. Even remembered assurances from Randi and the older bartender no longer support her because she no longer knows she is anything to Pace.

At midnight Vee imagines Pace’s arms around her and simultaneously imagines them around someone else. She falls asleep holding both Pace and Randi in her mind, able to place neither securely, while the cold room watches over the obsolete girl it still knows.
