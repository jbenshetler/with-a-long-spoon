## Reader reaction

This chapter hurts in a quiet, airless way. Vee’s loneliness got into my body almost immediately: her knees bent because she outgrew the bed years ago, the sheets at her chin, the “cold lavender dark.” I feel fiercely protective of her. She has grown beyond both this room and the version of herself her parents can recognize, but the larger life she found has no secure public shape. The line that catches all of it is: “The room did not know her. It had known Vivienne.” Home loves her, but loves an obsolete girl.

Her movement toward Randi makes me ache because I know how unequal their knowledge is. Vee offers “an open hand” while carefully protecting Randi’s Miranda Holdings secret; Randi answers affectionately but with a polished omission. Yet Vee experiences that answer as proof of dependable intimacy: “the one sure thing the new year held.” My sympathy for Randi remains, but my distrust sharpens here because Vee’s confidence in her is so complete and so undeserved in this particular respect. Randi means “miss your face,” but Vee does not know she is being managed.

Pace is mostly absent, and that absence changes how I feel him. I still want his warmth when Vee remembers the first fra diavolo night—the sheet slipping, his looking, “nothing that needed saying.” That memory has real erotic charge because food, nakedness, and being seen flow together without strain. But then the imagined blonde occupies Vee’s place, and the charge curdles. Pace’s habit of “leaving room,” once one of his most attractive forms of care, now feels almost cruel in consequence: “He always left you the room—a thing she’d appreciated once, and hated now.” The text does not establish that he is with anyone else or that he intends to abandon her. Vee is inventing from fear, and I know it. Still, his total silence across the break feels earned as grounds for suspicion about what he thinks their relationship is and whether he will come after her at all.

Vee’s jealousy is painful rather than ugly. Her invented blonde—“a fashion model’s body where Vee’s was a stripper’s”—shows how quickly uncertainty recruits her old shame. The most upsetting moment is when the imagined woman takes over Vee’s own memory: “She could not keep her own night.” That is where the chapter peaks emotionally for me. It makes Pace’s refusal to name the bond feel destructive beyond the argument itself: without a shared word or promise, Vee cannot defend even her private past against the possibility that she was replaceable.

The erotic charge is a simmer rather than an erotic scene: strongest in the remembered cooking after sex, especially “the sheet slipping and him seeing and her not fixing it,” and in the imagined weight of Pace’s arms at midnight. But desire is inseparable from dread. The fantasy will not hold because another woman enters it. That is effective for me, not clinical.

The chapter’s only slight friction is the repeated insistence that Vee’s room belongs to her former self. It works beautifully, but by the final sentence—“the cold room kept watch over the girl who was gone”—I already understand the image thoroughly. That last restatement feels a touch underlined. Otherwise the slow pace earns itself because the waiting is the action: eight minutes become a whole emotional collapse.

“Old Acquaintances” first suggests people from Vee’s past—the girls on the board, the family routines, the childhood self preserved by the room. By the end, it also makes her current self and former Vivienne feel like old acquaintances who can no longer quite inhabit each other. More ominously, I wonder whether Pace is in danger of becoming one: someone intimate being pushed into the past without either of them saying it is over. The title did not give anything away; it widened as I read.

*With a Long Spoon* still does not have a settled literal meaning for me. It promises dangerous intimacy approached with care or distance, but nobody here has enough distance. The tagline feels brutally active: Vee freely gave Pace everything because his care made it safe to do so, yet she never had the hidden facts or even a mutually spoken account of what those yeses were building. She also freely trusts Randi, who is keeping the largest truth from her. The trap is no longer that Vee regrets her desire; it is that genuine intimacy has left her dependent on two people whose relationship to each other she cannot see.

I want Pace to contact her and answer the question rather than smoothing things over with food, sex, or care. I dread that Vee will return to Randi first, confide the whole rupture, and let Randi comfort or steer her while Randi knows exactly where Pace has been. My guess from here is that the new term will make Randi’s dependable public presence even more precious to Vee while Pace’s silence makes him feel less real—and that this imbalance will pull Vee further toward Randi before either hidden lover tells her the truth.
