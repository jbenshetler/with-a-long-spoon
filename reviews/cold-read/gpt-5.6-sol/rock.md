This chapter makes me feel very tender toward both Vee and Pace, and then sickens that tenderness with one quiet piece of arithmetic.

The porch works on me immediately: the cold against the glass, the quilt, the fire, the robe smelling of Pace. It is intimate in a way the more explicit scenes sometimes are not. It’s the same face.” His hand settling on her hip in answer is intensely effective. Nothing graphic happens, but I feel their bodies throughout.

Vee deepens enormously here. Her account of Ohio food is funny, affectionate, and exact; she can recognize limitation without despising the people who loved her. “Casseroles deep enough to forgive almost anything” made me laugh, while the unopened saffron catches her childhood beautifully. I also love how well she listens to Pace. When he tells her about being put in the hall, she does not offer automatic pity; she finds the injury exactly: “They took the thing you loved, and made it the punishment.” Then, with the older tutor, she understands both the love he experienced and the uglier adult word hovering around it. Her choice not to impose that word on him feels caring, though I am relieved the narration does not pretend the arithmetic is harmless.

My feeling toward Pace moves more sharply. Learning that Pace is really Peter feels startlingly intimate. “Pace” has always sounded like motion, timing, and control; “Peter” feels ordinary, vulnerable, and young. His childhood makes his precision and self-containment newly painful. The seventh-grade hallway is cruel, and “Less than staying” makes me suspect home was worse than he is willing or able to say. His account of mathematics is beautiful enough that, like Vee, I see the stars differently for a moment. “Most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it, and I got to read some” is the chapter’s radiant center.

Then comes the tutor. A nineteen-year-old assigned to a fifteen-year-old child, becoming his lover for two years, is deeply troubling. The text earns that alarm directly through the ages, her assigned responsibility, his isolation, and the careful flatness of his defense. Pace insists, “Nobody did anything wrong,” but I do not believe that sentence. I do believe him when he says it was the best two years he had known. That combination hurts: he has named a formative wound as luck. It also changes how I see his present habits—not by explaining them away, but by making me wonder what he learned about love, age, guidance, authority, and gratitude from being the brilliant child attached to the one older person who was “his.”

Trust remains divided. In this room, I trust Vee’s emotional instincts almost completely. The chapter makes his tenderness more real, not his deception less serious.

The chapter title, **“Rock,”** did not give anything away.
