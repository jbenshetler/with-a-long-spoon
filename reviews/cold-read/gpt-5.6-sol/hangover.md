This chapter made my chest ache. The water waiting by the bed, the paper towel protecting the wood, the pills, the clean shirt, the bread made while Vee slept: Pace’s care is so complete and so unadvertised that I melted before the chapter opened its deeper wound. “You need to eat before you decide how you feel” is intensely him—practical, tender, faintly controlling in a way that feels safe here because he is restoring her ability to choose and think. Vee waking with only scattered cards from the night could have made me uneasy, but nothing on the page suggests he exploited her helplessness; everything says he quietly got her safely through it. I trust him here.

Then “Sheri’s my friend. My one friend” landed like a floor giving way. I believed Pace’s explanation because it sounds worn smooth rather than freshly invented: the boy too young for the men around him, the man too far ahead of the boys his age, every ordinary friendship question opening over a gap. His claim that “The window opens once” may not be universally true, but I believe that it feels true to him. What hurts most is the neatness with which he contains it: “It isn’t tragic.” Vee sees that neatness as the tell, and so do I. My feeling toward him moved from warm gratitude for his caretaking into something rawer—not pity, exactly, but grief for how thoroughly he has made solitude look like a settled fact.

Vee’s inward movement worked beautifully on me. She does not flatten Daphne into either salvation or harm; she holds “the best two years he’d had” against “the thing about it that was wrong,” and cannot reconcile them. I can’t either. That restraint matters. The chapter makes Daphne’s place in his life feel even more troubling because she was not merely a lover but apparently his whole available human world, yet I still don’t know enough to fix precisely what happened between them. Vee’s realization that he had nobody to take that heartbreak to is the point that hurt most.

There is very little erotic charge here, but there is enormous romantic pull. Vee coming around the counter and giving Pace the same fully committed hug Sheri gave him made me ache for them. She doesn’t say sorry, diagnose him, promise to become everyone he has lacked, or force comfort onto him. She simply puts her weight against him and stays. His delayed embrace feels honest: “He held her and let her hold him and didn’t try to make either one of them feel better.” That is intimacy I trust. I want them fiercely in that moment, even while I worry that Vee’s own thought—“One person couldn’t be all of that”—may become painfully relevant now that she knows how little support he has.

My only friction is that Vee’s reconstruction of Pace at eighteen becomes unusually complete: “teaching himself to cook because there was no dining hall and no one to eat with.” Some of that reads as imaginative empathy rather than known fact, and the certainty briefly felt like the page pressing the sorrow harder than it needed to. The emotion had already landed. Still, the chapter mostly resists melodrama, especially by refusing to turn the final hug into a cure.

“Hangover” begins literally—the wrong light, the gravel voice, the missing cards, the miraculous water—but becomes the emotional aftereffect of everything Vee learned the night before. Sheri’s story has left Pace’s hidden history in the room after the alcohol is gone, and Vee wakes into it. The cover-board line, *WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK THREE — A PLACE AT THE TABLE*, feels especially pointed now. Pace can create a place at the table for everyone else, feed them, draw them out, and make them belong, while having spent much of his own life outside the ordinary tables where friendship begins. “With a long spoon” still carries a guarded, possibly dangerous promise for me—closeness requiring distance or care—but there is no tagline supplied. I want Vee to help widen Pace’s life without becoming his whole life; I expect this knowledge to deepen her love, and I dread her deciding that loving him means carrying everything alone.
