## Reader reaction

The first thing this chapter does to me is make me feel cared for alongside Vee. The water waiting on the paper towel, the pills, the missing stretch of night quietly managed, Pace pulling his undershirt over her and kissing her forehead when he thinks she is asleep—none of it feels like performance. It is intimate precisely because he expects no witness and no thanks. “You need to eat before you decide how you feel” may be one of the most Pace sentences in the book: practical, tender, and gently authoritative. I melt at it.

Then the chapter hurts me.

Pace having only one friend lands much harder than I expected. The party proved how socially gifted he is, so “Sheri’s my friend. My one friend. There isn’t anyone else” feels almost impossible until he explains it. His account of boys making friends through ordinary competition is sad in a particularly convincing way: he could never “lose to somebody the ordinary way, and lose again, and come out the other side friends.” He was exceptional in ways that isolated him before he was old enough to understand what isolation would cost.

The line that really catches in me is: “And the ones already there, when I was small among them…either weren’t safe or weren’t welcoming.” I feel dread there, but not suspicion of Pace. The page has earned the sense that something was badly wrong around his father and the adults available to him, while still leaving the facts closed. I do not know what happened, and I do not want to force the chapter’s careful silence into certainty.

His explanation of ordinary questions having “a gap under” them hurts just as much. Christmas, parents, bringing someone home: the casual exchanges that deepen friendships are exactly the places where he has learned to withhold. “It isn’t tragic” does not persuade me. It sounds like the same protective ordering he uses when he calls Daphne the best luck he had. Vee’s thought—“He set it in good order and the good order was the tell”—feels painfully right.

Daphne becomes more troubling here without becoming simple. Vee finally sees how being Pace’s only person made that relationship indispensable to him, even while its age and power imbalance remain “the wrong shape.” I appreciate that she cannot reconcile those truths and does not overwrite his lived happiness with her own judgment. I cannot reconcile them either. I ache for the fifteen-year-old who experienced that relationship as rescue, and I remain deeply uneasy about the nineteen-year-old tutor who became everything to him.

Vee moves me enormously in the final beat. Her mental inventory of Meg, Kayla, Cassie, and Randi makes the scale of Pace’s solitude bodily comprehensible. And then she does not say sorry or turn his disclosure into a scene where he must soothe her sorrow. She crosses the counter and holds him “with her whole weight committed,” echoing Sheri’s embrace. That is swoon for me—not sexual swoon, but love expressed with unusual emotional precision. Pace allows himself to be held without manufacturing a reassuring response. The ending leaves him neither healed nor dramatically broken, simply accompanied. I trust that.

I also like Vee’s ease about Sheri’s attraction: “Best thing that’s happened to my legs in a Virginia January.” There is no defensive flinch, only pleasure in having been desired. Pace understands exactly what she means and treats it as ordinary. That small exchange is warm, funny, and generous.

There is almost no erotic charge here, but the bodily intimacy remains strong: Vee waking inside Pace’s shirt, his robe around her, food restoring her, then her whole body committed to the embrace. It is sensual care, not sexual wanting.

My only friction is that Vee’s conclusion that Sheri is “the only kind” of woman Pace could keep because she could never want him feels a little too absolute. Pace said women misunderstand friendship; he did not say every woman does, and Sheri’s sexuality does not make attraction to him the sole possible obstacle to every friendship. I understand how Vee gets there, especially given the evidence of his life, but that sentence fixes the explanation more firmly than Pace himself did.

“Hangover” begins literally—the wrong light, fractured memory, thirst, nausea—but the deeper hangover is the delayed impact of what Sheri disclosed and what Pace now confirms. Like the cider, the enormity arrives “late, all at once, and more than she’d counted.” The title did not give anything important away; it opens as a physical joke and widens into emotional aftermath.

The cover-board title, **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION**, feels newly resonant here. “A Warm Reception” has promised welcome, and this run of chapters has delivered it repeatedly: Pace receives Vee’s people; Sheri receives Vee; Vee receives Sheri; now Vee receives a lonely truth Pace has carried without dramatizing it. But “WITH A LONG SPOON” still feels more oblique and warier. It suggests closeness requiring distance or caution, which fits how much warmth here coexists with secrets and histories nobody can yet touch directly. No cover tagline was supplied.

I want to know more about Pace’s father, but I do not want Vee to pry it loose before Pace chooses to speak. I also want this new understanding to deepen her love without making her decide she must become everything to him. My guess from here is that his solitude helps explain both the astonishing concentration of his care and his terror or refusal around naming bonds: relationships may feel less like one connection among many and more like structures capable of becoming his entire world, then disappearing. That is only a guess. What I dread is Vee turning that loneliness into a reason to excuse every omission—including Randi.
