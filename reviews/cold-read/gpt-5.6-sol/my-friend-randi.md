I felt Vee’s exhaustion before anything else: the red-eye, the crying car, the bare half of the dorm room, and that awful conviction that silence has already answered her. Her collapse into Randi’s arms landed hard. The smell of Randi’s neck, the hug held “past where a hug stops,” and Randi sweeping expensive new clothes onto the floor because Vee matters more gave me a real ache of tenderness and attraction between them. Vee takes comfort from Randi bodily before either of them speaks, and Randi plainly clings back. I wanted Vee to notice what was happening in that embrace, even as I understood why she could only experience it as safety.

But my trust in Randi dropped sharply here. She knows Pace did not use Vee; she knows Sheri is not the invented blonde rival; she knows exactly why Pace cannot say love; and, most damningly, she knows Vee’s problem is not lack of sexual access to him. Yet she reframes Vee’s entirely legitimate need for a named relationship as an ambush: “You backed him into a corner in his own house.” That made me angry on Vee’s behalf. Randi identifies Pace’s fear accurately, but she turns his refusal into something Vee did to him. Then she steers Vee toward offering her naked body instead of asking again for the words she needs: “You don’t do it with words. Words are the whole war.” This is not neutral friendship advice. It serves Pace, preserves the silence, and uses Vee’s trust at precisely her most vulnerable.

That does not erase Randi’s love. Her care feels genuine: she protects Vee from the downstairs audience, holds her while she cries, helps separate the imaginary Sheri from the real evidence, makes her laugh, and visibly withholds the painful truth of her own perfect-but-empty night with John. “Everywhere but the bedroom” is a graceful lie offered to comfort Vee, and I felt both the generosity and the loneliness in it. Randi’s long second hug also made her longing almost unbearable. She loves Vee, wants her, and is still manipulating her. Both truths are blazing at once now.

The coat plan is erotically charged because Vee’s shame immediately turns into wanting. “The wanting coming up hand in hand with the mortification and sharper for it” works on me; so does the suspended suggestion, Vee herself completing “And nothing under it.” The fantasy of crossing the cold and presenting herself at Pace’s warm door has real heat. But I cannot simply swoon, because Vee is being encouraged to use sex as a peace offering when the rupture was about recognition beyond sex. Her consent to the plan is real—she wants it—but it is being shaped by Randi’s concealed knowledge and by Vee’s terror of losing him. “Pace is not going to open his own door, see what you brought him, and say no” also presumes his consent instead of allowing him a choice, which jars in a book that has usually made asking matter.

The title, “My Friend Randi,” hurts through its simplicity. Vee thinks she is going to the one sure, transparent friendship in her life, while I know Randi is Pace’s lover, knows far more than she admits, desires Vee, and is actively directing her back toward him. “My friend” is completely sincere and terribly incomplete. The cover-board title, *WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION*, now promises appetite approached with dangerous distance and a literal warm reception at Pace’s house; Randi is preparing Vee to become the offering that secures it. No cover tagline was supplied. I want Vee and Pace to meet because the longing on both sides is excruciating, and I expect the doorstep gesture to overwhelm him. My dread, as a guess from here, is that sex will reunite them without resolving the missing name—and that Vee will mistake his desire for the answer she originally asked for.
