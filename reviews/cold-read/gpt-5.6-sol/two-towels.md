## Reader reaction

This chapter gets me in the chest before it gets erotic. Vee arriving primed for sex—wearing satin “hoping the man won’t hand her a reason not to show it to him”—and instead being offered something vastly more attentive is almost painfully seductive. Pace’s house makes him more attractive to me: the paired shoes, used kitchen, chaotic mathematics office, handmade furniture, childhood fantasy books, immaculate workshop. He feels like a whole private person rather than simply the perfectly attentive man Vee encounters on dates. His delight in the MRI application—“somebody sick, somebody scared, a kid who can’t lie still”—wins me especially hard.

I trust his behavior with Vee here. He stops their kiss despite wanting it; he doesn’t open the bedroom merely because she leans toward it; and when he finally explains the dress, he states clearly what measuring would require and leaves the decision open. Vee’s “Yes” feels entirely hers. That is also precisely why the cover line hurts: Pace has created an offer so exquisitely fitted to her that refusal is technically possible and emotionally almost unimaginable. My suspicion is not that this particular consent is false. It is that he knows the hidden context and Vee does not—and the chapter’s own language makes that concealed structure feel increasingly dangerous.

The two towels are the sharpest unease in the tour. Pace lives alone, yet there are “two towels on the rod.” Vee notices them, but doesn’t stop there. Given what I know from the jacket and earlier Randi chapters, I immediately read the second towel as Randi’s trace. The shut bedroom may conceal more. I’m now actively frightened that Vee could discover Randi’s presence through some domestic object while undressed and emotionally overwhelmed.

Erotically, the opening kiss works: his hand in her hair, its depth, his uneven breath, and especially his choosing to stop create strong charge. Her disappointment at the closed bedroom keeps it simmering. The highest charge, though, is the final proposition: being measured “down to what you’d wear under it, in good light.” It is intimate, exposing, and formally practical at once. The chapter ends before anything explicit happens, but the measuring is already vivid in my imagination. The long house tour doesn’t go slack because every room accumulates attraction and makes being seen by this man feel more consequential.

My one point of friction is how comprehensively ideal Pace becomes. He is a mathematician, powerlifter, cook, furniture maker, tailor, patent holder, and apparently compassionate medical innovator. Vee’s “And he lit up like a six-year-old” keeps him human, but by the dress reveal I am close to feeling the author’s thumb pressing hard. The repeated pattern—“one right thing for each thing he did”—is so perfectly flattering that I briefly resist it. Still, Vee’s overwhelmed response is believable because the chapter has shown exactly why this gift reaches her.

“Two Towels” gave away nothing before reading, but afterward it points directly to the quiet evidence of another person inside Pace’s apparently solitary life. It recolors the whole tour: Vee thinks she is reading him correctly from his objects, while one of the simplest objects may tell a truth she cannot yet interpret. *A Polite Invitation* now seems to be not merely the invitation to the house but Pace’s “If you want it”—an invitation into exposure disguised as the making of a dress. *With a Long Spoon* still has no settled meaning for me, though it suggests approaching something desirable and dangerous with distance or caution. “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap” lands with full force at the ending: this yes is warm, eager, and free, but Pace has built the offer from intimate knowledge Vee gave him without knowing the larger game.

I want the measuring scene immediately. I also dread it. My guess from here is that Pace will measure Vee in the glass porch, perhaps down to the satin she deliberately wore, and the practical process will become intensely erotic without him violating any stated boundary. I expect the second towel, closed bedroom, or another object to bring Randi dangerously near the surface. I also want to know whether Randi helped choose the burgundy; if she did, this gift is even less innocent than it appears.
