## Reader reaction

This chapter swept me up first and then left me cold in exactly the place Randi was cold. The skiing, the champagne, the blue dress, his hands crossing over his heart, the white-and-silver ballroom, the sleigh under falling snow—I felt the fairytale working on me. John’s most attractive quality is that he has ability “to spare and spent none of it proving it.” That landed. So did the easy quiet on the lift and the way he could meet Randi’s speed without competing with her. For a while I wanted the night for her because she wanted it so completely.

Randi feels painfully vivid here. I understand the seduction of John’s decisiveness after Pace’s relentless insistence that she expose every want aloud. “She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked” gave me a real flash of relief with her—not because John’s assumption is better consent, but because being continually required to name herself is exhausting and terrifying for Randi. Still, the hotel turn unsettled me: he “had never asked her where the night was going” and simply took her up. She freely chose to follow, but the page makes the difference between invitation and assumption impossible to miss.

The erotic charge rises through the dance more than through the sex. His firm lead, her giving up the steering, the room watching, and his midnight kiss all worked on me. The actual sex is deliberately distant: “Afterward” skips the act, and even the later image of her lipstick around him feels more like evidence than heat. Her body enjoyed it, but she experienced herself “from a long way off,” so I did too. The devastating line is that it meant no more than “a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon.” Pleasure happened; intimacy did not.

John does not feel sinister to me. He feels charming, socially effortless, practiced, presumptuous, and fundamentally unable to reach Randi. I don’t know whether he could have if she had offered more; the chapter leaves that open. Her judgment that he “never found her underneath” is true to her experience, but she also knows she “put nothing on the table.” What hurts is that he is not a disappointing version of the approved life. He is its best version, and it still leaves her untouched.

My feelings about Pace sharpened. Randi’s comparison makes his delicacy newly precious: his lead leaves room for her, and his asking forces her to be present in her choice. Yet “Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best” also brings back why Pace frightens her. I ache for her wanting him while believing he cannot belong in the life she has built. I have no new suspicion of his care here, only the existing distrust around what he and Randi conceal from Vee.

The deepest pull is Randi’s love for Vee. “Vee was her gorgeous” melted me and hurt. This is no longer merely attraction or fascination: Vee is “the best friend she had ever had,” the one person Randi loves without using the word as currency. Her fear is believable—she cannot imagine risking the hour of unguarded talk she already has for the hope of more. But “Let Vee arrive at it on her own” makes me uneasy because I know Randi is not simply waiting. She has been guiding Vee toward that arrival while preserving her own cover. The feeling is tender; the method remains manipulative.

The breakfast with Randi’s parents made her polished face feel less like glamour and more like inherited survival. Her father enjoys the invented boys, her mother is “all edges and points,” and Randi’s bracelet is “locked on her wrist.” I feel revulsion with her, but I don’t yet know enough to decide exactly what the bracelet means or why she keeps it out of the light. Her parents land as emotionally barren surfaces here, especially beside Vee’s family loving her aloud.

The only readerly friction is that the fairytale sequence becomes almost extravagantly perfect, but the excess feels like Randi’s own intoxicated construction rather than pressure for me to believe John is destiny. The chapter earns the reversal precisely by making him faultless. Nothing is wrong enough to explain her emptiness away.

“Fairytale” first names the night Randi thinks she is entering—the origin story she imagines telling a future daughter. By the end it means the beautiful, wholly true lie she sends Vee: “it’s honestly a fairytale out here.” Everything described is real, and none of it tells Vee what happened inside her. The title did not spoil the emotional destination for me; it became bitter only afterward.

*A Polite Invitation* now points even more strongly toward the difference between being asked, being assumed, and being maneuvered into wanting. John is less polite and easier; Pace’s invitations demand that Randi speak; Randi intends to let Vee “arrive” without disclosing how carefully the road was laid. *With a Long Spoon* still promises intimacy with something dangerous enough to require distance, though everyone keeps eating from much closer than that. The tagline—“Every yes was freely given. That was the problem.”—lands hard here: Randi’s yes to John is real, enthusiastic, and insufficient to make the night mean what she wanted it to mean.

I want Randi to tell Vee, badly, but I believe her refusal. My guess from here is that she will intensify the covered version of her desire—brunches, kisses, guidance—because direct confession feels more dangerous than continued manipulation. I dread Vee learning how much of her “arrival” was watched and shaped. I also want to know whether Randi goes back to Pace after this, because John has made brutally clear that safety cannot feed her.
