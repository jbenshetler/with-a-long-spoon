## Reader reaction

This hurt before it made me think. The warm house, the wine already poured, the chicken cooking, the music “slow that moved like water”—all the things that have made me melt for Pace—turn almost unbearable because Vee cannot live on their implied meaning anymore. I felt her braced body immediately: shoulders up, jaw set, the coin taste in her mouth, coat buttoned against the warmth he made especially for her. When she leaves the bag in the car, I knew she had come to demand an answer rather than settle into another beautiful evening.

I am fiercely with Vee here. She asks clearly, repeatedly, and without pretending the relationship has given her nothing: “I am not complaining about one minute of it.” That matters. She does not diminish the sex, food, tenderness, or bodily acceptance just because they no longer answer the question. Her account of what Pace gives her is almost a love declaration in itself, especially “Like my body comes with me attached.” But she needs their life to exist outside his house and outside her body. She needs language she can carry to Cassie, her father, her mother—and to herself. After all her earlier avoidance, watching her finally hold the question steady made me ache and root for her.

Pace’s “You’re the most important person in my life” landed with real force. I believe him. It may be the most direct statement of her importance he has given her, and for one breath I wanted it to be enough. But it does not answer “What am I?” Then he evades into comparison: “Do any of your friends have a relationship you’d rather have than this one?” That is true and still beside the point. For once, his habit of taking a question seriously and returning only what he can honestly offer does not feel courtly or careful; it feels like refusal.

“Words are cheap.” That was the moment my sympathy for Pace dropped sharply. Not because I suddenly doubt his care—the entire room is evidence of it—but because Vee is explicitly telling him that words are the missing form of care, and he dismisses their value because actions are the language he prefers. Her answer, “Words are cheap, and you still won’t spend them on me,” is devastating and exactly right. The image turns language into another provision, and this is the one thing he will not set out for her.

His folding his arms and going silent is the first time his stillness has felt actively cold to me. Something “went across his face and shut,” but the page does not tell me what: fear, resistance, some conviction, something hidden. I cannot resolve it. I do not think he is indifferent—his face opened when she arrived, he calls her the most important person in his life, and the instant she cries his coldness collapses and he reaches for her. But the reach hurts because it is the familiar answer she has just said she cannot accept: “the hands that settled everything,” offered “in place of the word.” I trust that he wants to comfort her. I no longer trust that practical tenderness alone can carry what he asks her to believe.

There is no erotic payoff here, but the erotic history is everywhere. “We have sex, so much of it, and it’s the best of my life” has charge because I remember exactly what she means, yet the chapter deliberately refuses to let sexual fulfillment settle the emotional question. The warm domestic sensuality still pulls at me—the scent of him, the food, the low light—but now it makes the absence sharper rather than making me swoon. My strongest romantic ache is not a kiss; it is Vee refusing the kiss at the door and making him see that their ritual cannot proceed unchanged.

I have no new suspicion that Pace’s tenderness has been false. The text earns a different unease: he may mean everything he does and still be unwilling—or unable—to give Vee the named, public bond she needs. Against the secret fact of Randi and the original plan, his refusal feels worse, because Vee is asking what she is while he knows far more about the arrangement around her than she does. But this scene itself does not reveal why he refuses, and I am leaving that open.

There was almost no reader friction for me. Pace initially taking “What are we doing?” as a question about dinner could have felt evasive, but his literal answer fits him and makes Vee’s loneliness sharper. The chapter is concentrated and earned. The only pressure I felt was the repeated insistence on “the word,” but after the last chapters, Vee has been circling this exact wound long enough that the repetition feels like accumulation, not over-explanation.

“Believe Me” points directly to Pace’s demand: “Believe what I do.” But the title also becomes Vee’s unspoken demand of him—believe her when she says actions are no longer enough, believe that naming matters. It did not give the confrontation away beforehand, but afterward it feels painfully double-sided: both of them are asking to have their preferred evidence accepted.

*A Polite Invitation* now feels darker. Pace’s unlocked door, warm house, prepared wine, food, and planned evening are still an invitation, but Vee stops on the mat and refuses to cross fully into it. She wants to know what the invitation means beyond the room. *With a Long Spoon* still suggests appetite served at a distance, something enticing that requires careful handling; here Vee can no longer bear the distance built into the feast. The tagline, “Every yes was freely given. That was the problem,” feels newly exact: Vee has freely said yes to everything inside the house, but none of those yeses secured an answer about what she is outside it.

I want Pace to go after her only if he can actually answer, not merely warm her, feed her, touch her, or bring her back inside. I dread Vee interpreting this as proof that all his tenderness meant nothing, because I do not believe that. My guess from here is that the rupture will force whatever Pace has been protecting with silence into view—but I cannot tell whether the answer will be love, a limitation he has never named, or something involving the hidden life he shares with Randi.
