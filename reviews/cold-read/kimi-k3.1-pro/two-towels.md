## Reader reaction

I melted at this chapter, properly. Not immediately — the first kiss was lovely, and Pace being the one to stop it had exactly the frustrating, arresting effect on me that it had on Vee — but the dress undid me. The crucial turn was her brief disappointment that the silk was not the magazine red, followed by realizing that “he hadn’t matched the dress she’d described. He’d matched *her*.” That is such a precise form of being loved: not retrieving her fantasy literally, but understanding what the fantasy was trying to do for her. And then the tagless flannel becoming evidence, the absurd answer suddenly plain — I had the same delayed click she did. When she cannot finish “No one had ever—” and her eyes fill, mine nearly did too.

Pace has never been more attractive to me, and it is not only the competence, although this chapter lays that on lavishly. It is that he watches her hand on the furniture joint rather than her face; that he does not explain his work “down to her size”; that his favorite part of the patent is a frightened child spending less time inside an MRI tube. “Patience. Paying attention” could have sounded like a motto, but here it feels like the actual mechanism of his tenderness. His restraint is erotic rather than bloodless. He leaves the bedroom door shut while she has dressed underneath everything specifically hoping it might open, and I felt her drop. “Something in her dropped. He touched her elbow… and she went, and didn’t let her face do a thing.” That hurt in the good, heated way: she is practicing reaching, and he keeps requiring her wanting to remain her own choice rather than rewarding its first signal.

Vee herself feels wonderfully alive here — nosy, blunt, mathematically curious, vain enough to mind the wrong red for half a second, brave enough to say “Show me,” and still careful enough to hide disappointment. I love that her first response to the gift is “I can’t sew,” a small panic at being handed something beyond her competence, and that Pace simply removes the obligation. Her final “Yes — of course, yes” is unhedged, but the cost is clear: accepting the dress means consenting to be measured, seen, fitted, down to her underwear. That makes the yes both romantic and bodily. I feel fiercely protective of her because I know what she does not: this apparently private courtship began with Randi and Pace selecting her. The sincerity of the silk does not erase that deception, though neither does the deception prove the silk insincere. I am holding those together rather than choosing one.

My trust in Pace has warmed without becoming simple. Nothing in his manner newly feels false. The too-warm house, the low piano, the closed bedroom, and especially the two towels do acquire a faint charge because I have just seen Randi leave this house after sex and refuse to stay. But the chapter supplies no actual evidence that Randi is still nearby or that the towels mean anything clandestine; they might simply be a man’s ordinary guest provision. I am suspicious of the situation because of what I already know, not because Pace suddenly slips. Randi’s absence is almost louder for that. While Vee is being handed back something she thought she had thrown away, Randi is still hiding the self Pace found, and I dread what this depth of genuine attachment will do inside their arrangement.

If there is friction, it is the sheer accumulation of Pace’s excellences: precise directions, immaculate tools, beautiful joinery, books, cooking equipment, mathematical generosity, a humane patent, and now dressmaking. A lesser version would make me laugh and call him a fantasy assembled from catalog pages. It mostly survives because Vee is visibly dazzled and because he remains sheepish rather than self-congratulatory, but the MRI explanation did feel like one last careful brush of gold leaf. Still, I would not cut the tour. Every room converts an abstract virtue into something she can touch.

“Two Towels” means less as an event than as a little domestic disturbance after the fact: a pair, a preparation, a house arranged to receive someone. It did not give anything away, but it makes that plain bathroom detail keep blinking at me. **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION** now feels exquisitely apt: the invitation is polite in every particular, from “If you’ll let me” to “If you want it,” while I know there is more at the table than Vee can see. *With a Long Spoon* suggests that courtesy and distance may not keep her safe from whatever she is drawing nearer. No separate cover tagline was supplied. Next I want — and nervously anticipate — the measuring itself: good light, satin, Vee exposed not merely naked but accurately seen. My guess from here is that the dress will become one of the things she cannot easily surrender when she eventually learns how she was chosen.
