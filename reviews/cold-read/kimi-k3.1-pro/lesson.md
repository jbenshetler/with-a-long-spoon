## Reader reaction

The dancing undid me before the jealousy did. I loved that Pace does not ask Vee to become deliciously helpless; he makes her participate. “Give me something to push back against,” he says, and then waits while she learns that yielding to a lead is not the same as going limp. The patience of the concrete-pad lesson, the battered boom box, “There’s no sorry unless you take off a foot,” and then her sudden discovery that the pattern can arrive “through his body before it arrived in her feet” — that was genuinely swoony. His hand at her shoulder blade carried more erotic charge for me than a description of undressing would have. When she closes the protective space between them because “But you’ll step on my foot,” and he explains that the space is precisely what gets her stepped on, I felt the whole chapter go warm.

Pace deepened for me here. After the private vulnerability of *Rock*, it was good to see him in a room full of people who know him: a former dance teacher, someone regulars hug, someone with a competence and a social history Vee did not create. The women’s attention made me understand her jealousy without making me think he had encouraged it. He is even with everyone, introduces her each time, and answers her ugly question without evasion. The ease draining out of his face after “Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe from here, too?” hurt. I believed his hurt more than I believed her accusation. The bartender’s account — he takes drunk women home, leaves them at the door, and does not bring dates as a rule — confirmed what the chapter had already shown me rather than rescuing him from behavior I had actually witnessed.

Vee made me ache and then made me furious. The blonde’s approach is unmistakably needling: “Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to,” followed by “All us girls love him,” feels designed to remain warm on the surface while depositing something poisonous. I do not know what history, if any, lies behind it, but Vee receives exactly the wound it offers. Her mother’s voice is immediately waiting — “a man like that has girls” — and then her own shame supplies the rest: “a few dates and she’d spread her legs like a slut.” That does not excuse her cruelty, but it makes the cruelty horribly legible. She is not merely jealous; she is punishing Pace for having made her want him. Watching her turn down men, nurse a drink, and keep interrogating the room long after he has left was miserable in the right way.

What restored my sympathy was that she does not get to remain comfortably deceived. “Wasn’t him they were jealous of, honey” is almost too neat a verdict, but the older bartender’s flatness keeps it from becoming a sentimental absolution. Back in the dorm, Vee finally sees the man whole: “He could have had any one of them, and he hadn’t. He’d chosen her.” Her recognition that she “punished him for being the man she wanted him to be” feels earned by the sleepless night, not pasted on. The washed flannel got to me — she has finally laundered away the last smell of him and puts it on anyway, “smelling of fabric softener and no one.” And her refusal to wake Cassie hurt differently. Cassie would come instantly or sit up instantly; Vee cannot bear to make her actions sayable to the person who loves her most plainly.

There is an unease in “Don’t think. Just feel the lead,” especially because I know how thoroughly Randi and Pace have arranged Vee’s path, but I do not want to pretend the dance scene itself felt coercive. She asks, practices, resists, laughs, pushes back, and chooses to return to the floor. My more interesting doubt is Vee’s own fleeting one: “how much of this was Pace, and how much was the Pace she’d been building out of wanting him.” I trust his tenderness; I trust less her ability to keep seeing him rather than her rescue fantasy. The ending turns that unease toward Randi. Vee decides she has been wrong and will fix it — good — but her first move is to go to Randi, not Pace. I want her to apologize directly. I dread watching her hand this intimate failure to Pace’s lover, particularly after Randi’s jealousy and hunger in *Gone*. What Randi will do with it, I genuinely cannot settle.

“Lesson” is first the dance lesson, then the lesson Vee takes from the blonde and the bartender, and finally the harder one about what shame can make her do to someone good. It gave nothing away; it kept acquiring meanings. *With a Long Spoon* still suggests shared appetite shadowed by the need for caution. *A Warm Reception* and the blurb’s closing beat — “They welcomed her warmly. A warm reception is also what you call walking into fire.” — felt especially pointed in a bar that is literally warm with bodies, gives Pace an affectionate reception, and leaves Vee alone in the cold. I am pulled hard toward the next chapter: I want repair, and I fear she is carrying the apology into the most compromised hands available.
