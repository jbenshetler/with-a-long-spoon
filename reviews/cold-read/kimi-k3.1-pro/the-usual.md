## Reader reaction

This chapter ached in a different place than the last three. I’d been angry at Pace alongside Vee, and I still am, but being inside his silence made it much harder to reduce him to a man simply refusing her. The diner gave me a little warmth first — Sheri stealing his fries, the plates being restored to their rightful owners, the easy relief of someone who wants nothing from him but his company — and then the house slowly took all of it away. The sentence that undid me was: “at the moment she had asked him to be brave he had been weak.” That is not an excuse, and the chapter doesn’t ask me to treat it as one. It is the first time I feel he has located the actual injury.

Sheri became a whole person here rather than Pace’s blunt, useful friend. Paige, the big laugh, the noisy seven-person table she loved, and the girlfriend who could not stop prescribing reconciliation with her family — that hurt. “People who’ve never lost anything think grief is a choice” landed without becoming a speech. And she is exactly right with Pace: “Then not saying it matters more to you than having her.” I believed her impatience because she also sees when he is handing her his best material to avoid the real answer. Her crudeness about Vee’s body made me laugh rather than recoil; there is no appetite underneath it, just friendship with its boots on.

The swoon, such as it is, came from hearing what Pace actually treasures. Not only the air that “crackles,” but Vee understanding what his food is *for*, Vee looking directly into his camera instead of dropping her eyes, Vee’s face opening before she can compliment the first bite. That is being known in return, and it explains his devastation better than any generalized declaration could. But the imagined kitchen with Randi and Vee laughing “after” gave me a complicated pang rather than a clean one. It is domestically beautiful, and he plainly wants both women with real tenderness. Still, Vee does not know the arrangement he is privately furnishing for her. His wondering “how she would carry it” feels insufficient beside what she has never been told. I also felt his blindness around Randi: he misses her, honors her choice not to come, and yet still converts that terrible crying into a story he can wait out.

The erotic charge stayed at the edges — the remembered sidewalk kiss, Randi telling him about it while he was inside her, Vee’s unguarded body crossing his bedroom at night. Those images are alive, but grief keeps overtaking them. Even his three-person morning fantasy is less arousing to me than yearning. The sexual content did not go slack; the chapter simply was not running on that current.

Daphne’s fuller story worked on me, especially the brown hair, glasses, hand over her mouth, and the terrible line, “Daphne was the person he would have told.” His belief that the words themselves made loss unsurvivable is painfully understandable and plainly false as a way to live. The retrospective becomes a little diagrammatic around “He had taught himself that the words were the difference,” but the specific memories keep it human. Then the engine passing without slowing absolutely got me: his body assembling Vee’s arrival before his mind can stop it, and the hope lingering even after he knows the sound is wrong. His sobbing over prison food and then eating anyway was the bleakest, most convincing ending the chapter could have had.

“The Usual” means the diner order, but also Pace’s established way of surviving: routine, measured nourishment, other people’s needs met, his own want kept portioned and silent. The warmth of a place where everybody knows what he wants contrasts cruelly with the house where he cannot ask for what he wants. **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION** now feels poised between feeding and distance, and between literal warmth and the reception one person offers another. This chapter gives Pace a warm reception with Sheri and denies him the one he keeps imagining from Vee. No cover tagline was supplied. From here I want him to do more than understand himself: I want the truth spoken to Vee, while she is allowed to remain angry. I dread him treating this realization as sufficient. My guess — only a guess — is that Randi’s six-week silence and Vee’s absence are converging toward a break in his carefully preserved arrangement, but I cannot yet see him choosing how.
