## Reader reaction

I held my breath through that walk, and then I wanted to applaud. The first small, careful step was so recognizably Vee—the hand at her hip, the managed slit, the lifelong work of taking up less room—that “She took a breath, and made the second step long” felt like a genuine physical release. And it was hot: not because she was bare beneath the dress, but because she chose the exact moment to stop protecting the room from her body. “She had good legs. She stalked across the stage giving them away a step at a time” put the wanting squarely in her own stride. The murmur waking the dull room, the heat rising to meet her decision, the decorous hand closing the slit again afterward—I felt the whole exhibitionistic charge of being seen and of knowing precisely what the room could not prove.

The mother material hurt more than I expected. Her admission that she is both sad and relieved is painfully honest, but the fuller truth only arrives once she is seated: “nothing at all under the silk, and nobody in this room who had ever taught her what to be ashamed of.” That made the display feel bigger than daring. There is no mother’s face to monitor, no internal spectator she can quite satisfy, and the freedom is exhilarating without being clean or simple. I loved that the chapter did not turn this into a recovered, shame-free Vee. Her heart is pounding; pride and arousal remain tangled. By the final sentence, she cannot separate them, and neither could I—and I did not want the book to separate them for me.

Cassie was lovely here. “It’s a folding chair and a rolled-up piece of paper” is practical kindness, but the chapter rightly lets Vee feel that it is “not it”; Cassie cannot answer the confession Vee herself cannot yet articulate. Still, taking the phone, walking her to the doors, failing to finish “You look—,” and then catching that extraordinary photograph all felt like devotion without fuss. “Honor student,” dry and damp-eyed, got me. Pace and Randi are absent, yet the scene is full of what they have given her: his burgundy silk, her heels and plum polish. Sending Pace the uncropped frame was tender and sly—a gift to the man who built the dress, while the knowledge of what else the photograph contains remains hers. That privacy did not feel deceptive to me; it felt like Vee keeping one part of her becoming for herself.

I had no new suspicion of anyone here. Randi’s officers’ meeting and Pace’s Berkeley workshop are simply the circumstances on the page. The ache comes from what I already know and Vee does not: the two people woven into this triumphant appearance are lovers, and she still experiences them as separate sources of being chosen. That knowledge made the photograph’s journey to Pace especially charged, but it did not spoil her walk. Nothing in this chapter took the walk away from her.

I had no real friction. The opening waits a little among absences and explanations, but I needed that stillness for the second step to land. “The Induction” is beautifully literal and then not literal at all: she is inducted into an honor society, yes, but also into visibility, appetite, and the possibility of letting a room want her without immediately apologizing. The cover’s **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION** continues to suggest appetite, stirring, and being slowly fed; here “a warm reception” also becomes the room literally warming to her. No cover tagline was supplied. Next I want Pace’s response to the photograph, and I want to know what Vee will tell Randi about the walk—and what she will leave out. My larger dread remains the eventual collision between these two intimacies, though from here I cannot guess whether Vee will experience it first as betrayal, possibility, or both.
