## Reader reaction

This one hurt quietly. After the sexual and emotional saturation of “Four,” the cold lecture hall and the three girls in their usual seats felt almost ordinary — Randi’s head on Vee’s shoulder, Cassie empty-handed and certain, Vee happily boxed in between them. Then Cassie set down, “My dad got laid off in 2020,” with no handle on it, and I felt the project stop being an assignment. I loved Cassie in that moment: not campaigning, not asking to be comforted, just finally supplying the fact underneath her certainty. Randi’s answering faith in her father felt equally real to me. I didn’t read her as knowingly defending a lie; she seemed to be carrying a home truth she had never needed to test.

The stillness when MIRA opened into MIRANDA was the strongest moment in the chapter. “Not a motion — the withdrawal of motion” is exactly how it felt reading it: the whole busy, sparkling person suddenly wasn’t there. My stomach dropped for her. Those ragged “Cheating bastards” made me ache more than a bigger breakdown would have, especially because Cassie heard agreement and Vee heard something aimed much closer to home. Randi became more sympathetic to me here, not more suspicious. Whatever else she controls and performs, this looked like a daughter discovering that the story she had offered confidently was not true. Her brightness returning by the end didn’t reassure me; it hurt, because Vee alone had seen what the brightness cost.

Vee’s response swept me up emotionally. Sliding the laptop toward herself, manufacturing decisions, giving Randi “somewhere to put her hands,” keeping her included without making her explain — that is tender, intelligent care. She does the thing Pace so often does for her: she makes a place where someone can remain composed. But I also felt a small, genuine unease. Cassie is the one whose father was laid off, Cassie is sitting right there, and Vee decides without consulting anyone that protecting Randi means letting her remain unaware. I understand the impulse completely and still don’t find it clean. Randi “had no idea she’d been looked at,” so this isn’t even yet a shared confidence. It is Vee’s secret about Randi, kept partly from Cassie.

There was no erotic charge for me, and I didn’t miss it. The pull was the intimacy of perception: Vee watching Randi rather than the screen, feeling her leave and return. That reverses so many recent scenes in which Vee has been the one exposed under somebody else’s gaze. Here she is the person who sees, and she tries to make seeing merciful. The final image — the largest point returning to being one among a hundred thousand, “easy to see if you knew to look for it, invisible if you didn’t” — landed beautifully and left me cold.

I had no real friction with the chapter. The opening lecture and division of labor take their time, but that normality makes the record’s arrival brutal; even the professor’s rote clicking contrasts well with the one click that changes the room. The title, **“The Outlier,”** is immediately the MIRA point, but it also recolors Randi’s county as the bright exception she expected would vindicate her father — and Randi herself as someone whose private story no longer fits the account she gives. It brings back the earlier statistics lecture about outliers, especially the kind carried forward into everything after, without settling for me what Vee’s suppression of this knowledge will mean.

The cover titles, **WITH A LONG SPOON** and **BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION**, feel increasingly apt to a book where nourishment, welcome, and concealment sit at the same table. This chapter’s warm reception is Vee’s protection of Randi; the truth Randi receives is anything but warm. No cover tagline was supplied. From here, I want the next project session badly. Will the point be accounted for, set aside, or simply left unseen? My guess — only a guess — is that Vee will continue protecting Randi until silence itself becomes difficult. I dread Cassie discovering that Vee knew, and I equally dread Randi discovering the mercy after the fact.
