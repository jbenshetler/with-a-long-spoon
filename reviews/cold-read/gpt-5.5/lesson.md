### Chapter record

Wednesday night, Meg’s door is open two doors down from Vee’s. Vee enters talking before she is all the way in.

Vee says Pace asked her dancing on Thursday night, country dancing. He is picking her up. She told him she cannot dance and does not know how; he said, “If you want to, I’ll teach you.” He moved the date up to seven so there is time to teach her.

Meg says Dana on Third has ropers and is close enough to Vee’s size. Meg says dance girls trade and everybody has danced in everybody’s boots.

Meg gives Vee a black shirt on a hanger. The shirt has a keyhole at the neckline. On Meg it would have been a suggestion; on Vee “the keyhole had stopped suggesting.” Vee starts to lift her hand toward the mirror, then puts it back down. Meg tells her jeans, because serious dancers wear jeans.

Vee is dressed by five-thirty. She changes the shirt and changes it back.

Pace is in the common area when Vee comes off the elevator. He wears recently polished boots, jeans with a crease pressed into them, and a short-sleeved knit shirt despite the October cold because he has dressed for dancing rather than weather. He is freshly shaved. He carries a battered boom box with its handle wrapped in electrical tape, a machine from a decade Vee had not been alive for. He holds the door.

Vee and Pace walk across campus, up past the science buildings toward the basketball gym. There is no game; the building is dark. On the upper side is an empty concrete pad in the spill of field lights. Pace sets down the boom box and plays low country music.

Pace teaches Vee the three-step. He says most of what she will hear that night fits the three-step better than the two-step. For the same beat, the three-step takes slower steps than the two-step. The basic step is back on the right, back on the right, then back on the left: “One-and-two, three-four.” The “and” is a change of weight that lets the same foot go back twice.

Pace shows Vee the woman’s part by standing in front of her facing away so their feet take the same steps. He takes her right hand in his left and extends it straight to the side. His right hand comes under her left arm, the heel of his hand settling against her back, fingers curled and touching her shoulder blade.

Pace tells Vee she has a job: give him something to push back against so she can feel his lead. He tells her to push on his shoulder, firm but not hard. He says being jelly does not work. He presses at her shoulder blade and says that is where the information goes through. He tells her not to look at her feet.

Vee repeatedly softens and goes “jelly.” Pace corrects her without comment by repositioning, waiting, and starting again. Then she overcorrects and holds herself out with her left arm. Pace tells her the space she is keeping is what gets her stepped on; if she comes all the way in, his feet cannot land anywhere but past hers. She says “That’s not helpful” when he tells her not to think and just feel the lead.

The boom box tape warbles up to speed. Vee says the music is something her granddad listened to. Pace says older songs have a clearer beat and do not lose it in the extra. The music is sparse and pretty.

Vee fights the lesson for ten minutes by anticipating motion before the lead arrives. Pace keeps the frame even, waits out each early step, and picks the count back up. When Vee says sorry, Pace says there is no sorry unless she takes off a foot. Vee laughs, then Pace laughs too.

Vee finally stops guessing. The pressure of her hand on his shoulder becomes readable information. The pattern comes through Pace’s body before it arrives in her feet. They go around the pad once, twice. Pace says quietly, “There it is.”

The boom box rides behind the seat of Pace’s truck as the town falls away behind them.

The Rusty Nail is loud and warm when they enter, warm from bodies rather than heat being turned up. It is a real crowd of people who know what they are doing. The floor is already moving. Vee pauses in the entrance because she is going to be visible.

Pace pays at the door. A girl at the till stamps the backs of their hands and says “hey, Pace” familiarly. They look for a table along the wall. People know Pace. A man catches his shoulder passing; two women come from the rail with arms open. There are hugs. Pace introduces Vee each time as “This is Vee.” Vee gets handshakes or one-armed hugs mostly meant for him.

One woman keeps a hand on Pace’s arm and tells Vee he taught the class and is the reason half of them can dance. Pace says he taught two semesters: beginner country and a little swing.

The crowd includes more women than men. Women hug Pace longer, with more of themselves. Pace receives it unhurriedly and evenly, no more for one than another, and moves them gently along. Vee begins an unintended count of them.

Pace takes Vee’s hand and leads her onto the floor. For one song Vee is good because Pace’s lead is legible. Then she notices people watching, including a couple and a woman past Pace’s shoulder, starts counting in her head, and falls behind. She tells herself not to think and to feel the lead. She gives the frame her weight, and the floor comes back to her. She stops counting and does not touch other couples.

Vee notices the room is partly watching them, mostly women, with “that particular awareness of other women tracking a couple on the floor.” She thinks she is doing well, then that he is making her, then wonders how many women he has taught, but lets it go.

Pace goes to the bar for drinks. Vee stands near the wall with her arms at her sides, warm, pulse in her neck.

A blonde about Vee’s age speaks to her. She is country pretty in standard issue: hair done, makeup unapologetically makeup, working in the dark and too much by daylight. She tells Vee they look great together and says Pace is a great lead who knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to. She says all the girls love him and he is good about getting a girl home safe at the end of the night. She leaves pleased with herself before Vee can answer.

Vee thinks “The girls.” She sees the bright smiles, shiny eyes, and big hugs for Pace. She had felt like an exception and now feels she is looking at the rule.

Pace returns with two longneck beers, loose and easy from the floor, and offers one to Vee. She takes it and says, with venom, “Does your service include getting the girls home safe from here, too?” Pace’s ease drains out, not into anger but into stillness. He says yes, sometimes, when required. Vee says she does not need him to get her home safely and will handle that herself. She sets the bottle on the ledge and walks off fast, just away.

Vee is angry about the girls and angrier that she let herself be played: “three dates, and she’d spread her legs like a slut.” She gets away before any of it reaches her face.

From across the room, Vee watches Pace stand where she left him with his bottle. Regulars drift over, including someone putting a hand on his shoulder and someone leaning in to say something. Pace shakes his head, sets his beer down on the bar untouched, goes out, and the door lets in a wedge of cold before shutting behind him.

Vee stays at The Rusty Nail. She finds a stretch of wall and watches the floor. She notices girls she had not noticed while dancing: bare midriffs, shirts cut very low, tanned in October, bodies that know they are being looked at and move like they like it. She thinks “Floozies.” She wonders whether Pace taught them to dance, got them home safe, and how many know what his pillow smells like. She knows what she is doing and does it anyway. She thinks she had thought she was special and is just next.

Vee moves to a stool at the end of the bar. She nurses one drink and makes it last. Men come by because she is a woman sitting alone; she turns each down without quite looking until they stop coming. The band packs up. The floor thins. Vee stays until the lights begin to come up.

An older woman behind the bar moves with the economy of someone who has worked the room a long time. She sets a glass of water in front of Vee without being asked. She says she heard Vee tore into Pace. Vee says she does not need a man who “gets all the girls home safely.”

The bartender says Pace is a good guy. When a girl has had too much, Pace gets her home, leaves her at the door, and does not take advantage; the bartender says she would have heard otherwise. She says plenty of women have tried, hoping he would. She says Pace does not bring dates, and tonight he brought one on a Thursday, when people come there to dance. She tells Vee it was not Pace the women were jealous of.

Vee sits there as the lights come up fully.

Outside, Vee has her phone in her hand and could call Cassie, but does not. She knows Cassie would come immediately in whatever she had been sleeping in, and that there would be questions that night or in the morning. Vee cannot put words to what she has done. She calls a cab.

The Rusty Nail lot empties, with the last of the crowd spilling loud and unsteady into the cold. Vee waits under the one good light by the door, where the dark does not quite reach. The cab takes its time. She hears it before seeing it: something loose and belching under the hood, run well past maintaining. It lurches to the curb.

The cab’s back seat has a smell and sag Vee chooses not to investigate. The driver sees her in the mirror and asks, “Long night?” Vee gives him the address and nothing else, keeping her eyes on the window. The fare is more than she guessed, and the money had been meant for other things.

Vee lets herself into the dorm room. Cassie’s side is dark; Cassie’s breathing is slow and even. Vee eases the door shut, crosses without a light, pulls off the borrowed boots, peels off the borrowed shirt, washes her face in very cold water, and brushes her teeth in the dark.

Pace’s shirt hangs on the closet hook. It is hers now after the trade, though Vee has never thought of it as anything but his. She slept in it and did not wash it, rationing his smell until what was left went stale and wrong; then she finally washed it. It came back smelling of fabric softener and no one. She puts it on anyway and lies on top of the covers.

The room feels wrong at that hour: not dark enough, not quiet enough, with Cassie’s breathing the only easy thing. Vee cannot stop seeing Pace through the other women’s eyes. She thinks he made them light on their feet, feminine, safe, and like real people; he kept them safe; he could have had any of them and had not; he had chosen her. She understands she threw all of that in his face and punished him for being the man she wanted him to be. She thinks she did it to a good man.

Under the shame, Vee keeps feeling the sensation of Pace’s hand at her shoulder blade on the pad, the lead arriving before her feet knew what to do, and the moment the dance took her and she stopped trying to guess. She lies awake with both shame and sensation. She does not sleep.

When it is light, Vee knows what she will do, though not how: go to Randi’s. Her embarrassment has cooled into information. She was wrong, and she is going to fix it.
