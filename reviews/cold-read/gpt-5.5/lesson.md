## Reader reaction

This chapter made me physically tense in a way the recent lush Pace/Vee intimacy hadn’t. The dance lesson itself is deeply seductive — not overtly sexual, but almost embarrassingly intimate. His hand at her shoulder blade, the frame, the insistence that she not collapse into “jelly,” the line “Give me something to push back against” — all of that works on me. It feels like another version of everything Pace does with Vee: he gives structure, attention, permission, and then her body discovers something before her mind can manage it. The erotic peak for me is not at the CW, honestly; it is on the cold concrete pad when “the pattern came through his body before it arrived in her feet.” That is hot because it’s trust becoming physical.

But then the room changes everything. The CW makes Pace social in a way I haven’t really seen before. He isn’t just the solitary man at the end of the long drive, the private cook/mathematician/maker/lover. He has a public sensual competence. People know him. Women hug him. A girl at the till says “hey, Pace” like he belongs there. Someone says he taught the class. And Vee’s reaction hurts because I believe it. She has been feeling chosen, singular, remade. Suddenly she sees a whole female ecosystem around him and feels herself become one of many.

The blonde’s line is awful and perfect: “Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to.” It lands exactly on the nerve of the book for me. On the surface she’s teasing about dancing; underneath, it clangs against the cover tagline and everything I already know about Pace and Randi choosing Vee without telling her. The text earns Vee’s flare of suspicion here even though, on the immediate facts, she is wrong about Pace’s behavior at the CW. The bartender later gives me a very credible correction: Pace gets drunk girls home and “Leaves her at the door. Doesn’t take advantage.” That restores my trust in this particular version of him — almost painfully — but it does not erase the larger unease, because the phrase “get a girl to do just what he wants her to” is still true in another key. He can lead. He can make surrender feel like competence.

I felt very protective of Vee and also frustrated with her, in a sympathetic way. Her cruelty is ugly: “Does your service include getting the girls home safe from here, too?” She hears the venom herself, and I’m glad the chapter lets her know immediately that she has crossed a line. Pace’s response — “Yes. Sometimes. When it’s required.” — is plain and restrained. I believed his hurt because “the good mood just draining off him” is so unshowy. He doesn’t argue, punish, chase, or perform wounded innocence. He leaves. That moved me toward him again in the local scene.

At the same time, Vee’s spiral after he leaves is excruciatingly legible. “She’d thought she was special. She was just next.” That is the chapter’s wound. And the “Floozies” thought made me flinch because it is her mother’s shame-language coming out of her and aiming itself at other women. She watches women with bare midriffs and confident bodies and uses the old dirty word. It’s painful because she has been trying to climb out of exactly that inheritance.

The bartender is a strong minor presence. I trusted her immediately: older, economical, not sentimental, not flattering Vee. Her correction matters because she is not dazzled by Pace romantically; she is someone who has worked the room long enough to know its patterns. “Wasn’t him they were jealous of, honey” is a lovely reversal. Vee thought the women were looking at Pace as shared property; the bartender says they were looking at Vee as the rare date. That doesn’t solve everything, but it cracks Vee’s certainty.

The last dorm-room section really hurt. The shirt is devastating now. It used to be a warm trace of Pace, then a trade-object tied to her panties and their private erotic world. Here it has been washed; it “came back smelling of fabric softener and no one.” She puts it on anyway. That feels like trying to retrieve intimacy after she has damaged it. And the chapter is honest that two truths sit side by side: shame that she punished a good man, and the bodily memory of the dance, “the lead arriving before her feet knew what to do with it.” She does not sleep with either truth resolved.

I don’t think this chapter makes me suspicious of Pace’s CW life in the simple “he’s sleeping with all these women” way. The text actively argues against that through the bartender. But it does make me more alert to how Pace’s goodness is itself dangerous to Vee, because it makes yielding feel intelligent and safe. He really may be good; he is also still hiding Randi.

The title, “Lesson,” is very clean and maybe a little cruel. It is the literal dance lesson, yes. But Vee also gets a lesson in Pace’s public world, in jealousy, in how quickly shame turns outward, and in what it costs to mistake being guided for being used. The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, keeps feeling closer to food, hunger, feeding, and caution around dangerous intimacy. The tagline is ringing loudly here: every yes in the dance is freely given, and the lesson is that saying yes to being led can feel like finding yourself — which is exactly why the trap can work.

What I want next is the apology. I want Vee to go to Randi’s because that choice itself is fascinating: she is not going first to Pace or Cassie, but to Randi, “the person Vee gets to tell.” I dread what Randi will do with it. My guess from here is that Randi will comfort Vee, translate the shame, maybe even help script the apology — but because Randi is also Pace’s lover and part of the original game, that help is going to be double-edged.
