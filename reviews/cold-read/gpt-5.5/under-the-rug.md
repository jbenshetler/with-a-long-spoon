## Reader reaction

This chapter hurts in a more active way than the porch did. The porch was a missed answer inside tenderness; here the missed answer has become a whole weather system in Vee. I feel her lying there warm and loved and still frightened, and the opening lands immediately: “she'd say it and he wouldn't, and she'd be left lying here having said it into a room that didn't say it back.” That is exactly the wound from before, now awake in the dark.

Vee is painfully sympathetic to me here, even when she behaves badly. I don’t *like* the unfair accusation she makes — “You’re doing it again. Punishing me” — especially because the text tells me she knows it isn’t true: “Some back room of her knew which of them had gone cold that night… and she said it anyway.” But I believe why she does it. She is trying to force a shape onto something that keeps evading names. She wants public evidence because private evidence is no longer enough. She wants Chili’s, of all places, not because it is right for them but because it is public, ordinary, legible: a boyfriend-girlfriend place, a date place, a place that can prove she is not only a body in his house.

Pace moves me here, and also worries me. His response to her sharp question is almost maddeningly good: “Okay. Where do you want to go tonight?” No defensiveness, no punishment, no wounded male pride. He takes the request seriously and acts. Then at Chili’s, what first looks to Vee like proof that he doesn’t want to be seen with her becomes something else: the room is too much for him. The televisions, the music, the visual clutter, the whole “all at once” of it. His explanation — “In a room like that I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you. Somewhere quieter, I can. And I do. … I'm listening now” — made me soften toward him hard. It felt true to the Pace I know: intensely attentive, easily at home in controlled, quiet spaces, not socially flashy, maybe not built for bright noisy restaurants.

But the chapter refuses to let that be enough. And I’m with Vee there too. “It wasn’t the word. It would never be the word.” That sentence is brutal because it lets his explanation be real and still not be the answer. I do not feel like Pace is fake. I do not feel like he is merely using her. But I do feel more and more that he can give her everything except the thing that would make it socially and emotionally nameable. His care is enormous; his silence is enormous too.

The erotic charge works strongly for me, especially because it is tangled with Vee’s frustration. The peak is not only the sex itself but the shift when his patience finally breaks: “He came up over her then, and the patience went out of him.” That answers something she wanted from him — she wanted his control to give, wanted “for the want in him to get the better of him” — and when it happens, it is hot because it feels like she has reached something under his composure. The breast-feeding language before that is also charged: “her back lifting off the bed to feed him more of it.” It folds back into the food/appetite motif, but erotically it is intimate and needy in both directions. Where it hurts is after: the sex gives her proof in the body, then the body-proof evaporates under the demand for a name. “He'd given her all of it and not the name.” That line is the chapter’s ache.

I don’t really suspect Pace of malice here. The text earns something more complicated than suspicion. I suspect a mismatch. I suspect a limit. I suspect that he may be able to listen, tend, cook, touch, notice, explain, and desire with astonishing precision, while still not offering Vee the declared mutual belonging she needs. That may be fear, incapacity, restraint, or something he knows and she doesn’t. From only this page, I don’t know. But the book’s cover keeps vibrating under this: “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” Vee’s yes here is absolutely freely given — she goes to him, lets herself be tended, wants him, comes, feels herself his — and yet the trap feels like this exact thing: her body keeps answering in ways that temporarily drown the question her mind needs answered.

The friction for me is mostly productive, not bad friction. I did flinch a little at how perfectly Chili’s becomes the anti-Pace meal — “It was to food what a billboard is to a painting” — because it is very on-the-nose as contrast. But honestly, Vee’s perception is so sharpened by misery that I bought the excess. She is not neutrally reviewing nachos; she is realizing he has “ruined” ordinary satisfactions for her. That matters. The bigger readerly discomfort is that Vee’s conclusion in the restaurant is so fast and self-wounding — “The just-sex of it, proven in fluorescent light” — but again, that feels like her fear talking, not the author asking me to believe it.

The chapter title, “Under the Rug,” means several things to me after reading. First, Vee keeps trying to sweep the real question under the rug: love, publicness, naming, whether he belongs to her. Then the restaurant fight gets swept into an easier story — “she had forgiven him” — which feels clean for a few minutes but does not hold. And at the end, nothing is actually resolved; the not-knowing has just been covered over by sex, warmth, and his arm across her ribs. It also makes me think of domestic interiors: Pace’s house is so warm and ordered, but something is accumulating under its surfaces.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, keeps feeling more ominously apt. Food and feeding are everywhere, and Vee’s earlier “crackers” and “meal” sentence comes back here through the ruined nachos. Pace has fed her so well that Chili’s becomes unbearable; he has awakened appetite so thoroughly that she can’t go back to what used to satisfy her. The “long spoon” feels like nourishment with danger in it — you eat, but from a distance, or with caution, or with someone you may not safely get close to. The tagline now feels less abstract than ever. Vee’s yeses are real. That is why they may trap her.

What I want next: I want Vee to say the word plainly, or to ask him plainly why he won’t. I dread that she will instead bring the ache to Randi, who may soothe it beautifully and steer it somewhere else. My guess from here is that the next pressure point will be the difference between private possession and public naming: Vee can feel owned in Pace’s bed, but she cannot say what she has in daylight.
