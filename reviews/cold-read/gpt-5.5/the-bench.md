## Reader reaction

This is an intense opening. I’m pulled in immediately by how intimate the domestic quiet is before anything sexual happens: the unlocked door, her locking it behind her, neither of them calling out, the “small economy of two people who did not perform for each other.” That line makes the house feel private and grown-up and almost tender before the chapter starts troubling that tenderness.

He notices everything. That is seductive. But by the end that same noticing becomes frightening, because he is wrong about what he has done. It tells me the consent frame is not simple, even though the safeword is present and repeated.

The major friction is that Pace’s point of view sometimes explains Randi with enormous certainty after only three weeks. Some of that feels like character, not authorial overreach, especially because the bathroom section later proves he has missed something crucial. But lines like “The script was failing. He could see that it was failing before she could” made me tense in both ways: tense because the scene is working, and tense because I don’t fully trust his authority over her.

The chapter title, “The Bench,” is blunt and effective. At first it sounds plain, almost furniture-like, then the chapter makes the object into the center of the whole emotional and erotic event. It did not give too much away to me; it created a little curiosity, then became exact. The book title, *A Polite Invitation*, now feels chillingly apt: everything here is invitation, permission, patience, “tell me your word,” “tell me anytime.” And yet the tagline is already alive: every yes is freely given, and I can see how that can still become a trap when someone consents to the act but not to the self-knowledge it produces. *With a Long Spoon* does not mean much to me yet, except as a warning about intimacy with something dangerous.

I want to keep reading because the ending has a real dramatic hook: Pace thinks the evening was beautiful and successful; Randi is already hiding from him. My guess from here is that the central danger is not crude violation but misread tenderness — people doing things they think are loving while the other person silently reorganizes around damage, desire, or both.
