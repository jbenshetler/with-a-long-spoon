## Reader reaction

This chapter hit me physically before it hit me morally. I felt the climb in my thighs, the wrong boot at the heel, the sudden cold of rain inside a shirt, the strange heat of being seen. It is probably the most embodied Vee has been with Pace so far, and the erotic charge works on me because it comes out of practical care, discomfort, weather, hunger, shame, and choice rather than a posed seduction.

Vee is so vulnerable here, but not helpless. I loved the opening because it catches the gap between the yes she gave and the thing she has actually arrived at: “There had been no wall of trees in the yes. There had been him.” That line is beautiful and worrying at once. It makes her yes feel real — she really did want the date, wanted him — but also incomplete. She said yes to Pace, not to the full physical reality of the trail, wrong boots, rain, isolation, and truck. That feels like the book’s central trap tightening without anyone having to force her.

Pace, on the page here, is almost painfully attractive. He is careful in exactly the way that undoes her. He sees her fear at the trailhead and “didn't make anything of it.” He sets “the speed of her not knowing how.” When the boot rubs, he fixes the blister competently and without humiliation. The moment with the cherry pit could have made me wince harder than it did, because Vee’s shame flares so fast — “the *no one taught you* word, the *common* word” — but Pace handles it beautifully: “We take nothing and leave nothing,” then he lays his own pit beside hers and doesn’t look at her. That is such a precise form of kindness. He teaches without making her small.

And yet, because I carry the jacket and the earlier chapters, I do not trust the innocence of his perfection. The text of this chapter alone earns attraction and trust; the accumulated book earns unease. He is not doing anything overtly wrong here. If I met only this chapter, I might simply think: this is an attentive man who knows the woods and knows how to care for a woman who is out of her element. But I know Vee is out of her element partly because he and Randi picked her and have not told her. So every good thing he does has a second shadow. The same skill that reassures me also scares me.

Randi is absent, and I really felt her absence. This is Pace and Vee’s most private chapter yet, and that privacy makes the hidden arrangement louder. Randi is nowhere on the trail, nowhere in the truck, nowhere in Vee’s mind in any direct way — but I remember that Randi wanted reports, helped eroticize “May I choose,” and publicly claimed Vee. I kept wondering what Randi would feel if she saw this truck scene. Jealous? Aroused? Proud? Hurt? All of it?

Cassie is absent too, but her practical protection echoes through the boots. Meg’s boots are wrong, Cassie’s “borrow good ones” was right, and Vee is now literally paying in skin for entering Pace’s world without proper footing. Meg becomes physically present through the borrowed boots, even though she is not in the chapter.

The erotic charge peaks in the truck, obviously, but what makes it work for me is how long the chapter spends earning the conditions: the body tired, hungry, wet, cold, already cared for by his hands at her foot, already fed by his hands from his pack, already instructed gently and not shamed. By the time he gives her the flannel and gets out into the rain so she can change, the eroticism is wound very tight because the gesture is both protective and charged. He gives her privacy. She chooses not to use it as simple privacy.

The line that really flips the heat on is: “*I want him in here with me.* / And under it, slower — *I want him to see me.*” That feels completely convincing from Vee. Not abstractly “she wants to be desired,” but specifically: she wants this man, in this truck, after this day, to see the body she has been taught to shame. The maternal voice becomes fuel: “*Girls who do this. Girls in trucks. What kind of. Three dates.*” The shame does not stop the desire; it eroticizes the exact forbiddenness. That is uncomfortable, but it is alive.

The undressing itself is hot. It is hot because she makes him keep his eyes on hers first. “She held them there. / He didn't look away.” Then the slow buttons, wet cotton, cold fingers, bra gone sheer, her choosing to look down so his eyes go with hers — it is explicit without feeling mechanical. The line “She saw what he saw” is the hinge. It makes the exposure shared rather than merely observed. And Pace’s stillness matters: “He didn't lean in. He didn't lift a hand.” His restraint lets the scene remain hers.

Where I felt friction — not disbelief exactly, but a spike of discomfort — was with how perfectly Pace’s restraint keeps sanctifying the escalation. It is narratively and erotically effective, but I’m starting to feel the danger of his near-perfect responses. He always seems to know when not to press, when not to shame, when to give privacy, when to let her call him back. That can be tenderness. It can also be an incredibly powerful form of control, because she experiences every next step as her own discovery. The jacket keeps whispering: “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.”

Another friction point is the campus cop interruption. I don’t mean I didn’t buy it — the fire lane, the red curb, the rain, the quick stop all make sense — but emotionally it is abrupt in a way that almost hurts. Vee is about to say something: “The thing she wanted to say was bigger than any words she had for it,” and then the siren “took the rest of her sentence with it.” That worked on me, but it also frustrated me in the intended way. The chapter denies the conversation after the erotic act. They do not get to name it. The thing stays “in the cab with them, holding still in the warm air,” then she is left in his flannel, wet and unfinished.

The chapter title, “Leave No Trace,” first means the trail ethic Pace teaches through the cherry pit: take nothing, leave nothing. But by the end it feels much larger and more ironic. They try not to leave the pit, the wrappers, the evidence of being there. But this day leaves traces everywhere: moleskin on her heel, his flannel on her body, wet shirt in her hand, the smell of him and gym bag and her own wet hair, the unsaid sentence, the cop lights, the memory of his eyes. “Leave No Trace” does not give the chapter away, but it recolors it hard afterward. The rule is impossible emotionally. Everything leaves a trace.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, still feels like distance and appetite to me — eating, tasting, taking in desire, but maybe with some old caution about dining with danger. This chapter adds sandwiches, shared water, cherries, pits gathered on wax paper, and hunger made clean by exertion. The cover tagline feels sharper than ever here. Vee’s choices are plainly hers: stepping onto the trail, letting him fix her boot, eating what he packed, calling him back into the truck, unbuttoning her shirt, showing herself, bringing his freezing hand under the flannel. No one forces her. That is exactly what worries me.

What I want next: I badly want to know what happens to Pace with the campus cop, though I assume it is probably just a fire-lane stop. I want Cassie to see Vee come in wearing Pace’s flannel. I want Randi to hear about this and I dread her hearing about this. I expect Vee will report it, maybe to Cassie first, maybe to Randi with a more charged version. My guess from here is that the truck scene becomes a major inward threshold for Vee: not sex yet, but the first time she deliberately lets Pace see her body and likes the shame of it turning into heat.

My feeling for Randi is deeply conflicted: I believe she cares for Vee, maybe desires her and wants to protect her socially, but she is also lying to Vee’s face.

Vee has my strongest protectiveness. She is intelligent, observant, funny, hungry for attention, and shaped by shame around appetite, clothing, sexuality, display, and being seen. “The Long Way” made her loneliness plain: ordinary kindness felt astonishing, and her overfull froyo cup without her mother’s voice felt like a real little liberation. “Water Wings” deepened her bodily history: she once had synchronized-swimming competence and loved water before her body became a problem. “May I Choose” deepened her appetite and surrender: she let Pace order, ate without watching herself, let him carry the evening, and then chose to bend a rule because he did not push her. “Off Six-Fourteen” made her joy vivid and convincing: she needed Cassie to know the kiss was hers, chosen on purpose, and she was almost dancing with it. “Dear” shows how easily social shame can make her small — pilled cardigan, hanging feet, pink drink, “We have stats together” — and how powerfully Randi’s public claiming repairs it. “Leave No Trace” brings her desire and shame together in the body: wrong boots, rubbed heel, hunger, rain, wet bra, mother’s voice, heat, and the wish to be seen. I am moved by her yeses and frightened by how completely the situation has been prepared around them.

Cassie is the person I most trust near Vee by a wide margin. She is dry, loyal, observant, not easily dazzled, and protective without making herself the hero. Her healer joke gave Vee history; “Water Wings” gave their friendship real emotional authority; “May I Choose” showed her tact when she noticed Vee’s face and did not mock the vulnerable part. “Off Six-Fourteen” strengthened my trust: she was glad for Vee but still asked about alcohol, the man’s name, where he lives, and shoes. “Dear” makes me a little sad for her: she sees Randi reaching for Vee after class, folds away whatever she feels or notices, and leaves Vee to it. “Leave No Trace” proves Cassie’s practical concern about good shoes was right. She currently reads Pace as “not a boy” but a man, and I believe why she says it from what she knows, though I worry because she does not know the hidden setup.

Christine is a small but warm presence. Her ease with Pace reassures Vee and reassures me a little, while also making Pace more complex: he has ordinary places where people know him fondly.

Meg remains mostly offstage, but her boots physically affect this chapter and underline that Vee is improvising her way into Pace’s world with borrowed equipment.

Brooke remains formidable social power; warm but not safe. In “Dear,” her smile stays warm while taking Vee’s measure, and her “We should all get dinner sometime” is socially lovely but empty. Sloane is observant/classifying; Kinsey is eager and socially pressuring. The unnamed taller woman with Brooke in “Dear” feels like another polished social judge, silent and assessing. Randi’s mother and Vee’s mother both loom as sources of inherited shame and restriction, especially around food, bodies, need, sexuality, and respectability. Vee’s mother is especially loud now: her shame words are not just inhibitions but part of the erotic heat Vee feels with Pace.

Overall mood: the trap has become tender, romantic, social, physical, and now erotically private. Vee’s yeses are getting more beautiful and more embodied — yes to froyo, yes to water, yes to food, yes to Pace choosing, yes to making an exception, yes to hiking, yes to Randi’s “dear friend,” yes to calling Pace back into the truck and letting him see. Every yes still feels freely given, and that is exactly why I am worried.
