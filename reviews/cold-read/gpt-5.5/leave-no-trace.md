## Reader reaction

This chapter made me cold first, then hungry, then very hot, then anxious in a way that did not let the heat finish neatly. It’s one of the strongest Pace/Vee chapters for me because the sexiest thing in it is not just the undressing in the truck, though that absolutely works; it’s the repeated way he handles her lack of knowledge without ever making her feel ignorant. The boots, the moleskin, the cherry pit, the pace up the trail — each could have become humiliation, and each time he absorbs the awkwardness and hands her dignity back.

Pace, right now, is almost unbearably attractive to me. That is also exactly what makes me keep mistrusting him. On the page of this chapter, he is careful: he sees her fear at the start and only says, “It eases in”; he sets “the speed of her not knowing how”; he fixes the hot spot in her boot with practical tenderness; he shares food; he teaches “leave no trace” gently; he gives her privacy to change in the truck without even being asked. The line where he gets out into the rain and stands with his back to the truck is devastatingly good: he gives her both “His shirt, and then the truck. Both.” I believed completely that she would feel that as care, permission, invitation, and provocation all at once.

But because I know from the jacket and from earlier chapters that he and Randi chose Vee as part of a hidden game, all of his gentleness has a second edge. I do not think this chapter itself makes him seem false. It doesn’t. If anything, it makes him feel more genuinely responsive to Vee. The suspicion comes from the accumulated situation: he is creating conditions in which her yeses become easier, freer, more deeply hers — and he knows so much more than she does about the larger shape of what’s happening.

Vee has my whole heart here. Her fear at the trailhead is so bodily and immediate: “There had been no wall of trees in the yes. There had been him.” That feels exactly like her current danger and her current joy. She said yes to Pace, not to all the implications. But then she does climb. She learns. She gets to the top. The summit expands her beyond social looking for a minute: “it did not care that she was standing on the edge of it… and that not-caring was somehow the most beautiful part.” I loved that. After so many rooms where she is measured — Chi Latte, the pool, The Smoothie Bird — the valley not caring about her is almost a mercy.

Her erotic turn in the truck feels earned to me because it grows straight out of the same knot: shame, being seen, wanting to be seen, wanting to be the thing her mother’s voice condemns. The word “floozy” coming back at the exact moment she looks down and sees herself as Pace sees her is potent and awful and arousing. The erotic peak for me is not even the full opening of the shirt but the moment before it: she knocks on the window, crooks her finger, and he comes in but does not yet know what she has called him back to do. That little delay is electric. Then she keeps his eyes while unbuttoning, and he understands. “His face moved. Small. Nothing anyone outside the cab could have read. A little landing.” That is beautifully charged.

It also stays tender because he does not pounce. He looks because she has invited him to look. He does not touch until she reaches for him. And then the chapter turns, sharply, when she realizes his hand is shaking. That moved my feeling about Vee slightly: she is not just being cared for here; she cares back. Her erotic momentum stops because she registers his cold. “Pace. You’re freezing.” That saves the scene from becoming only exhibition and desire; it becomes mutual attention.

Randi is absent, and I felt her absence. After “Dear,” where Vee poured the Pace story into Randi and Randi publicly claimed her, it matters that this chapter is all Pace and Vee and the woods. Randi is not here to interpret, encourage, or steer. But the hidden triangle is still in my head, especially because this chapter advances Pace and Vee into a much more intimate physical space. I wonder what Vee will tell Randi, and what Randi will feel hearing about the flannel and the truck and the almost-next-thing.

Cassie is absent too, and I missed her practical eye. This is exactly the sort of date Cassie was half-jokingly worried about: “off 614,” gravel, mountain, man from coffee shop. On the page, nothing bad happens in the obvious danger sense. Pace is prepared and careful. But Cassie’s caution still echoes, especially when Vee is wet, cold, in his truck, in his shirt, and then the campus cop lights appear like the outside world suddenly interrupting the private spell.

The title, “Leave No Trace,” lands first as the hiking ethic: cherry pits, wax paper, taking nothing and leaving nothing. Pace teaches it kindly. But by the end it also feels loaded emotionally. They leave the mountain with no litter, but they absolutely do not leave no trace on each other. His flannel is on her. The moleskin is still on her heel. The almost-sexual moment is in the cab with them. The cop prevents whatever words she was about to say, so there’s no public trace, no completed conversation, no kiss at the dorm — but the chapter is full of marks. The title recolors the whole thing: what traces count? What can be cleaned up, packed out, hidden, or denied?

The book title, *A Polite Invitation*, feels painfully apt here. Pace’s politeness is not chilly; it is erotic architecture. He invites without forcing: the trail, the food, the privacy, the look. And Vee keeps stepping through. The series title *With a Long Spoon* still feels like eating, danger, distance, and appetite all at once; this chapter gives me sandwiches, cherries, shared water, horseradish, hunger made rightful. The tagline — “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” — is almost screaming over this chapter. Vee chooses the trail. She chooses the cherries after being corrected. She chooses to call him back into the cab. She chooses to show him. Nothing is forced. That is exactly why I’m worried.

Friction-wise, I bought almost all of it. The only slight jolt for me was how cleanly the campus cop interrupts at the exact moment she tries to speak: “Pace— / Blue and white came on behind them…” It’s dramatically effective, but I felt the author’s hand there more than elsewhere, because it so precisely prevents articulation. Still, emotionally, I accepted it: the world intrudes, the private charged cab gets broken open, and Vee is left wearing his shirt with the sentence unsaid.

What I want next: I badly want to know what happens to Pace with the campus cop. Is it just the fire lane? Does it matter? I want Vee to walk into the dorm in his flannel and run into Cassie, because Cassie will see everything. I expect Vee will report the hike, but I dread how much of the truck scene she will make small or funny to survive telling it. I also expect Randi will hear some version, and I dread her combination of arousal, jealousy, pride, and concealment. My guess from here: the flannel becomes evidence — not legal evidence, but social/emotional evidence — something Vee carries, returns, smells, maybe hides.
