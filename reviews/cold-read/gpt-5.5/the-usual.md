## Reader reaction

This chapter is a relief and a bruise at once. I felt my shoulders drop because we finally get Pace alive in a room again, not just as Vee’s silent phone-screen ghost. And almost immediately I’m tender toward him, because the chapter opens with him deciding “he would not bring her down today” — meaning Sheri, I think — and there’s that recognizable Pace habit of swallowing his own trouble because someone else has enough. But it also makes me angry with him, or at least impatient: he has “long practice at deciding a thing could keep,” and that sounds noble until it’s Vee freezing in Ohio with no answer.

Sheri lands wonderfully for me here. She is not softened into prettiness: “too much” makeup, roots grown out, “five feet of her and every inch in motion,” eating a double burger like it belongs to her. I like her at once because she gives Pace the thing almost no one else gives him: not desire, not awe, not need, just plain friendship and bluntness. “Don’t bullshit me, Pace” felt like a hand on the back of my own neck. She says exactly what I’ve been wanting someone to say: “Then not saying it matters more to you than having her.” That line hits hard because the book has let me feel both sides now. From Vee’s room, his silence is abandonment. From this booth, his silence is something he knows is hurting her and still cannot cross.

Pace moves for me in two directions. I trust his feeling for Vee more after this chapter. He isn’t blasé. He isn’t bored. He says “I want Vee. I miss her,” and when he talks about her, it isn’t only her body, though the body is very much there. “The air’s different when she’s in the room. It — crackles.” That works on me. So does “she gets what it’s for. The whole idea under it. The philosophy of it,” even though Sheri is right to mock him for the phrase. It’s very Pace: over-serious, reverent about food, slightly ridiculous, deeply sincere.

But I trust his choices less. He keeps giving a ledger of care — “Every weekend, I’m there,” “the weeknights were hers,” “I told her she was the most important thing in my life” — and it sounds true, but it also sounds like he is trying to make math equal love. Sheri catches that. Vee did not ask for the proof; she asked for the name. The chapter doesn’t tell me why he “can’t,” and because I still don’t know why, the refusal feels ominous rather than merely stubborn.

Randi is absent, but the mention of “the brunette” makes me flinch. Sheri clocked something real in Randi immediately: “She looked at me like I was one more of your harem applicants. Never did learn my name.” That is funny, but it is also ugly in a way I believe. Randi’s warmth with Vee is still powerful in my memory, but this reminds me of the possessiveness and performance around Pace. Sheri’s outside-eye on her is clarifying.

The erotic charge here is mostly conversational and remembered, not acted. It peaks in the teasing about Vee’s body because it is crude enough to feel alive, but not cruel: “Hell, that backside,” “the one with the big tits,” “Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you.” Pace’s embarrassment makes it hotter than if he had boasted. The more genuinely erotic moment, though, is his attempt to explain Vee’s presence: “The air’s different when she’s in the room. It — crackles.” That feels like desire going beyond inventory. The heat is not explicit, but it is definitely warm under the table.

I had almost no boredom here. The diner is vivid: griddle, fogged window, amber tabletop, Elvis under glass, jukebox silence broken by “home for Christmas.” The one place I felt a little pressure from the prose was perhaps “every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something,” but even that feels close enough to Pace’s mind that I bought it. The chapter’s best plain sentence may be Sheri’s: “Most men’d just lie — tell her whatever gets them through the night. Easier that way.” It shows why Pace’s honesty is both valuable and insufficient.

The title, “The Usual,” works several ways for me. On the surface it’s the diner order, the waitress already writing because “there was no question and never had been.” But it also points to Pace and Sheri’s ritual: this booth, this food, this friendship where she steals his fries and tells him the truth. More darkly, it points to Pace’s usual pattern — keeping what hurts to himself, caring by not burdening, refusing to say the one thing that would cost him something. It did not give anything away for me; it settled in afterward.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, and the tagline still feel like a warning about proximity without safety. This chapter sharpens that because no one is forcing Pace not to speak, and no one forced Vee to ask. “Every yes was freely given” keeps getting more painful: Vee gave weekends, trust, her body, her want; Pace gave tenderness and presence; Randi gives warmth while hiding the frame. The trap is looking more and more like a set of partial truths everyone can consent to because the missing piece is never placed on the table.

What I want next is contact. I want Pace to call Vee, or Vee to return and make him face the gap. I dread him continuing to wait because “the thing could keep.” My guess from here is that he will not solve this cleanly before term resumes; the book seems to be tightening the separation so that when Vee, Pace, and Randi are all back near each other, every withheld thing is loaded.

But Sheri says Vee did not ask him for reasons. She asks what he wants. Pace says, “I want Vee. I miss her.” Sheri tells him to say it. Pace says, “I can’t.” Sheri replies that not saying it matters more to him than having her, and that from her seat it feels pretty shitty. Pace does not argue.

Sheri then lightens the mood by stealing fries and talking about Randi, “that brunette”/“the sorority one” he brought dancing before Thanksgiving. She says Randi was easy on the eye but probably “a lot,” and that when Sheri hugged Pace hello, Randi looked at her like one more harem applicant and never learned her name. Pace does not answer, and Sheri drops it.

Sheri asks why Pace is so gone on Vee, since in two years she has never seen him act like this over a girl and he has never been a hit-and-quit man. Pace says, “Her name is Vee,” while Sheri keeps joking about Vee’s body. Pace says the air is different when Vee is in the room, that it crackles. Sheri translates this as good sex, and Pace admits yes, but says that is not what he meant. He says Vee sees things nobody else does: when he cooks, she does not just say it tastes good, she gets what it is for, “the whole idea under it. The philosophy of it.” Sheri mocks him — “The philosophy of your food. Lord almighty. Get over yourself.” Pace laughs fully for the first time that night. The chapter ends with the impossible thing sitting farther off for a while, the diner warm, and fogged glass keeping the cold outside.

**Running memory — how I feel now:**  
Vee feels more vulnerable than ever: not naïve, but displaced, under-informed, and emotionally exposed. Her longing for Pace is bodily and domestic; her reliance on Randi is social and emotional; neither is safe because both are entangled with withheld truth.

Pace’s silence still worries me, but it no longer feels empty. He misses Vee and wants her; he is not indifferent, and his description of her makes his feeling convincing. That makes his refusal more painful, not less. I trust his tenderness, but I mistrust his avoidance. The longer he decides things “can keep,” the more his care turns into harm.

Randi remains lifesaving and dangerous. To Vee she is the sure returning thing, but I know from the jacket and prior chapters that she is not transparent. Sheri’s outside memory of Randi as possessive and dismissive of another woman sharpens my unease.

Sheri feels like a gift: funny, wounded, earthy, blunt, and one of the only people who can tell Pace the truth without wanting anything from him. I do not currently see her as a real romantic threat; Vee’s jealousy of her now seems more like Vee’s insecurity than Sheri’s intent.

The overall mood moving forward: cold New Year outside, warm diner inside, unresolved declaration at the center. Vee and Pace both ache, but he still cannot name what she needs named. Randi waits as the bright sure thing with shadows around her. The trap feels increasingly made of omissions, partial honesty, and freely chosen hunger.
