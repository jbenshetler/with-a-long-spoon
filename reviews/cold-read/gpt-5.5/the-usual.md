## Reader reaction

This chapter makes me warmer toward Pace and more frustrated with him at the same time. Seeing him from inside, with Sheri, clears away Vee’s jealous fantasy of him casually replacing her. He is not indifferent. He misses her; he wants her; he is hurting. But the chapter also makes the refusal sharper, because Sheri asks the simple thing I’ve been waiting for someone to ask: if she told you what she needs, why not say it? His “I can’t” lands harder than all his careful acts. It is not that he does not understand the request. He understands enough to avoid it.

Sheri is a relief. I like her immediately: blunt, hungry, loyal, funny, not impressed by Pace’s specialness, and able to cut him exactly where he needs cutting. The diner ease between them is genuinely comforting, and it also makes Vee’s fear more understandable in retrospect: Pace does have other intimate, knowing female spaces. But this one does not feel erotic or threatening on the page. Sheri’s open lesbian/bi implication with Paige, her “curvy one” comments, and her complete lack of awe make her feel like friend-family, not rival.

Erotically, the heat is mostly remembered and verbal: Sheri talking about Vee’s body, Pace blushing, his attempt to say the air “crackles.” It works because it is not a sex scene; the charge is in how unable Pace is to make Vee merely sexual even while admitting the sex is good. The peak for me is not the body joke but “She sees things nobody else does.” That is the closest he comes to a love-sentence, and the fact he can say it to Sheri but not to Vee hurts.

Trust vs suspicion: I trust Pace’s feeling for Vee more after this. I trust his practical devotion, and now I trust that her absence wounds him. I still do not trust his silence, because “I can’t” is too naked and unexplained. It feels like an interior lock, not mere awkwardness. I also notice he decides at the very start “he would not bring her down today” — likely not Vee but something painful Sheri carries — and that tells me he is withholding pain as care again. Sometimes that is kindness; sometimes it is the whole problem.

The chapter title, “The Usual,” starts as diner shorthand — their regular order, their familiar booth, the usual friendship rhythm. By the end it also means Pace’s usual: acts, presence, care, reasons, everything except the one word. The book title and tagline keep darkening: every yes can be freely given inside warmth and attention, but if the naming/context is withheld, the person saying yes may still not know what table she is sitting at.

What I want next: I want Pace to go to Vee, not with food or a warmed house but with whatever truth sits behind “I can’t.” Guess from here: he may try to win her back through the usual acts, and that will not be enough. I dread Randi becoming Vee’s comfort while Pace is stalled, because Randi is the one who knows the most and says the least.

Her room preserves old Vivienne in high resolution — ribbons, synchronized-swimming trophies, clarinet, old friends’ faces — but does not know the woman now in the bed. Christmas days have been beige: ornaments, wrapping, tidying, casseroles, potatoes, good meat, Wheel of Fortune, and daily questions about her “young man” that she cannot answer in any way that fits this house.

  Vee checks her phone: nothing from Pace. She scrolls old warm messages and starts to type “are you—” but deletes it, knowing no message she sends will come back in the shape she needs. Pace leaves room, which she once appreciated and now hates. She looks at photos: laughing with Pace on his couch; herself in keyhole shirt and boots before the dance went wrong; the induction photo where the burgundy dress, slit, leg, and Randi-buckled heel made her feel wholly visible. She remembers sending Pace the whole induction photo, while cropping it at the waist for her mother downstairs, dividing herself by house.

  Vee looks at Randi’s feed — ski run, golden chalet, fancy dinner, bare shoulders, white-gold bracelet, solitaire, black mountains — and wants that whole visible life even though she knows from the PPP project what kind of money bought it. She texts Randi warmly, carefully, with no demand and no hint that she knows about Miranda Holdings: “thinking about you 💛 hope it’s beautiful out there. i’m here if you want to talk. or not.” Randi does not answer immediately. Vee calculates the time difference and imagines Randi in a lit public spectacle with a storybook man. What Vee envies is not the sex but the life lived out loud, in front of everyone. Her own best nights with Pace happen behind his door, and the one public outing went badly.

  Near midnight Randi replies with the fairytale text: “you ANGEL 😇 it’s honestly a fairytale out here — dad’s got us in this insane chalet, mountains for days, i’m never coming home. miss your face most though, gorgeous 💋.” Vee receives it as proof Randi is fine, glamorous, and still taking time for her. She thinks Randi is the only girl she can talk to about real things, though those real things are mostly Pace. Then she spirals over Pace: the doorway confrontation, his warm wrong answer, the quiet since, no word across break. Maybe it is over; maybe he thinks it is; maybe he thinks nothing happened. With no name for herself, she has no name for him and no standing to ask whose he might be. Remembering Sheri at Thanksgiving and Pace cooking for someone alone, she imagines him cooking over Christmas for an invented opposite of herself: blonde, tan, model-bodied. She tries to retreat into her best memory, the first sex/fra diavolo night at his table in the slipping sheet, but the imagined blonde replaces her in it. The bartender’s and Randi’s earlier reassurances no longer hold when Vee no longer knows she is the one. Midnight passes with small poppers and a dog bark. Vee imagines Pace’s arms around her, then cannot stop imagining whose he has them around instead. The new year has Randi penciled into it — class, brunches, texts, a face that will light up and call her gorgeous — and Pace as a blank. She falls asleep still reaching, in the bed her legs outgrew, while the cold room keeps watch over the girl who is gone.

  In “The Usual,” Pace is seen after the rupture in a quiet county-road diner with Sheri. He has decided, before the door even shuts behind him, not to bring some unnamed pain down on her today. The diner is a converted double-wide with white siding, tin roof, griddle smell, dark jukebox, amber-edged glass tabletops with old images beneath, and enough quiet that Pace thinks he could hear Vee whisper there. He remembers the Chili’s date from his side: screens, music, and crowd arguing with him until Vee’s voice was buried. In this diner, he wonders whether Vee would love it or never see it at all, and badly wants to know which.

  Sheri arrives small, restless, country, over-made-up, and sharp. The waitress knows their usual order. A new kid gives Pace the double burger and shake by assumption; Sheri silently corrects the plates and claims the shake. They talk lightly about Berkeley, school, and a Brooks and Dunn cover band, then Elvis starts singing about Christmas on the jukebox and the bruise under the holiday surfaces. Sheri tells Pace about Paige, a design-school girl she liked, and how it ended because Paige kept pressing her to reconnect with her family. Pace says people who have never lost anything think grief is a choice. Sheri tells him not to waste the good stuff on her and asks about Vee.

  Pace admits Vee wants a declaration — wants him to say “it.” He builds his defense out of true acts: weekends, weeknights given back for school, nothing else in the room when he has her, telling her she is the most important thing in his life. Sheri cuts through it: Vee did not ask for reasons; most men would lie, but Pace is not them; if Vee told him what he has to do, he should say it. Pace says, “I can’t.” Sheri answers that then not saying it matters more than having her, and from her seat that feels pretty shitty. Pace does not argue.

  Sheri then shifts the talk back toward Randi/the brunette, whom she remembers from dancing before Thanksgiving as beautiful but looking at Sheri like one of Pace’s harem applicants and never learning her name. Sheri says Pace is gone on Vee in a way she has not seen before and asks what Vee has. Pace says the air is different when Vee is in the room; it crackles. Sheri translates that as good sex; Pace says yes, but not only that. He says Vee sees things nobody else does: when he cooks, she understands not just the taste but what it is for, the idea under it, the philosophy. Sheri mocks “the philosophy of your food,” tells him to get over himself, and makes him laugh fully for the first time that night. For a while, in the warm diner, the thing he cannot do sits farther off.

- **How I feel**
  - Pace/Peter remains intensely attractive: careful, strong, patient, domestic, exact, sexually overwhelming, eloquent through acts, and believable in his sensory difficulty at Chili’s. “The Usual” increases my trust that he truly misses and wants Vee and is not casually replacing her with another woman. It also increases my unease because he knows exactly what she asked for and says plainly, “I can’t.” His inability is now a central locked door, not a misunderstanding. Sheri’s judgment makes his refusal feel morally serious: not saying may matter more to him than having Vee.
  - Vee feels brave, hungry, alive, endangered, and painfully alone. She named the exact problem with Pace and refused warmth/food/touch as substitutes for words; in “Old Acquaintances” she paid for that refusal in a cold childhood room. “The Usual” proves her fear that Pace does not care was wrong, but not in a way she can know yet, and not in a way that gives her what she needs.
  - Randi is more sympathetic and more alarming. I feel the cage around her: father/mother/table/approved men/beautiful face. Her longing for Vee feels genuine, not just tactic. But she knowingly chooses not to tell Vee and to let Vee “arrive” without being asked directly. In Vee’s New Year, Randi is becoming the reliable bright thing — the face, class, brunches, texts — which feels tender and dangerous because Vee does not know the truth underneath that reliability. Sheri’s outside view of Randi as the brunette who treated her like a harem applicant adds a small sour note to Randi’s social arrogance/possessiveness.
  - Sheri is now one of the people I trust most in the book. She is blunt, funny, wounded, hungry, and able to tell Pace the truth without flattering him. Her friendship with Pace feels real and nonsexual; her history with Paige and family estrangement gives her weight beyond comic relief.
  - John is attractive, competent, safe, and hollow only because Randi cannot be reached by him. I do not blame him much yet; he is the fairytale prince who assumes, performs, pleases, and still never finds the real person.
  - Randi’s father repels me through Randi’s revulsion, PPP shadow, and Vee’s awareness that his money bought the dream she envies. He is hearty and adoring on the surface but part of the approved table where truth cannot sit.
  - Randi’s mother feels cold, exquisite, restrictive, and cutting by presence alone.
  - Vee’s parents remain loving but category-bound. I feel their care concretely — heat turned down, work schedules, fabric softener, casseroles, Wheel of Fortune, repeated questions — and also how impossible it is for Vee’s changed life to fit inside it.
  - Cassie remains deeply trustworthy, perceptive, blunt, and tactful, though absent. She is still the person Vee should be able to tell and cannot.
  - Paige is only briefly known, but she makes Sheri’s loneliness visible: not cruel, maybe loving in her way, but unable to understand that family estrangement is not fixed by holiday sentiment.
  - Overall mood carried forward: the new year begins with two women and one man caught around unsaid words. Vee has demanded a name from Pace and left when she did not get it; Pace misses her and can tell Sheri she changes the air, but still cannot say the word. Randi has recognized her desire for Vee and chosen not to name it. Vee, not knowing either hidden truth, is starting the year with Randi as the sure visible comfort and Pace as a blank private ache. The book feels colder and more dangerous because the care, pleasure, beauty, family love, friendship, and longing are all real — and all insufficient without the thing that must be said.
