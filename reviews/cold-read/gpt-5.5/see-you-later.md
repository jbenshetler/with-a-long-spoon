## Reader reaction

I felt this chapter almost entirely through Vee’s body: the too-bright “Hi,” the deliberate not-looking at the door, the way a casual invitation becomes a social lifeline. It is tender and intoxicating, but because I’m carrying the previous chapter, it also makes my stomach tighten. Vee is not just flattered; she is starving for exactly this kind of entry, this kind of girl, this kind of room. Randi arrives like weather: “looking at Vee with her whole face lit,” touching her forearm, making jokes, making Cassie welcome, making Vee feel chosen. It works on Vee completely, and honestly it worked on me too — I understood the seduction of that warmth.

Vee deepens a lot here. Until now she was Pace’s and Randi’s object of attention: red hair, laugh, abundance. Now I’m inside her, and she is funny, self-aware in flashes, socially fluent, yearning, insecure, observant. Her read of Chi Latte is painfully good: she notices hierarchy, clothes, who belongs, who performs. She is not naïve in the sense of stupid. She is perceptive. But she is vulnerable to being “let in,” maybe catastrophically so. The line that catches her whole weakness is that Randi’s attention feels like “an hour she'd been let borrow inside a brighter life.” That is beautiful and sad and dangerous.

Randi, from Vee’s side, is dazzling and almost frighteningly effective. My sympathy for her from earlier chapters does not go away — I know she is also trapped in performance, shame, appetite, Pace’s frame — but here I see how powerful she is when she turns the face outward. She is not just pretending warmth; the warmth feels real on the page. But it is also practiced. Her opening bit to Cassie — “Yes, like the adjective. No, it's not a coincidence” — is charming, but Vee notices it has “a shape to it,” a place where the other person is supposed to respond. That made me trust Vee’s eye and distrust Randi’s fluency a little. Randi knows how to make rooms move.

Cassie immediately became important to me. I like her. She is blunt, ungiving, maybe protective, maybe just not susceptible. Her “Cassie” lands flat in the space where Randi expects play, and she does not take the offered rhythm. I felt relieved by her resistance. Then her “Goodbye, Vee” chilled me. The chapter title, “See You Later,” turns around that little exchange: Cassie says “Goodbye,” wrong and ominous, while Randi says “See you later,” warm and binding. Vee chooses the warmer phrase, of course. I don’t know whether Cassie actually sees danger or is just socially odd, but the text absolutely earned my unease. That “Goodbye” is too plain and too final not to matter emotionally.

Erotically, this is not explicit, but it is charged. Heat level is lower than the last chapter, but the sensuality is everywhere: Randi’s citrus smell, her shoulder against Vee’s shoulder, the hand on the forearm, the hug, the high table by the window, Vee’s awareness of bare midriffs and low waistbands. The erotic peak for me is not the hug, though that is intimate; it is Vee looking around Chi Latte, judging the “floozies” in her mother’s voice while also thinking, with a private flash of bodily pride, “she had more than they had, and better than any of them.” That is hot and sad at once: desire, shame, comparison, self-knowledge, repression. The chapter keeps sex sublimated into belonging.

The place I felt the book press hardest — though I bought it — was the professor’s last line about “latent variables”: “The factors you can't measure directly, that you have to infer from the ones you can.” It is almost too perfectly named for what is happening. I did notice the authorial spotlight there. But because this is a stats lecture and because the chapter is literally about Vee trying to infer meaning from gestures, it landed for me more as a pleasing on-the-nose bell than a dealbreaker.

The book title keeps getting sharper. *A Polite Invitation* feels exactly like this: Randi does nothing overtly coercive. She invites. She makes it feel easy. “Come to Chi Latte with me. Both of you.” “I'm not asking, I've decided” is jokingly impolite, but socially it still functions as charm. The trap is that every yes is emotionally prepared before it is spoken. Vee’s yes to coffee is “fully formed” before she lets it out. The series title *With a Long Spoon* still feels ominous and oblique to me — distance, danger, dining with something perilous maybe — but the spoon-sound in Chi Latte, “spoons going against the insides of cups,” suddenly makes me hear it inside this world of sweetness being stirred up. The cover tagline is practically glowing over this chapter: Vee freely goes. Freely sits. Freely warms. And I am afraid for her.

What I want next: I want Cassie to keep existing near Vee, because she feels like the one person not dazzled. I want Vee’s perspective to stay in the book because it changed the whole emotional field. I expect Randi will report back to Pace, maybe with pride, maybe with a performance of “look what I got us.” I dread Vee becoming attached before she has any idea there is a game — and after this chapter, she already is.
