## Reader reaction

This chapter hurt in a very quiet, bodily way. I felt the warmth first: the cool porch, his quilt, her legs across his lap, the coffee already waiting, his hand on her shin. All of that is exactly the kind of Pace tenderness that has been working on me for chapters. It made me soften before anything went wrong.

And then Vee says the sentence, and I felt how brave it was. “I had been eating crackers my whole life. And you fed me a meal.” It is intimate, almost naked, because she has built it around the thing she cannot say. I believed every inch of her fear and craft there: she is trying to give him the truth without breaking the rule she has found inside herself.

His answer is warm, but it does not land in the same place. “I love how good we are together” is not cruel. It is not nothing. It is affectionate, admiring, maybe even awed. But it is also sideways. Her sentence is about transformation, gratitude, love disguised as appetite; his comes back as mutual fit, bodies, how good they are together. The ache is that I do not know whether he missed her, dodged her, or answered as far as he could. The chapter earns that uncertainty, because Vee cannot know either.

I still trust Pace’s care in the immediate physical sense: he receives her warmly, pulls her closer, kisses her hair, does not shame her. But my trust in his emotional clarity is less settled. The unease is page-earned. It is not because he behaves badly, exactly; it is because she gives him her best sentence and he gives her something smaller, or at least differently shaped.

The erotic charge here is not explicit, but it is charged through aftermath and body memory. His hand under the quilt, her body “knowing what to do with being held this way,” the language of being fed a meal after crackers: it all carries sexual history without sex on the page. The heat peaks in the emotional exposure, not the touch.

The friction, for me, is mostly deliberate pain rather than a flaw. The line that lands hardest is: “She had said her best sentence. The chapter title, “Seconds,” now feels cruelly apt: second cups of coffee, second thoughts, a second version of love-saying that does not quite count, and maybe the leftover portion after the meal.
