## Reader reaction

This chapter made me love Cassie. Not in a grand dramatic way — more in the way you love someone who knows exactly when not to ask. The whole scene is low-stakes on the surface, just Vee and Cassie at the rec pool, but my body tightened almost immediately with Vee having to walk past the bleachers in that suit. “It fit, and it gave her nowhere to hide” is such a clean little ache. I felt the humiliation before anything happened, and then of course the guys deliver exactly the lazy, ugly commentary the chapter has prepared me to dread: “You won’t need floaties with *those*!” and “Legs for *days*.” It’s not surprising, but it still lands as exhausting.

Vee here deepens for me. She’s not just vulnerable to attention; she’s practiced at surviving being looked at. “She put her face on: chin level, shoulders open, the walk of a girl who had *meant* to arrive.” That connects hard to Randi’s face motif, but Vee’s face feels different — less polished social weapon, more armor against being reduced to her body. I felt protective of her, but also impressed by her. She knows how to make an entrance because the only alternative is to look like prey.

Cassie’s line — “Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman” — is perfect. It’s funny, dry, loyal, and, crucially, kept low “just for the two of them.” That moved my feeling about Cassie from trusted-observant to actively tender. She has this almost anti-sentimental care. She doesn’t perform protection by yelling at the guys; she gives Vee a private escape hatch. And later, when Vee comes up from underwater with “her face on wrong,” Cassie again doesn’t ask. The line “asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger” feels so exact to their friendship. Cassie knows when attention would make the wound worse.

The erotic charge here is not exactly sexual for me, though it is very bodily. The chapter is full of Vee’s body: the too-small-old suit, “there was more of her now,” the bleachers’ comments, the water taking the body out of social circulation. But the charge is more sensual than erotic — chlorine, wet tile, cold smoothness, light through water, the slow scull of her hands. The peak for me is underwater: “Nobody up there could see her. Nobody down here wanted anything.” That hit hard. It’s almost anti-erotic in the sense that it is a relief from being wanted, appraised, interpreted. After so many chapters where being chosen and looked at has been intoxicating and dangerous, this is Vee in a place where no one wants anything from her, and I found that deeply moving.

The title, “Water Wings,” is sweetly double after reading. On the obvious level it brushes against the crude “floaties” heckle, but it also points to Vee’s old ability in water — synchronized swimming, sculling, weightlessness, the kind of wings you get underwater when your hands remember how to hold you. It didn’t give anything away for me; it recolored the chapter afterward. “Water wings” are childish flotation devices, but Vee does not need them. The water itself is what lets her fly, or disappear, or return to a self before her body became “a problem.”

The book title, *A Polite Invitation*, and the tagline keep feeling more ominous because this chapter shows what Vee needs refuge from. She is not naive in a stupid way. She knows male attention can be degrading; she knows social looking; she knows how to armor herself. So when Pace and Randi attend to her in ways that feel respectful, warming, chosen, it makes terrifying sense that she would say yes. The trap is not that she does not know danger exists. It’s that their kind of attention feels like the opposite of the bleachers.

No one feels “off” inside this pool chapter except the guys in the bleachers, and they are plainly meant to be gross. Cassie feels absolutely solid. Vee feels exposed but real. Pace and Randi are absent, which is interesting: without them, Vee has a whole prior life, a body history, a friendship, and an old belonging in water. I want more of this Vee. I dread her being pulled back from “Nobody down here wanted anything” into the beautiful wanting machine Pace and Randi are building around her.

She is tall, lush, athletic, abundant, with full breasts, deep waist, wide hips, careless clothes, soft boots, a denim jacket, a crosswise canvas bag, warm dark red hair sparking copper in the wind, freckles, a snub nose, and a whole-body laugh with her head thrown back. She seems to laugh as if she has forgotten she can be seen. Pace says “Her,” watching Randi rather than the girl. Randi stops fully, stares helplessly, and says “Yes” with nothing around it. Then she recovers into “Uh — yeah. Yeah. I like her,” making it again into something a girlfriend is offering.

As Vee and her friend come up the walk, Pace tells Randi, “Go meet her.” Randi looks to him for permission; he smiles, nods, and lets her see that this is hers to take. She goes. Pace stays back by a light pole and watches without hearing. Randi engineers a small collision so cleanly that it looks like Vee walked into her. Vee is flustered and apologetic; Randi touches her arm lightly, stays a half-beat longer than necessary, and the three women laugh and exchange names. After about two minutes, Vee and her friend go on. Randi returns to Pace glowing and failing to hide her grin. She says, “Her name is Vee. And we’ve got a stats class together.” She does a small happy footwork movement on the brick. Pace lets her see his pride and thinks Randi’s yes now has a name, a class schedule, and copper hair. He says, “Let’s go feed you.”

Fourth chapter, “See You Later.” The stats lecture meets in a tiered room on the second floor of the math building, built for a hundred and seating sixty, with bolted paired seats. Vee arrives early as she always does and sits two-thirds of the way back with Cassie beside her. Cassie unpacks a granola bar and the wrong textbook, then the right one. The professor scrolls his slides forward and back like “a man checking that the road was still there.”

Vee opens her notebook and pencil and decides not to look at the door. She has decided this on the walk, on the stairs, and now in the room, but she is very much waiting for Randi. She remembers meeting Randi yesterday after the dining-hall collision with Meg from her floor. Vee had been mid-laugh, not thinking, when Randi collided with her or she collided with Randi. Vee looked up to apologize and forgot to because Randi seemed “better than the morning around her”: better lit, better put together, the kind of somebody a campus orbits. Randi smiled warmly, touched Vee’s arm, laughed, asked what she was studying, and discovered they had this exact class together. Randi said “oh my god, we should sit together” as if it were something she had hoped for. Vee walked back to her dorm not feeling her feet and carried the encounter like “an hour she'd been let borrow inside a brighter life.” Since then she has worried over whether Randi meant the invitation or merely said it.

Several people enter who are not Randi. Vee writes the date to give her hand somewhere to be. Cassie says Vee keeps looking at the door; Vee denies it. The professor’s throat-clear means class is about to begin and Vee thinks the question has been answered by default: Randi isn’t coming, or didn’t mean it. Then Randi says, “There you are.” She has entered from the back, which Vee had not thought to watch, and sidesteps into the row past annoyed people, looking at Vee with her whole face lit as though finding this exact row is what she wanted.

Vee says “Hi” too brightly. Randi says she almost didn’t find her and that the room is a cave. She sits on Vee’s other side, aisle side, putting Vee between herself and Cassie. Vee notices Randi’s cold-citrus scent, exact expensive clothes, skillfully done face, and the forearm squeeze repeated from the lawn. Randi compliments Vee’s right pen and jokes she will copy off her all semester. Vee laughs and feels warmed by the nothing-praise.

Randi then turns her warmth to Cassie and introduces herself with a practiced joke: “Yes, like the adjective. No, it’s not a coincidence. Yes, my parents had a sense of humor.” Vee notices the bit has a shape, an expected place where Cassie is supposed to laugh and hand her own name back. Cassie only says, “Cassie,” flatly, and picks up her pencil. Vee fills the social hitch by saying Cassie says about four words a day and you learn to read them like tea leaves. Randi receives even Cassie’s flatness as delightful and says she needs that because she says four hundred words a day and only nine land. Randi leaves openings for Cassie, who does not take them, but keeps returning to Vee. Vee is aware of being the one the warmth returns to, and of Randi’s bag touching her bag.

During lecture, the soft-spoken professor talks about residuals for fifty minutes and overuses a laser pointer. Vee means to take notes but is distracted by Randi at her elbow: recrossing her legs, writing, leaning in to whisper whether “this” will be on the thing, breath warm and shoulder touching Vee’s shoulder. Vee answers that it will be on the midterm and feels absurdly happy to have a small role as “the one who knows things.” The usual pleasure of the mathematical structure passes by like scenery because Randi’s presence is better.

At the end, the professor says next time they will get into “latent variables”: factors that cannot be measured directly, only inferred from measurable ones. Vee writes the phrase because her pencil is moving, underlines it once, then turns to see if Randi is getting up. Randi is. She says she is dying and needs coffee, and invites both Vee and Cassie to Chi Latte, the yellow one on Draper, saying, “I’m not asking, I’ve decided.” Vee knows Chi Latte as the place with lines and photos, the place she once looked into but did not enter alone because it felt like walking into someone else’s party uninvited. Her yes arrives fully formed, but she waits one beat to fold down her eagerness before saying yes.

Randi is delighted and then invites Cassie too. Cassie is already standing, putting on her ball cap, and says she has a thing. Randi offers to wait; Cassie says she is good and means it, simply declining something not hers. Cassie looks at Vee with a brief expression Vee cannot name and says, “Have fun.” Then, plainly and oddly, “Goodbye, Vee.” It lands wrong because it is not “see you later” or “bye,” but a whole, finished word for a longer parting. Vee smooths the wrongness by laughing and saying it is just coffee and she will see Cassie at home in two hours. Cassie says “Yeah,” does not fix it, lifts a hand, and leaves.

Vee turns back to Randi, already half forgetting the oddness because Randi is holding the row open and urging her to come before the line. They go to Chi Latte. Outside, Vee finally reads the sign: **Χ ΛΑΤΕ**. The Greek Chi and the latte joke resolve for her because Randi has said it. She realizes she has stood outside this joke and is now walking under it with Randi holding the door.

Inside, Chi Latte is yellow and white, bright as a commercial kitchen, smelling of coffee and chocolate, with spoons ringing against cups as everyone stirs sweetness from the bottom. It is full of girls, and Vee reads the room in detail to find her place. She sees the “Chi Latte girls”: a cluster in leggings and cropped quarter-zips, sage and dusty mauve, big scrunchies, carefully loud faces, matched prettiness. She sees two bar girls in revealing athletic clothes, bare midriffs, low waistbands, worked-for bodies wanting to be seen. She thinks “Floozies” in her mother’s voice before she finishes looking, but beneath the judgment she keeps looking longer than the word accounts for. She thinks she could wear what they wear, and that she has more and better than any of them; then her mother’s/nice-girl warning voice closes the picture down. She remains covered to the collarbone in a cardigan she wore over the better shirt on purpose.

Vee finishes placing herself in the room: she is the most of what the room sells and the one with the most fabric on. Then she looks at Randi, and the room quiets for her because Randi seems outside the performance — simply Randi. Vee wants her with “flat helpless want.” Randi has ordered her an oat-milk latte, recommends the lavender thing, and takes her to the high table by the window. Vee has seen girls sitting there like they belong, up in the window like a display. Now Randi pats the stool and Vee sits there in one clean motion, suddenly visible to the whole room but level with Randi, knees nearly touching, latte warm in her hands. The drink is good.

They talk. Vee asks who Randi rooms with; Randi says she has her own room and moves on as though it is nothing. Vee understands that means Randi is not only in that house but near the top of it, and that Randi has turned warmth on Vee anyway. Randi then really talks, and Vee listens. Later she cannot reconstruct the transcript. She remembers the shape: Randi was funny, told a story about a sister and a parking ticket that made Vee laugh hard enough to put down her cup, asked things and seemed to want the answers, mentioned a guy, and said something flattering and offhand that went into Vee like a key in a lock. Vee had leaned in and felt clever, chosen, and lit up at the window with spoons ringing around them. She did not keep the words; she kept the feeling. It is the best hour she has had in months.

When the cups are empty, Randi checks her phone and says she has a meeting, sounding genuinely sorry. Outside under the sign, she pulls Vee into a quick, complete hug, cold citrus and a hand flat between Vee’s shoulder blades. She pulls back holding Vee’s upper arms, looks at her with the lit face, and says, “This was the best. Love you, girl.” Already moving away, she adds, “See you later, okay?” Vee hears “See you later” not as empty “let’s do this again” politeness, but as a flat fact, a later scheduled somewhere she cannot see. She says “See you.” Randi leaves up Draper into the bright cool day. Vee stands under the sign, holding an empty cup, spinning a little. Already the words of the hour are gone, but she keeps “love you, girl,” “see you later,” the hand on her back, and the feeling that she has been chosen by exactly the person she would choose to be chosen by. She drops the cup in the bin and walks home the long way, warm the whole way.

Fifth chapter, “Substitution.” Vee is stuck on a hard challenge problem for forty minutes before Randi suggests they move to the place on Wilson, and then remains stuck there another twenty minutes. She is frustrated enough to set the pencil down because she thinks she might break it. Randi tells her to take a break; Vee says she has been taking a break for an hour; Randi correctly says she has been not solving it for an hour, which is different. Cassie sits two tables over with her own work and headphones in low enough to hear things.

Vee explains that she has the setup and understands the econ part, but an integral will not go. She remembers her econ instructor teaching that applied math means turning the thing in front of you into something you already know how to do. She has tried several approaches and all circle back to the start. Randi texts on her phone; Vee assumes it is some boy. Cassie watches Randi with her flat patient look.

The bell above the coffee-shop door rings. Cassie notices a cute guy in line looking at Vee. Vee turns and sees a man crossing to their table, eyes only on her face. Up close he seems ordinary in features but impressive as a whole; the room seems to settle around him. He smiles at Vee as if she is why he crossed the room. He says, “Pardon me. It sounds like you’re having trouble with math. Can I help?” Vee, not wanting the looking to stop, says it is pretty hard, calculus. He asks, “Let me try?” His voice is smaller, and she says yes, please, thank you.

He acknowledges Randi and Cassie courteously, sits, and introduces himself as Pace. Vee automatically introduces herself by her full name, “Vivienne Thorne,” confirming the jacket name on page. Being looked at loosens her and she borrows Randi’s style of introduction: “V.T. Yes, like the university. No, my parents didn’t plan it. Yes, they find it funnier than I do.” It lands, and she enjoys the borrowed poise, then quickly adds that people call her Vee. Randi gives Pace only “Randi,” without her usual adjective bit. Cassie says “Cassie” from the next table.

Pace rolls his cuffs back before reading the problem, and Vee notices his forearms, steady hands, thick knuckles, and clothes that do not fit her usual social categories. He reads the problem structurally, looks at her scratch work, says her work is right and the problem is built to send her in circles. He writes a horrible formula in the margin, tells her not to memorize it, then marks two short strokes at right angles and asks about Euler’s formula. He explains that the two difficult pieces, put together at right angles, become one plain thing. He gives her back the pencil at the exact point where she can solve it herself. Vee uses the old known thing and solves the problem cleanly.

Vee then realizes what he has done: not merely solving the math, but choosing the tutor’s path instead of showing off. He asked what her professor had said, worked from her setup, made a margin note rather than a rewrite, gave her the pencil back, and kept the solving hers. She is already smiling at him before she finishes thinking this through. Randi asks Pace why sample standard deviation uses n-minus-one instead of n; he answers briefly and well, “Bessel’s correction,” then turns back to Vee. Vee registers that he answered Randi like anyone but answered her longer.

Pace asks Vee what she studies apart from stats. She says econ. He asks, “What’s drawing you to it?” This question matters because it has room in it and is not party small talk. Vee says she likes how econ is about how people actually behave when they think they are rational — “the gap between what they say they’re doing and what they’re doing” — and likes applied math because it finds structure in real situations. She talks for several minutes about a research project she wants to do, one she has not articulated in this much detail before because no one had asked. Pace listens closely, asks follow-up questions, and does not check his phone. Vee glances at Randi, who smiles like a friend watching a friend be charmed, but whose hand keeps busy with her coffee cup: twice she lifts it without sipping. Vee reads this as boredom and wraps up.

When Vee asks what Pace does, he says he is a grad student in math, finishing a PhD more or less. Vee is embarrassed that she warned a math PhD her calculus was hard; Pace says she gave fair notice and he took it seriously. He glances at his watch, says he should run, but does not stand immediately. He says he would love to see her sometime and asks, “May I call you?” Vee hears the “may” as formal, unusual, and deeply pleasing; her body says yes before her mouth catches up. She gives him her number. He repeats it back, pockets the phone, nods politely to Randi and Cassie, says “talk soon,” and leaves without getting coffee.

After he leaves, Vee sits with her hand on the solved page and feels her chest race. Randi says he was cute and that Vee should go out with him. Vee protests that he only took her number and she does not know if he will call. Randi says it was cute watching Vee not understand what was happening. Cassie says Pace looked at Vee like he had forgotten anyone else was in the room, and at Randi like somebody’s great-aunt. Randi agrees and points out that he left without coffee: he gave up his place in line, spent the whole time on Vee, and walked out empty-handed. Randi says a man does not forget his coffee over a girl he is not going to call. Vee looks at Pace’s crossed mark and her solved work, then at her facedown phone, and lets herself want it to light up.

Sixth chapter, “The Long Way.” Pace has been gone twenty minutes, and the table has returned to books and cooling coffee when Vee’s facedown phone lights up. Her body reacts first, like missing a stair in the dark. The text is from an unknown number — Pace’s, because she gave him hers but forgot to ask for his — asking whether she likes frozen yogurt. Vee tries to defend herself from wanting by asking whether he is desperate to text this fast. Randi takes the phone, says he did not look desperate but like a man who wants Vee, jokes that he wants to see her naked, and tells Vee she should go. Cassie jokes that Pace had better not make Vee roll a healer again, revealing that Vee once spent a whole semester staying up until two keeping a business major alive in a video game. Vee considers making Pace wait because of “the rule” that you do not answer the first text for a day, but Randi says that rule is for boys you are not sure about, and Vee is not unsure.

By evening, Vee and Pace stand together under neon in a too-bright frozen-yogurt shop with dusty plastic ivy, humming machines, people coming and going, lemon cleaner cutting through the sugar smell. Pace studies the flavor placards with the same attention he gave her calculus and explains his system: tart on the bottom supports heavier flavors, while chocolate on tart is “an argument the whole way down.” Vee laughs when he says he has thought about most things.

Vee builds her cup without thinking and takes too much; it leans and threatens to drip. Usually she hears her mother’s voice saying “that’s plenty, that’s enough now,” but this time there is nothing, and Vee realizes she does not care. Pace weighs his cup and pays for both before she can get her wallet. Vee grabs spoons and napkins; Pace holds the door with his back so they can both get out with full hands.

Outside, evening has gone blue and cool. They walk the long way, not toward anything. Pace asks questions the way Vee’s grandfather used to: as though the answer mattered and there was no wrong one. He waits through all of her answers. Vee realizes she is not used to being the thing a person waits on. She says she is going to say something embarrassing and then they can both pretend she did not. She tells him, “This is the nicest a person has been to me in a really long time. And it’s froyo.” She winces and asks him to pretend. Pace does not pretend and does not make it too large. He walks beside her for a moment, letting the true thing have space, then says, “Your tart’s holding up.” Vee looks down and sees that the whole stack is still standing.

Seventh chapter, “Water Wings.” Vee and Cassie go to the rec pool. The pool requires a real suit, checked by a lifeguard at the desk. Cassie fights into an old plain navy suit “like it had insulted her family.” She does not go to a pool to be admired and is not bothered that the suit does not flatter her boxy frame. Vee wears a green-and-white one-piece from when she was sixteen and did synchronized swimming, back when there was less of her. Now there is more of her; the suit fits but gives her nowhere to hide.

They walk from the locker area to the water along the bleachers. Vee rolls her towel with old trained knowledge and carries it down at her side rather than using it to hide. She “puts her face on”: chin level, shoulders open, walking like she meant to arrive. Guys in the bleachers call out gross comments — “You won’t need floaties with those!” and “Legs for days” — and laugh in the way of men applauding each other. Vee answers with a blink and an eyeroll, holding her game face. Cassie quietly says, “Tossers,” then, “They will never know the love of a woman,” just for Vee. Vee laughs harder than the joke earns because Cassie has kept the rescue private.

Cassie wades in only to rib depth, plants herself, and tells Vee to go on; she will be right there, “Standing. Like a person.” Vee puts the towel on the bench and goes into the deeper water. She crosses the dark stripe where the shallow gives out, lets her feet leave the floor, takes the familiar breath, and goes under. Underwater the room switches off: no bleacher laughter, lifeguard radio, booming space, or Cassie, only green cold, light through the surface, and her heartbeat. Her hands remember the slow sculling figure-eight from synchronized swimming. She feels weightless, with nothing holding her and nothing to hold. She thinks she loved this before she had a body to be a problem, and that water is the one part of it that was never taken. She stays until her lungs ask, and a beat past that, and grieves a little.

When Vee surfaces, the noise and business of having a body in public return. Cassie sees that Vee has gone somewhere and come back “with her face on wrong,” but she does not ask because asking would make the sad version bigger. Instead, from ten feet away, Cassie shoots a sheet of water at Vee. It barely gets her, which makes Vee’s outrage disproportionate and funny. Vee swims toward her, gets her feet in the shallow, and splashes Cassie back hard. Cassie takes the wave full in the chest without changing expression. Vee shriek-laughs, an ugly laugh she has not made in a while, and splashes again. The grief from underwater does not survive the water fight. The chapter ends with the sense that the body that is such a problem has stayed up on the deck with the rolled towel, waiting.

**How I feel:**  
Pace attracts me strongly: his patience, competence, care, restraint, and erotic confidence are persuasive. In “Substitution,” his intellectual generosity — especially giving Vee back the pencil — makes him even more attractive. In “The Long Way,” his frozen-yogurt date with Vee is almost painfully tender: he listens, pays attention to ordinary things, waits through her sentences, and answers her embarrassing confession with exactly the right lightness. I also distrust his certainty. He is not reading the same ending Randi is living, and that gap feels dangerous. After “Standards,” the old line “He’s hard to say no to” made me more uneasy around him, even though the page has shown him asking, waiting, and checking consent. After “The Pointing Game,” my unease sharpened: he knowingly asks an unfair question during sex, then builds a whole daylight structure around the answer her body gave. In “Substitution” and “The Long Way,” he is tenderly courteous to Vee, but because I know about the game, every courtesy feels double.

Randi has my sympathy and fascination. She is beautiful and controlled, and the book keeps revealing the cost of that control. Her bathroom decision after the bench hurt; her numb morning after in the sorority house hurt in a different way. In “The Pointing Game,” her desire for women, and specifically for Vee, feels real and exposed, but she immediately needs to make it survivable by handing it to Pace as a threesome “with you.” In “See You Later,” seeing her through Vee’s eyes makes her power vivid: she is dazzling, warm, practiced, and capable of making another woman feel admitted into life. In “Substitution,” her stillness and coffee-cup tells make me wonder what it costs her to watch Pace charm Vee. In “The Long Way,” she pushes Vee toward Pace with joking warmth and apparent certainty. I still feel for her, but I also fear what she can do.

Vee/Vivienne has my full sympathy. Her first entrance felt like sunlight and danger; her POV reveals longing, intelligence, shame, humor, and acute social perception. She wants Randi, or wants what Randi represents, or both, before she can name it. In “Substitution,” her intellectual life becomes vivid: she likes econ, applied math, structure, and the gap between stated motives and real behavior. In “The Long Way,” her hunger for ordinary kindness becomes unmistakable, especially when she admits that frozen yogurt with Pace is the nicest anyone has been to her in a long time. In “Water Wings,” I see how long she has had to manage having a body in public, and how precious it is for her to have any place where nobody wants anything from her. I want her protected because she does not know she has been chosen in a game, and now Pace and Randi have reached her through the parts of herself that long to be heard, chosen, admired, and allowed.

Cassie has become even more important to me. Her flatness resists Randi’s charm; her “Goodbye, Vee” feels like either warning, unease, or simply her strange plainness. In “Substitution,” she again notices what matters, including Pace’s look and Randi’s odd social placement. In “The Long Way,” her healer joke suggests she knows Vee has a pattern of overextending herself for others. In “Water Wings,” she is quietly, beautifully loyal: she protects Vee from bleacher humiliation with a private joke, gives her space underwater, and brings her back from grief with play instead of questions. I trust her more than anyone.

Brooke now feels personally formidable: not just imagined social pressure but a warm, seated authority who can interrogate without seeming to. I distrust her.

Sloane and Kinsey feel like the common room’s social weather: not individually threatening yet, but part of the watching system that makes Randi perform.

Randi’s mother feels like an old source of deprivation/shame, especially around wanting, food, and the childhood voice Randi no longer uses.

Vee’s mother feels like another source of internalized shame, especially around clothing, exposure, sexuality, “nice girl” attention, approved politeness, and food/appetite.

Vee’s grandfather now feels like a remembered source of gentle attention: someone who asked questions as though answers mattered and there was no wrong one.

Overall mood: aroused less by explicit sex now than by attention, longing, appetite, and emotional safety; increasingly anxious because the trap is working beautifully. “Water Wings” adds a quieter grief: Vee already knows what it is to be made into a body by strangers, which makes Pace’s and Randi’s gentler, more attentive wanting feel even more powerful and dangerous.
