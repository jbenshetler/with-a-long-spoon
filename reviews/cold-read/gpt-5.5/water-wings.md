## Reader reaction

This chapter feels like a sudden breath, and I mean that almost physically. After all the charged social seduction and hidden pressure of Randi and Pace, being with Vee and Cassie at the rec pool is a relief — not because Vee is untroubled, but because Cassie is real air. The chapter gives me Vee’s body without Pace’s gaze, without Randi’s glamorous social sorting, without men being permitted to interpret it for her. And the difference is enormous.

I feel so much affection for Cassie here. She is funny, loyal, and exactly calibrated. Her complaint about the swimsuit — “Who designed this? Genuinely. What man?” — made me laugh, but it also sets up the chapter’s whole world: women having to put bodies into public rules and public fabrics that do not feel made for them. Cassie’s plain navy suit and “boxy frame” are described without making her pathetic or insecure. “Nobody was going to look at Cassie in it. Cassie knew that, and it didn't bother her one bit.” I like her even more for that. She has a kind of freedom Vee does not have.

And then when the guys in the bleachers call out — “You won't need floaties with *those!*” and “Legs for *days*” — I felt Vee’s old armor click into place. She has “a hundred pool decks” of practice: “chin level, shoulders open, the walk of a girl who had *meant* to arrive.” That line hurts, because it is confidence as survival, not ease. She knows how to look like she meant to be seen because she has been seen too much, and wrongly. My protectiveness toward her deepened a lot here. The suit “fit, and it gave her nowhere to hide” is simple and devastating.

Cassie’s “Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman” is perfect because she does not escalate the moment into something Vee has to manage. She keeps it low, “just for the two of them.” That moved me. Cassie could have fought the bleachers, but instead she gives Vee a way to exhale. That feels like friendship in the body, not friendship as speech.

The erotic charge here is present, but not in a directly arousing way for me. It is bodily, sensual, vulnerable: the wet tile, chlorine, the too-fitting one-piece, men noticing breasts and legs, Vee underwater. But the chapter very deliberately moves away from the kind of gaze that turns her body into use. The peak is actually anti-erotic, or maybe pre-erotic: “She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem, and the water was the one part of it that had never been taken.” That line hit hard. It made me sad, and tender toward her. The water gives her a self before shame, before display, before being chosen or wanted.

The underwater passage is the center of the chapter for me. It works beautifully: “The room switched off,” “the swallowing green,” “the bright moving ceiling of the surface from underneath.” It made me feel my own breathing slow. Vee gets to be unheld and unwatched: “Nobody up there could see her. Nobody down here wanted anything.” Given how much of the book has been about looking, choosing, arranging, being seen, this feels like a sacred little pocket of privacy.

I do not feel suspicion toward Cassie. If anything, my trust in her rises. She sees Vee come back “with her face on wrong,” and she does not ask, because “asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger.” That is such a Cassie kind of care: not verbal, not sentimental, but exact. She throws water at Vee instead. And it works. The grief does not get explained; it gets interrupted by play. I loved that.

The only small friction I had was with “Nobody was going to look at Cassie in it.” It is in Vee’s perspective and seems affectionate, not cruel, but it still gave me a tiny pinch because it files Cassie so bluntly as unlooked-at. But the chapter immediately complicates that by making Cassie the freer one, the one not trapped by admiration or sexual appraisal. So the pinch didn’t become mistrust of the writing; it felt like part of Vee’s sharp social/body awareness.

The title, “Water Wings,” lands in two ways for me. First, it echoes the bleacher insult — “floaties” — and all the childish/public humiliation around buoyancy, breasts, and being made visible. But after reading the chapter, it also feels gentler: the water gives Vee wings, or gives her back a body that can move without being a problem. Actual water wings are for people who cannot yet swim, but Vee can swim; she has old competence. So the title feels a little ironic and a little protective. Maybe Cassie is the water wings too: the thing that keeps Vee afloat when the public world makes her body heavy.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, still feels like a warning I do not fully understand, and the tagline keeps darkening everything around Vee’s yeses. Here, though, the chapter reminds me Vee has a life and a body outside Randi and Pace’s trap. She is not just the girl being seduced. She has history, skills, grief, friendship, and a place — underwater — where nobody wants anything from her. That makes me dread the seduction more, honestly. There is more to lose.

What I want next: I want to stay with Vee and Cassie a little longer. I want Cassie to remain close enough to see what is happening. I dread Vee moving from this private, cleansing, playful chapter back into Pace and Randi’s arranged attention. My guess from here is that the pool matters because Vee’s body is about to become more explicitly desired, and the book wants me to know what being looked at has cost her before that happens.

I believe his care in the physical and sensual sense, and I believe he wants Randi happy and pleasured. “The Pointing Game” made clear that he will knowingly use unfair timing, bodily evidence, and strategic framing while telling himself this is kindness. “Substitution” makes him even more dangerous because he is not only erotically skilled with bodies but pedagogically and emotionally skilled with minds. With Vee, he behaves respectfully and beautifully on the surface: asks permission, does not show off, gives her back the pencil, listens to her mind, asks “May I call you?”, texts quickly, takes her for frozen yogurt, pays, holds the door, listens through her sentences, and handles her vulnerability gently. That charm works on me, and because it works, I distrust the hidden context more. He is absent in “Water Wings,” but Vee’s history of being looked at and wanting bodily privacy makes his attentive future gaze feel more powerful and more dangerous.

Randi has my sympathy, fascination, and growing unease. She is not passive: she desires, reframes, chooses, evaluates, performs, and acts with stunning social skill. With Vee, she is luminous and effective; I feel why Vee wants her attention. In “Substitution,” she is also harder to read: the bare “Randi,” the coffee-cup gestures, and her warm encouragement after Pace leaves all suggest she is managing something beneath the friend-performance. In “The Long Way,” she pushes Vee toward Pace’s quick text while teasing sexually that Pace wants to see Vee naked. Her attention may be sincere and still manipulative. She is absent in “Water Wings,” but the chapter makes me think more sharply about what it means that Randi first saw Vee laughing as if she had forgotten she could be seen.

Vivienne / Vee has my full sympathy. She is warm, observant, socially alert, intellectually serious, and deeply hungry to be chosen and taken seriously. She is not naive in the sense of not reading rooms; she reads them constantly. But she does not know the hidden premise of either Randi’s approach or Pace’s presence/contact, and her longing for entry into a brighter, more attentive life makes her vulnerable. Her attraction to Pace now feels as believable as her attraction/admiration toward Randi. In “The Long Way,” I felt especially protective of her because she admits that ordinary froyo feels like the nicest anyone has been to her in a long time. In “Water Wings,” my protectiveness deepens further: Vee has old competence and old grief around her body, knows how to survive public sexual attention, and still has a private underwater self where nobody wants anything from her. I badly want that part of her protected.

Cassie has become one of my most trusted people on the page. She is blunt, sparse, not charmed by Randi’s bit, and her “Goodbye, Vee” felt like either warning, instinct, or refusal to participate in the social spell. In “Substitution,” she notices Pace looking at Vee, Randi’s phone/cup, and the different ways Pace looks at Vee and Randi. In “The Long Way,” she jokes about Vee’s healer semester and remains dry and grounding. In “Water Wings,” she becomes even more beloved to me: she protects Vee without making a scene, sees Vee’s sadness without demanding its story, and knows how to bring Vee back through ridiculous play. She may not know what is happening with Randi and Pace, but she sees Vee.

Brooke feels like a real and formidable social power: warm, polished, friendly, possibly caring, but also capable of interrogation through smiles and institutional authority. I do not know if she is malicious, but I do not feel safe around her.

Sloane feels like social commentary in human form so far: amused, observant, classifying Pace through truck/status jokes while sounding complimentary.

Kinsey feels younger or less powerful in the room, eager to join the warmth, but still part of the social pressure on Randi.

Randi’s mother feels like a source of old pain around need, food, beauty, and refusal; Randi has had twenty-one years of not-going toward crying or need.

Vee’s mother feels like another source of internalized shame, around sexual display, nice-girl respectability, and food quantity. The absence of her “that’s plenty” voice during froyo feels like a small but significant loosening, while “Water Wings” shows the body shame continuing even without her voice explicitly named.

Vee’s grandfather now stands as an apparently positive memory of being asked questions as if her answers mattered and there was no wrong one; Pace touches that memory through his listening.

Overall mood: erotic heat has shifted into social, intellectual, ordinary-romantic, and now bodily/private seduction with dread underneath. The story now shows both halves of the origin: Pace and Randi choosing Vee as part of a game, and Vee experiencing Randi and Pace as spontaneous, respectful, life-brightening attention. “Water Wings” pauses that seduction to show Vee’s own body-history, friendship with Cassie, and private refuge underwater. The tagline feels sharper than ever: every yes is real — Vee’s yes to coffee, help, the phone number, answering the text, frozen yogurt, the long walk, honest confession, and even her bodily yes to water and play — and that is exactly why it frightens me.
