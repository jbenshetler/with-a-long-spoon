## Reader reaction

This chapter made me relax in a way I wasn’t expecting, and then it hurt a little. After all the charged attention from Randi and Pace, it is almost a relief to be with Cassie and Vee in a place that is not seductive except by accident — the rec pool, chlorine, locker keys, an old swimsuit, ugly institutional rules. The body is still the battleground, but nobody in the scene is trying to claim Vee. Cassie, especially, feels like clean air here.

I love Cassie more after this. She is so beautifully unsentimental. She hates the suit, names it perfectly — “Who designed this? Genuinely. What man?” — but she is not being written as insecure in the way Vee is. “The suit didn’t do her any favors. Cassie knew that, and it didn’t bother her one bit.” That sentence made me trust her even more. She has a kind of bodily indifference that feels almost holy next to Vee’s inherited shame and Randi’s polish/control. And the way she protects Vee without making a performance of protection is exactly right: she keeps “Tossers” low, “just for the two of them,” when she could have humiliated the boys. She chooses Vee’s ease over victory.

Vee breaks my heart here. The green-and-white one-piece from synchronized swimming is such a painful object: “the one she’d worn at sixteen, for synchronized swimming, when there’d been less of her to fill it. There was more of her now.” It fits, which is worse than not fitting, because “it gave her nowhere to hide.” That’s a very Vee sentence: abundance, shame, exposure, and the cruel practicality of clothing all at once. The boys in the bleachers are gross in an ordinary way, and I felt that whole walk down the bleachers as a kind of armor drill: “chin level, shoulders open, the walk of a girl who had meant to arrive.” She can do it, but it costs her.

The underwater passage is the erotic/emotional center, though not sexually erotic exactly. It is sensual and bodily, but private: “the swallowing green,” “the light coming down through the surface in slow sheets,” “her own heartbeat, close and steady.” It worked on me as a kind of anti-seduction: nobody looking, nobody asking, nobody wanting. The line that landed hardest was: “She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem, and the water was the one part of it that had never been taken.” That feels like the chapter’s core. Vee has a place in her own body that predates being watched.

Trust vs suspicion: I have no suspicion of Cassie here. None. If anything, the chapter sharpens my sense that Cassie is the safest person in Vee’s life so far because she notices without harvesting. “Cassie never asked — asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger.” That could be too cute in another context, but for Cassie it feels earned: she intervenes with a sheet of water instead of concern. The only “off” thing is not Cassie or Vee; it is the world around Vee, the bleacher boys, the requirement that she pass through public looking before she can reach the water.

Erotic charge is low but not absent. Vee in the too-revealing suit, the comments from the bleachers, the “there was more of her now” awareness — all of that has bodily charge, but the chapter refuses to make it pleasurable for the hecklers or for me. The most intimate charge is in her competence: the rolled towel, the old sculling figure-eight, the underwater ease. It peaks not at being looked at, but at being unseen.

I had almost no friction. If anything, the last sentence is strong enough that I paused over whether it overstates itself: “The body that was such a problem stayed up on the deck with the rolled towel, where it could wait.” But I bought it. It’s not literally true, of course — her body is in the water — but emotionally it is exactly what the chapter has earned. The deck holds the problem-body, the social-body, the looked-at body. Underwater, she gets the old body back.

The chapter title, “Water Wings,” is tender and a little ironic. There are no floaties except the boys’ crude joke — “You won’t need floaties with those!” — so the title first points at humiliation. But by the end, water itself gives Vee wings: suspension, privacy, escape from gravity and gaze. It also makes me think of childhood, before the body became sexualized and watched. The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, still feels like indirect reach and careful stirring from a distance; after the literal spoons at Chi Latte and froyo, this chapter adds water instead of spoons, but the tagline is ringing louder. “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” Here, Vee’s yes is not to Pace or Randi but to her own old pleasure. That makes me dread how easily genuine appetite and genuine refuge can be routed into someone else’s design.

What I want next: I want more Cassie. I want Vee to tell Cassie more than she thinks she can. I dread the return of Pace and Randi now, not because I don’t enjoy them — I do — but because this chapter reminded me what Vee is like when nobody is arranging her. My guess from here is that the pool/body imagery will matter when Vee is later asked to be exposed or seen. This chapter has made her body feel more sacred to me, which makes the hidden game around it feel more dangerous.
