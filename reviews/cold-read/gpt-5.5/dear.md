## Reader reaction

This chapter made me feel that slightly sick-sweet social vertigo of being taken somewhere glamorous and realizing, too late, that the room has rules you don’t know. I felt Vee’s brightness walking beside Randi — “like a person things happened to. Good things” — and then felt it dim almost bodily at The Smoothie Bird. The cardigan “covered her like an apology” is exactly the kind of sentence that lands in my stomach: not because anything has happened yet, but because she has been returned to the old condition of being visible and wrong.

Vee is so vulnerable here, and not in a foolish way. She’s smart enough to know some things and not yet able to look directly at others. I noticed the line where she tells herself it’s healthy to have separate people and “underneath it something turned over, small, and she let it lie.” That feels important emotionally, not like a clue I’m solving: she is choosing not to notice that Cassie leaving and Randi claiming her are not neutral. I still adore her. I am also frightened by how much being “picked” answers something starved in her.

Randi moved me in two directions at once. She is lying constantly by omission — the fake date story is especially poisonous because I know Pace was her lover, not some anonymous disaster she can compare Vee’s romance against. But she also genuinely gives Vee something here. When Brooke appears and Vee shrinks herself to protect Randi, Randi says, “Vee’s my dear friend,” and takes her hand publicly. I felt the force of that. I believe Vee’s read that it “had cost something.” Randi spends social capital on her. That does not erase the game; if anything, it makes it more dangerous, because Randi’s affection is not fake enough to reject.

Brooke immediately chills the room for me. She does almost nothing overtly cruel. She smiles warmly. She says, “We should all get dinner sometime.” But the text has earned my suspicion of her already, and here it earns it again: her smile “take[s] her measure, top to bottom, and stay[s] warm the whole way.” That is horrible because it is so socially plausible. The taller unnamed woman is also uncomfortable: silent, appraising, “the look you turn on a thing once you are done turning it over.” I don’t know her name yet, but I don’t like being under her eyes.

Cassie’s brief exit hurt more than I expected. She sees Randi light up and reach for Vee, and whatever is in Cassie’s eyes gets “folded away.” I trust Cassie, and this felt like a tiny private warning or hurt. The text doesn’t tell me she knows anything; she doesn’t. But she sees the rearrangement of Vee’s attention, and Vee “did not watch her go.” That stung.

Pace is absent, but his presence is everywhere because Vee is recounting him to Randi. The erotic charge peaks not in touch but in Vee saying, “May I. Not can I. Who even says that,” and again in Randi’s slow, almost hungry repetition: “You let him choose for you. And you liked it.” That exchange is charged because Randi understands the shape of the surrender better than Vee does. The chapter is not sexually explicit, but it is deeply erotic in the way Vee confesses submission to someone who already knows the machinery of Pace’s attention. “Because of him” lands as romantic for Vee and ominous for me.

The fake “my love life is a disaster” story gave me friction in the best uneasy way. I did not dislike the writing; I disliked Randi doing it. “Friday. A date. Tall, dreamy, dressed like money…” — she’s fabricating a clean social cover while encouraging Vee to praise the man they both share, though Vee does not know that. It made me feel complicit and trapped as a reader: Vee is telling the truth into a room where Randi is not.

The chapter title, “Dear,” blooms late. At first I took it as affectionate, maybe Vee being cherished. Then Randi says, “Vee’s my dear friend,” and the title fastens there. It’s tender, public, socially meaningful — and also slightly performative, a word with etiquette in it. “Dear” is warmth, but also a category safe enough to say in front of Brooke. It illuminates the chapter without giving too much away. The book title, *A Polite Invitation*, feels more and more exact: every door opens through courtesy, through “May I,” through “dear friend,” through invitations to beverages and dinners. *With a Long Spoon* still suggests eating with danger at a distance, and this chapter’s smoothies, swallowed shame, and appetite-confession keep that alive. “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap” is practically glowing over Vee saying yes to Randi’s outing, yes to telling the story, yes to being publicly chosen.

What I want next: I want Cassie to say what she saw. I want Randi alone after this, because I need to know what it cost her and whether she reports it to Pace. I dread Brooke taking an interest in Vee. I also dread Vee’s next step toward Pace’s private world, because now both seductions — his and Randi’s — are feeding the same hunger in her.

**How I feel:**  
Pace is intensely attractive and intensely suspect. His patience, competence, restraint, courtesy, and listening are seductive. He seems genuinely attentive to Vee’s safety and pleasure, and in “May I Choose” he earns her trust on the page by leaving room, choosing carefully, feeding without shaming, and respecting a boundary. In “Off Six-Fourteen,” Vee’s report confirms he accepted her no and let her initiate the kiss. In “Dear,” his absence does not weaken him; Vee’s account of him to Randi makes his “May I choose?” and his listening feel even more powerful. But because I know he and Randi chose Vee in a game, every good act is double. The better he is, the more dangerous the trap feels. The hiking invitation still raises the stakes because it points toward his private house/land/road.

Randi remains sympathetic, fascinating, and alarming. She is wounded by being seen, trapped by her face and Standards, and genuinely drawn to Vee, but she is also an accomplice in bringing Vee toward Pace without honesty. In “Dear,” she lies directly/performatively about a disastrous date while encouraging Vee to confess her feelings about Pace, which makes me distrust her more. At the same time, calling Vee “my dear friend” and holding her hand in front of Brooke feels real and costly. Her affection is not fake; that makes it more dangerous, not less.

Vee has my full protectiveness. She is intelligent, observant, hungry for kindness, ashamed and defended around body/appetite, and opening quickly because Pace’s and Randi’s careful attentions reach exactly where she has been deprived. Her “yes” to letting Pace choose dinner, her initiated kiss, her fast yes to hiking, and now her joy at Randi publicly claiming her all feel freely chosen and emotionally real, which is precisely why I worry. In “Dear,” her need to feel picked becomes unmistakable.

Cassie is still my trusted anchor. She sees Vee’s patterns and protects without smothering. She does not know the hidden game, so her approval of Pace is limited, but her love for Vee feels steady. In “Dear,” her folded-away look as Vee turns toward Randi makes me wonder if she is starting to feel Vee being pulled away or at least sees more than Vee notices.

Brooke remains socially formidable and untrusted. Randi’s mother and Vee’s mother remain sources of internalized shame; Vee’s grandfather remains a memory of gentle attention. Christine makes Pace seem rooted and known in a life outside campus.

Overall mood: sensual, tender, hungry, socially anxious, and tightening. The romance is working on me, but the title/tagline keep making every beautiful yes and every public kindness sound like a lock clicking.
