## Reader reaction

This chapter made me feel warm and sick at the same time. Warm because Vee is so alive inside being chosen — by Pace, by Randi, by the sudden feeling that her life has “color in it.” Sick because I can feel how vulnerable that makes her, and because Randi’s kindness lands beautifully while I know there is another layer underneath it.

Vee is the person I’m most inside here. The first little hurt is Cassie leaving: “whatever had been in them was folded away.” Vee doesn’t watch her go because “Randi had her by the wrist,” and that felt painfully exact — not malicious, not even dramatic, just the way one bright attachment can eclipse a steadier one. Then the smoothie shop undresses her socially: the cardigan “covered her like an apology,” the high table makes her feel “set up on a shelf,” her feet hang, the room can look at her. I felt that bodily. I trusted her embarrassment because it isn’t overexplained; it arrives through chair height, clothing, noise, and the sweating pink drink in her hand.

Randi is dazzling again, and more dangerous because she is so good. Her fake story about the disastrous date — or at least the story she presents as her love life — is a big shift in my suspicion. On the surface it is a perfect intimacy move: she makes room for Vee’s good news by saying, essentially, tell me your man listens better than mine. But because I know Pace is Randi’s secret lover, the whole thing feels staged and unclean. “My love life is a disaster. Friday. A date.” Is that a lie? Almost certainly, from what I know. The line “what you have” also pricked me: “Before you give me your good news, you have to hear my catastrophe, so you understand what you have.” Randi is teaching Vee how to value Pace. She’s not just listening; she’s framing him.

And yet — the chapter keeps making me believe Randi’s feeling for Vee is real. When Brooke appears, Randi could easily reduce Vee to “stats friend” and preserve her own social smoothness. Instead Vee says, “We have stats together,” shrinking herself for Randi’s sake, and Randi answers, “Vee’s my dear friend.” That hit me. The hand on the table in public hit me more. I don’t know if Vee is right that it “had cost something,” but the text made me feel the cost: the sorority/social world watching, Brooke’s polished warmth, the taller silent woman’s appraising circuit. Randi spends social capital on Vee there, and Vee feels picked out loud. I was moved by it even while distrusting it.

Pace is absent physically but very present through Vee’s report, and my feeling about him splits exactly as before. Vee’s retelling of “May I choose?” is erotic and tender all over again. The phrase has become almost a spell for her: “May I. Not can I. Who even says that.” Her surrender — “I stopped keeping track of myself” — is intimate, and I understand why it works on her. But Randi repeating, “You let him choose for you. And you liked it,” makes the same tenderness feel more openly like training, or at least like a discovered appetite being named and encouraged. The peak erotic charge of this chapter is not physical contact, really, but that conversation: Vee admitting she liked handing choice over, Randi holding that admission gently, and then Vee saying she kissed Pace “because of him.” It’s clothed, public, smoothie-shop eroticism: shame, confession, praise, being seen.

Cassie moves slightly away from the center here, and I missed her. I also felt protective of her. The opening glance suggests she notices something — Randi lit up, reaching for Vee; Vee half-turned away from Cassie — and folds away whatever she feels. I don’t think Cassie is jealous exactly, or not only that. It reads like she sees the social/emotional shift and chooses not to make Vee manage it. That made me trust Cassie more and feel a little sad for her.

Brooke’s appearance chilled me. She is warm, socially flawless, and terrifyingly measuring. “And who’s your new friend?” is perfectly friendly and still a test. The taller woman — not named on the page here, though I recognize from memory she may be one of the sorority women if named before; in this chapter she remains “the taller one” — feels even colder because she says nothing and only looks. The line “the look you turn on a thing once you are done turning it over” made me wince. It’s not cartoon cruelty. It’s worse: polite assessment.

Trust vs suspicion: the text earns suspicion of Randi very strongly here, not because she is cruel, but because she is lying and guiding. Her “Paul?” made me pause. Is she pretending not to remember Pace’s name to hide that she knows him intimately? That felt like an active deception, and it was ugly because Vee receives it as casual girlish forgetfulness. I also don’t entirely trust the “dear friend” moment, even though I believe it emotionally. It may be both true and useful, and that is the trap’s whole flavor.

Erotically, the chapter works best in the confession about letting Pace choose and in Randi’s slow repetition of it. “You let him choose for you. And you liked it.” That made the air change. The kiss report also has charge because Vee is proud and a little defiant: “Not six years old when I kissed him.” It goes slack only slightly in the smoothie-shop social inventory, but not badly; the room’s texture is doing emotional work. Nothing felt clinical.

The title, “Dear,” lands first through Randi’s “Vee’s my dear friend.” That is the hinge. “Dear” means beloved, socially named, costly, publicly claimed — but also “dear” as expensive, something that costs. Vee senses that: Randi “had spent it on her.” I like the title more after finishing than before; it did not give too much away, but it quietly points to the price of being named dear.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, keeps gathering around appetite and distance. Here we get smoothies, the wrong drink chosen under Randi’s influence, the high table, the sweating cup, and the sense of being fed emotionally from a careful distance. The tagline — “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” — feels painfully active. Vee freely says yes to Randi’s company, yes to telling her everything, yes to accepting “dear friend” as a place. None of those yeses are forced. That is why they scare me.

What I want next: I want Cassie to keep watching, and I want Vee to notice that Randi pretended not to know Pace’s name. I dread the hiking date because it takes Vee farther from public rooms and closer to Pace’s private world. I also now want to know what Brooke will make of Vee, because the sorority world has officially noticed her.

Boys in the bleachers call out crude comments: “You won’t need floaties with those!” and “Legs for days.” Cassie quietly calls them “Tossers” and says they will never know the love of a woman, making Vee laugh and release the held breath of the walk. Cassie stops rib-deep in the water and tells Vee she will be right there “Standing. Like a person.” Vee crosses into the deep, goes underwater, and experiences the room switching off: noise, looking, wanting, and the business of having a body disappear into green quiet. Her hands remember old synchronized-swimming sculling shapes. She remembers loving the water before she had a body to be a problem; the water feels like the one part of that old belonging never taken, and she grieves a little. When she comes up with her face wrong, Cassie notices but does not ask, because asking would make the sad version bigger. Instead Cassie shoots a sheet of water at her. Vee retaliates, they splash-fight, and Vee makes an ugly shriek-laugh she has not made in a while. The splashing wins over grief. The chapter ends with the body that was such a problem seeming to stay up on the deck with the rolled towel, where it can wait.

Eighth chapter, “May I Choose.” Friday evening, after the pool, Vee is still carrying chlorine in her hair and good heavy tiredness in her arms while Cassie sits folded on the bottom bunk, narrating a professor’s crimes. Vee’s phone lights: Pace texts asking for Saturday dinner. He does not say where and does not require her to answer that night. Vee reads the text repeatedly and is moved by the room he has built into it. She tells Cassie that other boys always made her answer immediately so they would know where they stood. Cassie teases her “whole face” but then takes the deeper thing seriously and says, “That’s not a boy. That’s a man.” The next evening Pace comes to get Vee for the first time. She sees him in the dorm lobby before he sees her: showered, shaved, wearing a clean pressed broadcloth shirt and cared-for good boots. She nearly misses the elevator opening because she is looking at him. He asks if she minds walking and does not tell her where; Vee decides she is already learning to trust where he is taking her. He takes her to a small warm restaurant on a nearby block she has never noticed. Christine, an older woman who knows him warmly, seats them. Vee recognizes only hummus on the dog-eared menu and says the rest is beautiful nonsense. Pace asks, “May I choose?” Vee says, “Yes. Please.” He asks if there is anything she won’t eat, then orders deliberately. Vee does not know the food words — baba ghanoush, tabbouleh — and experiences them as soft closed doors with promises behind them. Food arrives: bright green, smoke-dark, blistered steaming bread. Pace tears bread, folds food into it, and Vee follows. The sharp bright taste wakes her hunger; the smoky dark taste folds her under. She eats without watching herself eat, and he does not make anything of it. She watches his right hand repeatedly — tearing bread, eating, catching a drip from his hand with his mouth — and loses the thread of his words. For once she lets herself stop keeping up and lets him carry the evening. Mint tea in small glasses with worked brass cages wakes her back up; the room has half-emptied and it is late. Pace settles with Christine while Vee gets her coat; Christine says something that makes him duck his head. Outside, Pace takes Vee’s hand without ceremony. It feels much larger than a hand should feel, like something resolving. He walks her back the long way through quiet blue campus. Under a tree shadow, he slows and turns; Vee says, “I don't kiss a man I've known a couple of days,” naming an old rule. Pace does not argue or press; he smiles and starts them gently back along the path. Vee does not go. She stops him with their joined hands, lets the quiet hold, then says, “But,” lets the thread go, closes the distance, and says, “I’m making an exception.”

Ninth chapter, “Off Six-Fourteen.” Vee comes back to the dorm room after the dinner date still warm from the evening, the walk, and Pace. Cassie has the window cracked because the building overheats; the cold line from the sill does not bother Vee. Vee has been talking since she came through the door, spilling out the story while Cassie abandons her reading and listens from the lower bunk, asking small questions. Vee retells the tea in little brass cages, then slows at the part under the tree. She tells Cassie she said the “stupid starchy thing” about not kissing a man she had known a few days, and that Pace did nothing — he smiled and started walking on as if it was fine. Vee says she did not go, that she stopped him, pulled him back, and kissed him herself: “Me.” Cassie is genuinely glad but asks how much Pace gave her to drink. Vee laughs and insists there was no wine, only tea, and that nobody did anything to her or talked her into it; she did it because she wanted to and on purpose. Cassie accepts this and says, “Look at you.” Vee cannot contain herself, spins once in the small room, nearly hits the closet door, collapses into the bean bag laughing, and says, “Look at me.” She then tells Cassie Pace asked her hiking on a trail he likes and that she said yes fast, though she has never hiked in her life. Cassie jokes that she needs Pace’s name for the state troopers when Vee goes missing on a mountain with a man she met at a coffee shop. Vee throws a pillow and says Pace is lovely and she is not going missing. She says he lives out west of town, off Six-Fourteen, past where the road goes to gravel, and that he will have water and will not walk her off a cliff. Cassie repeats “Off six-fourteen” and points out that this is not much of an address. Cassie asks whether Pace is rich because “Rich people hike. Poor people walk.” Vee realizes she has not been to his house yet but feels this as something pleasant still to find out. Pace told her to wear real shoes. Vee says she will borrow real shoes. Cassie returns to her reading and tells her to borrow good ones. Vee lies back in the bean bag warm and does not feel the cold from the window.

Tenth chapter, “Dear.” After stats, Cassie gets up to leave for practice while Vee is already half-turned toward Randi, who has lit up and reached for her. Cassie sees this, folds away whatever look she had, says she will see Vee at the dorm, and leaves. Randi takes Vee by the wrist and demands a full report on Pace, pretending not to remember his name and guessing “Paul?” Vee corrects her. Randi has forty irresponsible minutes and takes Vee to Smoothie Bird, a bright cold noisy smoothie shop full of polished matched girls in leggings and exact light makeup. Vee feels her pilled cardigan cover her like an apology and feels underdressed and displayed, especially when the only seats are a high white window table where her feet hang and the room can look at her. Randi orders a beet/wheatgrass/egg white/oat milk smoothie, a bruise-colored disciplinary drink; Vee orders strawberry banana with oat milk and senses she said oat milk because Randi stood there. Randi tells a story about her disastrous Friday date with a tall dreamy money-dressed man who took her to a French restaurant and talked only about himself, using it to say a man who does not listen at dinner will not take care of you elsewhere. Vee tells Randi about Pace’s dinner: the unglamorous Middle Eastern restaurant, unreadable menu, his “May I choose?”, how she let him order, how the whole night went soft, and how she stopped watching herself. Randi repeats that Vee let him choose and liked it; Vee admits yes, then tries to minimize it as just not having to be in charge. Randi lets her have that without pushing further. Vee continues, describing liking Pace’s excited voice about a bridge, comparing it to a little boy talking about dinosaurs, then proudly says he was not six years old when she kissed him: she kissed him after two days, though she never does that. Randi names it as Vee kissing him because she couldn’t not, because of him; Vee accepts the smaller truer version. Brooke and a taller silent polished woman arrive. Brooke warmly says they missed Randi Thursday, making Vee aware of Randi’s separate sorority life. Brooke asks who Vee is. Vee shrinks herself to “We have stats together,” trying to leave nothing that could count against Randi. Randi instead says, “Vee’s my dear friend,” and takes Vee’s hand on the table in plain view. Vee feels that this costs Randi something and that Randi spent it on her. Brooke smiles unchanged, says they should all get dinner sometime, meaning nothing and ending the encounter. The taller woman silently assesses Vee and leaves with Brooke. Vee is shaky afterward and apologizes without knowing for what; Randi tells her she was perfect, knocks knees with her, and says she may have to stop taking Vee out in public. Randi’s phone lights; she has to go. Outside, she hugs Vee, smelling cold-citrus, says “Love you, girl,” and leaves radiant. Vee walks home with her empty pink cup through the gold afternoon, feeling not like something the room could look at but picked.

**Running memory — how I feel:**  
Pace is intensely attractive and increasingly alarming. I believe his care, patience, erotic skill, intellectual generosity, social tact, and emotional restraint. He is almost impossibly good at giving someone the kind of attention that makes them feel safe and real. “May I Choose” made him even more seductive: the text with room in it, the showering/shaving/dressing, Christine’s warmth, the unfamiliar meal, the hand-holding, and especially his refusal to press after Vee stated a kissing rule all made him feel like a man who knows how to let a woman choose. “Off Six-Fourteen” confirms that Vee did choose the kiss and that Pace did not push in that moment. “Dear” shows how powerfully Vee has internalized him as someone she can surrender choice to and still feel safe: “May I choose?” has become a cherished phrase, and she tells Randi she stopped tracking herself. But I trust him less because I know he has helped stage Vee’s selection and entrance without her knowledge, and because he knows when something is unfair and sometimes does it anyway. His kindness to Vee feels real; the hidden frame around it remains the danger. The hiking invitation and road past gravel pull Vee toward his private world, which excites and worries me.

Randi has my sympathy, fascination, desire, admiration, and suspicion. She is not passive; she wants Vee, chose Vee, and keeps actively moving this forward. Her warmth toward Vee feels real, but it is not innocent. In “The Long Way,” her teasing encouragement was funny and intimate, but “He wants to see you naked” was chilling because she knows more than Vee does. She was absent in “Water Wings,” “May I Choose,” and “Off Six-Fourteen,” making me wonder what she felt while Pace deepened intimacy with Vee. “Dear” brings her back as both tender and deceptive. Her “Paul?” fake-forgetting of Pace’s name and the alleged bad-date story feel like cover and manipulation. She draws out Vee’s surrender to Pace and helps Vee eroticize/romanticize it. But when Brooke appears, Randi publicly calls Vee “my dear friend” and takes her hand, which feels real and costly. My feeling for Randi is now more conflicted: I believe she cares for Vee, maybe desires her and wants to protect her socially, but she is also lying to Vee’s face.

Vee has my strongest protectiveness. She is intelligent, observant, funny, hungry for attention, and shaped by shame around appetite, clothing, sexuality, display, and being seen. “The Long Way” made her loneliness plain: ordinary kindness felt astonishing, and her overfull froyo cup without her mother’s voice felt like a real little liberation. “Water Wings” deepened her bodily history: she once had synchronized-swimming competence and loved water before her body became a problem. “May I Choose” deepened her appetite and surrender: she let Pace order, ate without watching herself, let him carry the evening, and then chose to bend a rule because he did not push her. “Off Six-Fourteen” made her joy vivid and convincing: she needed Cassie to know the kiss was hers, chosen on purpose, and she was almost dancing with it. “Dear” shows how easily social shame can make her small — pilled cardigan, hanging feet, pink drink, “We have stats together” — and how powerfully Randi’s public claiming repairs it. I am moved by her yeses and frightened by how completely the situation has been prepared around them.

Cassie is the person I most trust near Vee by a wide margin. She is dry, loyal, observant, not easily dazzled, and protective without making herself the hero. Her healer joke gave Vee history; “Water Wings” gave their friendship real emotional authority; “May I Choose” showed her tact when she noticed Vee’s face and did not mock the vulnerable part. “Off Six-Fourteen” strengthened my trust: she was glad for Vee but still asked about alcohol, the man’s name, where he lives, and shoes. “Dear” makes me a little sad for her: she sees Randi reaching for Vee after class, folds away whatever she feels or notices, and leaves Vee to it. She currently reads Pace as “not a boy” but a man, and I believe why she says it from what she knows, though I worry because she does not know the hidden setup.

Christine is a small but warm presence. Her ease with Pace reassures Vee and reassures me a little, while also making Pace more complex: he has ordinary places where people know him fondly.

Brooke remains formidable social power; warm but not safe. In “Dear,” her smile stays warm while taking Vee’s measure, and her “We should all get dinner sometime” is socially lovely but empty. Sloane is observant/classifying; Kinsey is eager and socially pressuring. The unnamed taller woman with Brooke in “Dear” feels like another polished social judge, silent and assessing. Randi’s mother and Vee’s mother both loom as sources of inherited shame and restriction, especially around food, bodies, need, sexuality, and respectability.

Overall mood: the trap has become tender, romantic, social, and now publicly witnessed. Vee’s yeses are getting more beautiful and more embodied — yes to froyo, yes to water, yes to food, yes to Pace choosing, yes to making an exception, yes to hiking, yes to Randi’s “dear friend.” Every yes still feels freely given, and that is exactly why I am worried.
