## Reader reaction

This chapter makes me feel the intoxicating part of Randi’s attention much more strongly again, and also makes me feel how socially dangerous it is for Vee to want it. I felt Vee’s buoyancy at the start — she has Pace and Randi both attending to her, and the world really does seem to have color in it now — but the smoothie shop immediately tightened my stomach. The room is so bright, cold, female, polished, and measuring. Vee’s cardigan becoming “an apology” hurt. It is such a small, believable humiliation: she was happy until the room taught her she was underdressed.

Vee remains the person I am most tender toward. Her hunger to tell Randi everything is almost childlike, but not stupid; she wants the experience witnessed by someone whose opinion matters to her. I loved the line that she wanted “to put all of it into Randi’s hands,” because that is exactly the risk of her right now: she is handing precious private things to people who make her feel chosen. Her description of Pace choosing the food and making the night go soft confirms that the dinner really did feel freeing from inside her. But I also felt a little loss when Randi reframed the kiss: Vee had proudly told Cassie, and herself, that she made an exception on purpose. Here, with Randi, it becomes “Because of him.” That may be true emotionally, but it is a softer, less self-possessed version of the story. Randi pulls it toward Pace’s power over her, and Vee lets that become the truer sentence.

Randi is dazzling and troubling here. Her affection feels real on the page. The “dear friend” moment landed for me: I believed Vee would feel protected, elevated, publicly claimed. Randi taking her hand in front of Brooke costs something socially, or at least Vee feels that it does, and I felt the charge of that. It is romantic in a sideways way — not confessed, not named, but weighted. At the same time, I am more suspicious of Randi than Vee can be. Randi forgetting Pace’s name as “Paul?” felt off to me because I know she is secretly his lover. On the surface it reads as airy Randi performance, but because of what I carry, it feels like an active disguise. Her invented disaster date also made me uneasy. Maybe she really had some other date; but from what I know, it feels like cover story, camouflage, and maybe a way to compare Pace favorably without admitting she knows him. The lie has charm, but it is still a lie-shaped charm.

Pace is absent in person but very present erotically. Vee’s account keeps making him more seductive: “May I choose?” has become almost a spell, and her memory of his voice going boyish about a bridge adds another appealing texture to him — competent man, tender listener, excited child. The erotic peak, for me, is not physical contact in the chapter but Vee saying she stopped watching herself. That is intimate and dangerous. The second peak is Randi’s hand over Vee’s on the white table: public, cool, damp, weighted. The chapter’s heat is mostly triangular charge rather than explicit sex.

Cassie worries me here by absence. She sees something, folds it away, and leaves. I felt a pang that Vee does not watch her go because Randi has her by the wrist. Cassie has been the safest presence, and this chapter makes it easy to understand how someone safe can be gently edged out without anyone feeling cruel. Vee reassures herself that it is fine to have “your own people,” but “underneath it something turned over, small” is exactly the kind of line that made me feel a quiet warning.

Brooke is frightening in the most socially polished way. Nothing she does is openly hostile. She smiles, says they missed Randi, asks who Vee is, suggests dinner. But Vee experiences being measured, and I believe her. Brooke’s warmth has weight; it makes the room remember itself. The older silent woman is also uncomfortable — not because she does anything dramatic, but because her gaze is “the look you turn on a thing once you are done turning it over.” That is a hard, objectifying sentence.

Trust vs. suspicion: Vee has no real reason, from her knowledge, to distrust Randi here. Randi publicly chooses her, so Vee’s trust deepens. I, however, feel more suspicion because Randi’s entire social performance in the smoothie shop is built over secrets: Pace, the prior selection of Vee, and possibly the invented love-life catastrophe. What is off is not that Randi is unkind. It is that her kindness is both real and strategic.

The chapter title, “Dear,” lands directly on “Vee’s my dear friend.” It points to affection as a public word, but also to cost: dear as beloved, dear as expensive. That doubled meaning worked for me. Randi spends something by saying it, or at least Vee receives it that way. The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, feels present in the drinks, the smoothie counter, the high table, and all this sweet/cold consumption, but more importantly in the caution around eating with people whose social world you do not understand. The tagline — “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” — feels sharper because Vee keeps saying yes to being chosen: yes to Pace choosing, yes to Randi receiving the story, yes to being dear friend in public.

Friction: the only moment I slightly questioned was how perfectly Randi’s “catastrophe” date tees up Pace’s virtues. The line “a man who can't be bothered to listen to you across a dinner table, honey, is not going to bother taking care of you anywhere else” is delicious and plausible as Randi, but it also felt very convenient because it gives Vee exactly the interpretive frame for Pace. That may be Randi’s social brilliance rather than authorial pressure, but I noticed it.

What I want next: I badly want Cassie’s read on this, because she saw something and left. I also want to know what Brooke makes of Vee and whether Randi will have consequences for calling her dear friend. I dread Vee losing Cassie as her anchor while she is being pulled into Randi’s and Pace’s warmer, brighter world. Guess from here: the next pressure may not be sexual first, but social — Vee being drawn into Randi’s sorority orbit, where being chosen comes with being watched.

  Randi tells a story about a disastrous Friday date with a tall, dreamy, money-dressed man who drove her to a candlelit French place and talked only about himself, Switzerland, and lacrosse; she says a man who will not listen across a dinner table will not take care of you anywhere else. Vee laughs and tells Randi about Pace’s dinner: the tiny Middle Eastern restaurant, not reading the menu, Pace asking “May I choose?”, and the relief of handing it over and no longer keeping track of herself. Randi says slowly that Vee let him choose for her and liked it; Vee admits yes, then minimizes it as just nice not to be in charge. Randi warmly lets her have that without pressing.

  Vee keeps telling the story because she wants to put it into Randi’s hands. She describes Pace’s boyish excited voice when he talked about a bridge. Then she says, proud before she plans to be, that she kissed him after only two days. Randi turns this over tenderly and says Vee did it because she could not not, because of him. Vee had meant to say she made an exception, but instead says yes, because of him. Randi delightedly calls her a little minx.

  Brooke enters with an older polished woman. Brooke warmly greets Randi and says they missed her Thursday, making Vee aware of Randi’s private sorority week and the life Vee is not part of. Brooke asks who Randi’s new friend is. Vee shrinks herself down to “We have stats together,” trying to be no one’s business and leave nothing to hold against Randi. Randi instead says, “Vee’s my dear friend,” taking Vee’s hand on the white table in plain view. Vee feels a warmth with weight: she cannot name it, but she senses that saying this cost Randi something socially and that Randi spent it on her. Brooke’s smile takes in the dear friend and the hand, suggests they should all get dinner sometime, and ends the exchange. The older woman silently gives Vee one last friendly, measuring look before leaving.

  Afterward Vee is shaky and apologizes without knowing why, but Randi tells her she was perfect and jokes she will have to stop taking Vee out in public. This answers the fright for Vee. Randi soon has to leave when her phone lights up. Outside, she hugs Vee quickly and completely, says “Love you, girl,” and goes back into the bright matched crowd. Vee walks home with her empty pink cup through the gold afternoon. She no longer feels like a thing the room could look at. She feels picked, and carries that feeling home.

- **How I feel:**
  - Pace remains intensely attractive because of his patience, competence, tenderness, sexual skill, adult steadiness, and attention. His letting Vee’s no-kissing rule go without pressure makes him even more seductive in her account, and her memory of his boyish excitement about a bridge makes him warmer and more human. My wariness remains extremely high because the very behaviors that make him feel safe also make surrender feel natural, and I know he and Randi selected Vee before she knew there was any game. The hiking invitation still moves Vee toward his private world off six-fourteen, which feels exciting and ominous.
  - Randi is more seductive and more alarming after “Dear.” Her public “dear friend” claim feels emotionally real and generous, and I understand completely why Vee feels chosen by it. But Randi’s performance around Pace — forgetting his name, inventing or at least deploying a contrasting bad-date story, drawing Vee’s agency into “because of him” — keeps her morally complicated. Her warmth may be genuine, but it is not transparent.
  - Vee is deeply sympathetic and vulnerable. I like her intelligence, humor, bodily appetite, openness, shame, and capacity to feel pleasure when not mocked or rushed. I ache for her because she is being genuinely lit up by both Pace and Randi, and because the feeling of being picked is so powerful that it can make her ignore the small thing turning over underneath.
  - Cassie feels trustworthy, blunt, funny, and increasingly perceptive. Her brief look in “Dear” matters to me; she seems to notice the pull between Randi and Vee and quietly step aside, but not because she is unaware. She remains one of the safest presences in the book, and I worry about Vee drifting away from her.
  - Christine feels warm and grounding; her affection for Pace makes him seem socially rooted and known beyond his private house and campus appearances.
  - Brooke feels fascinating and dangerous: warm, intelligent, socially graceful, and powerful enough to interrogate or measure without seeming to. In “Dear,” her smile makes Vee feel assessed while never becoming openly cruel.
  - The unnamed older woman with Brooke feels socially powerful in a quieter way: watchful, finished, and able to make Vee feel turned over like an object without saying a word.
  - Sloane feels like ambient social pressure, quick to laugh and classify.

  - The unnamed boys in the bleachers remain ordinary ambient misogyny: crude, entitled, and socially self-rewarding.
  - The overall mood I carry forward is warm, giddy, socially anxious, and increasingly triangulated. Vee’s joy at being chosen by Pace has now been doubled by being publicly chosen by Randi. That feels wonderful and dangerous. The cover tagline feels even more painful because Vee’s yeses and leanings are so genuinely hers, while the hidden context around them keeps thickening.
