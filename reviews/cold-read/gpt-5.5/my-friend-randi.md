## Reader reaction

I felt this chapter in my chest before I felt it anywhere else: the relief of Vee reaching Randi’s room, the door shutting, the arms around her, and then the slow unease of realizing that Randi’s comfort is not just comfort. It works on Vee. It works fast. It is loving, observant, physically intimate, and it steers.

Vee arrives absolutely wrecked: “swollen eyes,” “plane-creased clothes,” still carrying Pace’s doorstep rejection in her body. I feel horribly tender toward her here. The front hall is painful because it turns her private devastation into a social fact — “the stats friend, crying in the front hall on a Friday” — and I wanted her out of those eyes as badly as she did. When Randi gets her upstairs, I trust the immediate instinct: she protects Vee from being seen. She does the exact thing Vee needs: “Up the stairs… Come on. Up.” That felt good to me.

And the hug is charged. Not overtly sexual, but physically alive: Vee breathing in Randi’s neck, “bitter orange, green at the edge, nothing sweet,” taking another breath and not wanting to stop. Randi holding the hug “past where a hug stops” lands as more than ordinary friendship, or at least friendship with the erotic edge the book has been quietly loading into their bond. I don’t think Vee consciously names it that way, but my body noticed.

My feeling about Randi moved a lot in this chapter. She is dazzlingly good at care. She does not panic; she does not make Vee explain in the hallway; she does not apologize for the mess when Vee needs a bed more than Randi needs polish. She listens, and she is smart enough to separate Vee’s invented Sheri from the real facts. “Fear needs a face to point at, so you gave it one” is genuinely kind and clarifying. I believed the comfort.

But then the steering begins, and that is where my suspicion rises — not from the jacket alone, but from the page. Randi does not simply help Vee calm down; she builds an interpretation, then a plan. She tells Vee Pace is not done with her, tells her she “blindsided him,” tells her “Words are the whole war,” and then guides her toward showing up naked under a coat. The line that made me feel the manipulation most sharply is: “her voice dropped, certain, and Vee felt it settle over her like a thing being decided for her and by her at once.” That is exactly the trap-feeling: Vee is choosing, but the choice is being warmed and shaped around her.

Randi’s argument is seductive because it uses true things. Pace did heat the house. He did cook. He did put away his phone. He did want her, as I know from “The Usual.” But Randi also softens the fact that Pace just treated Vee with brutal coldness at the door. She reframes almost everything in his favor and puts the burden of repair on Vee: “You have to show him you don’t want to fight.” Some of that may be fair — Vee did arrive unannounced and wounded — but the speed with which Randi turns Vee’s humiliation into an erotic peace offering made me uneasy.

The erotic charge peaks, for me, not in any explicit act but in the conception of the plan: the long coat, “nothing under it,” the shame-word “slutty,” and Randi walking her through the humiliation into bravery. That is hot in a very dangerous way. Vee’s body responds exactly as it has before: “the wanting coming up hand in hand with the mortification and sharper for it.” That sentence feels like one of the book’s engines. Shame becomes heat; heat becomes choice; choice becomes vulnerability. It works erotically on me, but not cleanly. The charge is braided with dread.

I also felt a weird little ache when Randi tells the New Year story about John. On the surface it is charming — the lift line, fake marriage, children named by the top of the lift, the blue dress “you buy to tell your daughter about someday.” Vee’s reaction is heartbreaking because she hears exactly what she lacks: “a man who’d build the whole future out loud in the first hour and not be afraid of one word in it.” That line made Pace feel even more painfully withheld by contrast. John is almost too storybook-perfect, and then Randi breaks the spell with “Everywhere but the bedroom, and the bedroom’s the room I actually live in.” It made me laugh with Vee, but it also reminded me that Randi’s deepest allegiance is to erotic truth, not social fairytale.

I am suspicious of Randi now in a much sharper way. Not because I think she hates Vee — I don’t. She seems genuinely attached, maybe even hungry for her. But she is far too good at knowing where Vee’s shame lives and how to turn the key. “Say it” — making Vee say “slutty” — could be liberating, and maybe it is. But it is also Randi taking charge of Vee’s inner gate. The jacket’s “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap” feels very present here: Vee is going to do this willingly. That is exactly what frightens me.

Pace is absent but powerfully in the room. My feeling about him is still torn. Randi’s reading of him matches some of what I know: he walls off when he cannot say the thing; he is not casually done with Vee; he had prepared that last night with care. But he still hurt her. Randi makes him sound like “a boy holding himself still so it won’t show how much you got him,” and I partly believe it, but I also feel protective of Vee against having to eroticize his failure to speak.

Brooke is a small warmth here. She clocks Vee’s distress and gives kindness without prying. The unnamed blonde at the door is just a quick social threshold, but even that moment stings because Vee is entering Randi’s world as a visible crisis.

The chapter title, “My Friend Randi,” lands almost painfully. It sounds simple, even innocent, like Vee’s category for her: friend, rescuer, the one with the shut door and the right shoes. But after reading, the title feels unstable. Randi is absolutely acting as Vee’s friend — holding her, protecting her, helping her laugh — and also doing something more intimate and more directive than friendship usually admits. The title did not give anything away beforehand, but afterward it feels like Vee clinging to the safest possible name for someone who may not be safe.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, still feels like a warning about supping with danger, needing distance from someone or something seductive. The tagline burns brighter after this chapter. Vee’s yes is forming right in front of me: she will go tonight, showered, dressed, bare under the coat, in Randi’s heels. It will be freely chosen. And yet the chapter has shown me exactly how a free yes can be prepared inside someone else’s warmth.

What I want next: I desperately want to see the scene at Pace’s door tonight, because the chapter points straight at it. I dread it too. My guess from here is that Pace will not simply reject her; the gesture is too loaded, too attuned to him and to Vee’s shame/desire, not to break something open. But I am afraid of what it will teach Vee: that if words fail, she should offer her body harder, more bravely, more completely. I also want to know whether Randi gave those shoes as care, as possession, as authorship, or all of that at once.

**Running memory — story so far:**  
The book opened under the cover promise that Miranda/Randi and Pace secretly chose Vivienne/Vee as a third in their bed without telling her, and that every yes would be freely given but trapped by what she did not know. The durable emotional spine so far is Vee becoming split between Pace’s private erotic tenderness and Randi’s bright social/emotional intimacy, while the full truth remains withheld from her.

Vee and Pace have built an intense erotic and domestic bond around his house, his quiet attention, his cooking, consent, and the feeling that he sees her wanting without shaming it. Pace asks before taking and takes only what she gives; Vee experiences his care as bodily and transformative. Food has become one of their languages: garlic, oil, fra diavolo, aftersex warmth, and the sense that being fed by him means being understood. Vee has also become attached to Randi as a friend who listens, draws out shame, calls her gorgeous, and offers a more public, glamorous kind of chosen-ness.

A rupture came in “Believe Me,” when Vee asked Pace what she is to him and his answer failed her. He told her she was the most important thing in his life, but to Vee that felt like half of something, not a declaration or name. She left, and over the holiday break there has been no new message from him as far as Vee knows.

In “Fairytale,” Randi was in Gstaad amid wealth, chalet glass, mountains, and curated holiday beauty. Vee texted her warmly for New Year’s, and Randi replied with the glittering surface: chalet, mountains, never coming home, missing Vee’s face. Randi’s family money and Miranda Holdings are morally shadowed by what Vee saw in PPP-related data, but Randi’s public life still looks enviable and bright.

After Randi’s New Year’s fairytale, the same night was seen from Vee’s side in Ohio. Vee was home for the holidays in her cold childhood bedroom, surrounded by preserved evidence of old Vivienne: ribbons, synchronized-swimming trophies, clarinet, old friends, familiar family routines. Her parents are loving and ordinary — hospital shifts, shop mornings, careful heat, Christmas food, Wheel of Fortune — but their daily questions about Pace land on the shut door of a relationship she cannot explain.

Vee heard nothing from Pace since leaving his house after asking what she is to him. She checked old texts, started to write and deleted it, remembering that no small message can bring back the answer she needs. She looked through pictures, including the induction long-step photo, and reflected that she sent it whole to Pace but cropped it for her parents. She then looked at Randi’s Gstaad posts. Though she knows from “The Outlier” that Randi’s family money is tied to Miranda Holdings and likely PPP harm, she envied the mountains and the visible life anyway.

Vee repeats the phrase quietly, feeling the ache of a story already shaped. Randi describes the ball: marble floor, Christmas trees in silver and white against black, the room lit like the inside of a bell. John could dance beautifully and led so effortlessly that Randi only had to let herself be led. Vee is drawn into the fairytale and asks what happened next. Randi, usually explicit about men, stops and says only that it got late and they went up, looking down at her hands. Vee reads the quiet as discretion. When Vee asks if Randi will see him again and says he sounds perfect, Randi smiles again and says John was perfect, a masterpiece, everywhere but the bedroom — “and the bedroom’s the room I actually live in.” Vee laughs for real for the first time that day.

Randi and Vee hug again, as long as the first, both still frayed from separate bad days. When it breaks, Randi kisses Vee goodbye as an unremarkable habit, “only what they did.” Vee goes down through the front hall and out into the cold with a plan where the morning’s wreckage had been, and drives to the dorm to sleep. Tonight she intends to shower, dress, “be the thing,” and go to Pace.

**Running memory — how I feel now:**  
Vee feels more vulnerable than ever, but no longer inert. She has been humiliated at Pace’s door, emptied in the car, then gathered and redirected by Randi. Her jealousy of Sheri has been partly exposed as a fear-built fiction, which is a relief, but her longing for Pace remains desperate. I feel protective of her because the plan she now carries is both erotically brave and emotionally risky: she is preparing to offer her body as the answer to a wound made by missing words.

Pace’s silence and coldness still worry me deeply. I believe, because of “The Usual” and the break in his face at “Peter,” that he wants and misses Vee. Randi’s reconstruction of his care — warmed house, chicken, wine, phone away — matches the Pace I have seen. But he still closed the door, used a stranger’s phrase, and let her leave destroyed. I trust his feeling and his bodily tenderness; I mistrust his avoidance and his capacity to turn care into harm by withholding speech.

Randi is now the most complicated presence for me. She is lifesaving: she protects Vee from the front hall, holds her, listens, clarifies the invented Sheri, makes her laugh, and gives her a plan when she is wrecked. She is also dangerous in the exact way the book’s cover warns about: she helps Vee choose freely while shaping the choice with warmth, certainty, shame-work, and erotic imagination. Her physical intimacy with Vee feels increasingly charged. Her black heels going with Vee to Pace feel like care and authorship both.

Sheri feels less like a romantic threat and more like a real woman Vee has not yet been able to see. Randi’s questioning confirms that Vee’s blonde/model version of Sheri is invented. From Pace’s side I still remember Sheri as blunt, funny, wounded, and truth-telling, not a seductress stealing Vee’s place.

Brooke feels kind and peripheral: warm enough to make the front hall less cruel, but not inside the private story. The unnamed blonde door-opener adds to the feeling of Randi’s house as a social world where Vee can become a visible incident.

John is charming but possibly only a fairytale contrast for now: he gives Randi easy future-talk, public glamour, beautiful dancing, and the romantic shape Vee longs for, but fails in the bedroom, which for Randi matters most.

The overall mood moving forward is charged, anxious, and pulled taut. The cold doorstep rejection has turned into a planned erotic return. The trap feels closer than ever: Vee is going to say yes to the plan with her whole body, and the fact that she wants it does not make me feel safe.
