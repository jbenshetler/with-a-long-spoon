## Reader reaction

This chapter hurts in a quieter, older register. After the cold threshold with Vee and Pace, I expected the next move might be aftermath; instead I get Randi inside the approved life, the fairytale life, and watch it fail to touch her. The first feeling is bodily: glare off snow and glass, cold under ski clothes, champagne, white tie, sleigh bells, fur, the beautiful story assembling itself — and then the hotel room where she is warm only where she touches John and cold everywhere else. That image lands hard because it echoes Vee with Pace’s shirt and Pace’s house: borrowed warmth is not the same as being reached.

Randi moves for me here. She is still deceptive, still dangerous around Vee, still choosing the path that avoids a real confession. But I feel much more compassion for her. Her revulsion at her father, her mother’s brittle perfection, the table where invented boys are safer than real desire — all of that makes her poise feel less like mere polish and more like survival. John is attractive and competent and almost absurdly perfect: huge, rich, funny, direct, a better skier, a better ballroom lead, good in bed, the exact approved future. I do not dislike him; if anything, the absence of a flaw makes the failure more convincing. He is not cruel. He just never finds her.

Trust versus suspicion: Randi’s warmth toward Vee feels more plainly genuine now, but her chosen method feels worse. She knows she wants Vee. She knows saying it would change the friendship. So she chooses the “other way,” the way that never asks her to say the word and never makes Vee decide. That is frighteningly close to Pace’s “Words are cheap” refusal from the last chapter, except Randi names the avoidance to herself. The page earns my suspicion: she is preserving aliveness by withholding truth.

Erotic charge is strong but strangely divided. The skiing and dancing are more charged than the sex with John. The peak for me is the dance, where she lets him lead and thinks easier is better, and the kiss at midnight where Pace intrudes for one second. The hotel sex is deliberately less hot on the page because Randi’s body can enjoy it while the deeper self stays untouched. The most erotic line may actually be comparative: “Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best.”

Friction: very little. The fairytale could have tipped into too-perfect, but the chapter seems to know that and uses perfection as the point. The one line that feels like a deliberate key is “This was the best the acceptable world could do for her.” It is slightly summative, but I bought it because the whole scene has demonstrated it.

The title, “Fairytale,” is exact and sad. It means the chalet, white-tie ball, blue dress, sleigh, approved prince, future-daughter story — and also the falseness of a story that looks perfect from outside and leaves the real girl cold. The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, now feels even more about appetite and caution: Randi can consume the fairytale meal/sex/social future and still starve; Vee’s text offers plain warmth from Ohio, and Randi gives back the glittering version instead of the truth. The tagline keeps tightening: every yes here is freely given too — to the skiing, dancing, sex, lie-by-truth, silence — and the trap is that the safest yes protects the hidden self from being known.

What I want next: I want to know whether this is happening while Vee is cold after leaving Pace, or just before/around New Year after Thanksgiving. Guess from here: Randi will come back from this more certain that Pace and Vee are the only people who reach her, and therefore more dangerous, not less. I dread her returning to Vee with renewed hunger and still no confession.
