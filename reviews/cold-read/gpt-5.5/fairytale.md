## Reader reaction

This chapter lands on me like a cold glittering bruise. It’s beautiful, and I feel the seduction of it — the snow, the lift, the ball, the pale blue dress, the sleigh — but the stronger feeling by the end is loneliness. Randi gets exactly the fairytale version of the life she has been trained to want, and it leaves her colder than before.

My feeling for Randi moves a lot here. I already felt she was warm, desirous, dangerous, managing more than she said. This chapter makes her lonelier and more exposed to me. The breakfast with her parents is awful in a quiet, rich way: her father hearty and adored but morally/physically heavy in the room, her mother “all edges and points,” living on black coffee and egg whites. John is attractive in the moment, and the chapter lets me feel why. He is tall, bold, socially fluent, good at everything, able to make a table appear, a sleigh appear, a whole night become story. Randi likes not being asked — “She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked” — and I believe that as her experience, but because I’ve been reading so much Pace/Vee consent language, it rings dangerously. Not as assault on the page; she clearly goes with him. But as a contrast, it feels loud. John assumes. Pace asks. Randi thinks she wants assumed. Then afterward, the assumption has delivered pleasure but not contact.

Pace is not present, but he is everywhere as the measuring instrument. The chapter uses John to show me what Pace is not. “Pace had never assumed anything in his life. Pace made her say it, out loud, every time.” That line hits differently after Vee just left Pace because he would not say the word. Pace asks for erotic consent; Pace does not give relational naming. Vee also is not present physically, but when she appears by text I felt my chest hurt. And Randi lies by telling the truth: “it’s honestly a fairytale out here.” Every word true, “None of it the thing.” That is such a strong summary of the chapter and of so many of these relationships: true surfaces hiding the thing.

Trust versus suspicion: this chapter deepens my suspicion of Randi, but tenderly. I don’t think she is fake in her feeling for Vee. I think this chapter proves the feeling is real and costly. But her decision at the mirror is chilling because it is explicit: she will not tell Vee; she will use “the other way,” the way that “never asked Randi to say the word and never made Vee decide what to do with it.” That is exactly the cover-trap feeling. She wants to preserve Vee’s freedom from a decision by withholding the information that would let Vee decide. The text earns that suspicion directly; I’m not importing it. Randi chooses silence because speech might change the brunches.

Erotically, the chapter works most for me in the skiing and dancing, less in the actual sex. The skiing has the strongest physical charge: speed, competence, rivalry, cold, sweat, her thighs burning, him staying just off her shoulder. The dance peaks too: “there was nothing for her to do but let him,” and “her body went where it was sent.” That surrender has heat. The sex itself is mostly aftermath, and that’s right for the chapter: the point is not how hot John is in bed, but how complete pleasure can still mean nothing. The lipstick ring on him is vivid and intimate, but it feels hollow: “Her body had spent itself on him and she remained unmoved.”

The friction I felt is small and maybe intentional: John is almost too perfectly storybook — law school in New Haven, white tie, chef with a star, sleigh, faultless room. But the chapter is called “Fairytale,” so that excess feels like the point. If anything, the line “This was the best the acceptable world could do for her” is a little clarifying in a way I could feel the author’s hand on, but I also needed it emotionally. It names the emptiness.

The chapter title, “Fairytale,” at first seems to promise romance, snow, ball, dress, sleigh — and it gives all of that. But by the end it means the lie Randi can inhabit and send back to Vee. The fairytale is true as scenery and false as salvation. It also points at the story Randi imagined telling “to a daughter she didn’t have yet”: “the year I met your father, the ball, the snow.” That imagined daughter hurts, because the life appears in full and then dies in the hotel room.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, keeps gathering food and caution. Here there is breakfast restraint, champagne, star-chef illusions, venison, sour cherry, white truffle — another world of feeding, but it doesn’t nourish her. And the tagline, “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap,” feels newly attached to Randi too, not just Vee. Randi says yes freely to John, to the story, to not telling Vee, to “the other way.” The trap is not coercion in the blunt sense; it is the set of conditions that make the yes seem like the safest available shape.

What I want next: I badly want to know where Vee went after leaving Pace, and whether she texts Randi or Cassie or no one. This chapter answers something about Randi’s desire, though: she knows what Vee is to her. My guess from here is that Randi will continue to present warmth without confession, especially because Vee is now vulnerable after Pace’s non-answer. I dread Randi becoming the person Vee runs to while hiding the fact that she is also part of the trap.

Vee is holding erotic secrets, moral secrets, family-safe edits of her own happiness, Pace’s missing declarations, and the fear that she belongs to him more fully than he belongs to her. Randi is holding conscious desire for Vee and choosing not to speak it. “Bare” made the fear quiet and severe; “Believe Me” made it spoken and costly; “Fairytale” shows that even the perfect approved story can be another beautiful way not to say the thing.
