## Reader reaction

I came into this chapter still raw from Vee walking out into the cold, and the turn into Randi’s snow-bright memory hit like a sideways mirror. At first it is all surface competence and disgust: the father with “his adored daughter,” the mother made of labels and edges, Randi ordering the tiny disciplined breakfast and inventing acceptable boys because “the invented ones were safer than the real ones.” I felt immediately protective of her and also a little chilled by how fluent she is in falsehood here. Not malicious falsehood, survival falsehood. The table teaches it.

John works on me at first. I hate that he does, because the chapter slowly proves why that matters. His entrance is outrageous and charming, and I did feel the lift of it: “Play along,” the fake wife, the made-up children, the ease of two beautiful people improvising a life in public. The skiing is genuinely sexy, not because anyone is naked but because he has power and does not spend it showing off. “He could have left her behind whenever he wanted, and didn’t” landed. That kind of restraint has heat. For a while I understood exactly why Randi lets the story form around him.

The fairytale part really did sweep me up: the champagne, the second-shop dress, the white-tie ball, the Byron line he can’t finish, the sleigh. It is almost too perfect, but in a way that belongs to Randi’s own hunger for the acceptable version of romance. The blue dress waking up her eyes is a gorgeous, aching detail because she knows how to be seen beautifully, and she wants this to become the story she can tell a daughter. That imagined daughter hurt me. It showed how badly she wanted a life to click into place, not just a night.

But the erotic charge goes cold in the hotel, and I believed that cold completely. The sex is good; the page insists on that without pretending good means enough. “No more than a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon” is devastating because it makes pleasure real and empty at once. Her getting up to repair her face while John sleeps is the chapter’s cruelest clarity. He did nothing wrong that I can see. That is the point that makes it worse. “This was the best the acceptable world could do for her.” That line made the title collapse inward: the fairytale is real, and it still cannot touch her.

My feeling about Randi moved a lot here. I already knew she loved Vee and wanted her, and I already knew she was managing Vee, but this chapter makes the fear under the management much more human. I still don’t trust what she is doing. “Let Vee arrive at it on her own” has the same dangerous shape as Pace’s slowness around Vee: it sounds respectful and is also self-serving, because Randi keeps control by keeping the word covered. But I understand her cowardice more intimately now. She is not withholding because the feeling is shallow. She is withholding because the feeling is the first thing she cannot scenario-plan safely.

Vee’s text at the end broke me a little. From Ohio, still kind, still offering “or not,” still imagining Randi inside a beautiful life and sending warmth into it. And Randi gives back “every word of it true. None of it the thing.” That is the whole chapter to me: truth used as cover. The title “Fairytale” did not give away the plot exactly, but it pointed me toward enchantment, and the chapter then made enchantment feel like a decorated cage. The cover board, “WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION,” keeps feeling more pointed: politeness, invitation, distance, danger at a civilized table. “Fairytale” makes me expect that the beautiful invitation is not safety.
