## Reader reaction

I came into this chapter raw from Vee’s cold room and that awful no-answer ache, and the first surprise is how much it steadies me to be with Pace somewhere ordinary and ugly-warm. This diner works on me immediately: “the warm griddle-smelling dark,” the dead jukebox, the ambered tabletop, the county road lost outside. It’s not glamorous like Randi’s Gstaad, and not curated like Pace’s house, but it feels honest. A place where nobody performs too hard, except maybe Sheri with that makeup “worn like she’d dare you to mention it.”

Pace lands more human here, and less safely godlike. That matters. Seeing him choose “he would not bring her down today” makes me suspicious at first, because I don’t know what he carried in from the truck and “long practice at deciding a thing could keep” sounds like the same locked-room habit that is hurting Vee. But then the chapter lets me sit with him rather than only judge him through Vee’s wound. He misses her. He wants her. He is not indifferent. And still, when Sheri says, “Then not saying it matters more to you than having her,” it hits because she is right from where I’m sitting too. He can make every warm room in the world for Vee and still leave her outside the one word she needs.

Sheri is wonderful to read here. Blunt without being cruel, funny without being decorative, hungry without apology. I trust her more after this chapter. The way she eats the double burger and steals his shake and then his fries is comic and bodily and alive, but she also cuts cleanly through Pace’s self-justification. “Don’t bullshit me, Pace” felt exactly right. She likes him enough not to flatter him. That is such a relief in a book where so many people want something from each other in charged, hidden ways. Sheri wants a burger, the truth, and maybe not to be pitied.

The Paige piece moved me more than I expected. It’s quick, but not thin: “made these little prints, birds mostly. Had a laugh way too big for her.” I felt the little life of that relationship and its loss. And the Christmas pain around family lands hard after Vee’s room and Randi’s parents. Everyone is being measured against home right now, and almost nobody fits. Pace’s line, “People who’ve never lost anything think grief is a choice,” is beautiful, but Sheri’s “Don’t waste the good stuff on me” keeps it from turning too polished. She refuses to become a tender object for him to be wise over.

Erotically, this is not a sex chapter, but it is charged because everyone is talking around bodies they know or imagine. Sheri’s comments about Vee are crude and funny and a little startling: “Hell, that backside,” “the one with the big tits,” “those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you.” I didn’t feel offended by it exactly; it reads like Sheri’s language, not the book sneering at Vee. But it also reminds me how visible Vee’s body is to everyone, even women who only saw her for “maybe four seconds.” Pace’s blush and his “Sheri” made me grin, because he is embarrassed and still unable to deny the truth of it.

The pull of the chapter peaks for me when Pace tries to explain Vee: “The air’s different when she’s in the room. It — crackles.” That is the closest he gets to the real thing, and I believe it. Then he says she understands “the philosophy” of his food and Sheri punctures him beautifully. I laughed, but I also felt the tenderness under the absurdity. Vee does see what his care is for. That is why this hurts so much. She can read the meal and the heat and the hands, but she cannot read the silence.

My suspicion of Pace is sharper but more specific now. I do not think he is using Vee casually. I do not think he is bored, or done, or looking for someone else. The text earns the opposite. But I do feel that his refusal has a weight he is not explaining, and his habit of answering adjacent questions is becoming unbearable. “I answered the one next to it” is basically what he does with Vee too. He gives true things that are not the asked thing. That is not nothing; it is also not enough.

The chapter title, “The Usual,” first means the diner order, the practiced ritual between Pace and Sheri, the place where the waitress doesn’t have to ask. But by the end it points at habits more broadly: Pace’s usual way of giving actions instead of words, Sheri’s usual grief being misunderstood, the usual Christmas wound, the usual avoidance. It did not give anything away, but it quietly tightened the chapter after the fact.

*A Polite Invitation* still feels more ominous now because Pace’s politeness, his careful room-leaving, is not solving everything. Sometimes an invitation is not enough if the person invited needs to know what room she is entering. *With a Long Spoon* keeps feeling like desire handled at a distance, or danger approached with manners. The blurb’s closing beat, “Every yes was freely given. That was the problem,” is glowing brighter here: Vee has said yes to so much, but she is now asking what yes has made her. I want the next chapter badly. My guess from here is that Pace will not be able to solve this by doing more of his usual. I dread him trying anyway.
