This one hurt in a clean, awful way. I was warm with Vee at first, almost giddy with her, because the lesson is so perfectly him: “If you want to, I’ll teach you.” That line lands right in the established erotic grammar of the book. He offers, she chooses, and the teaching itself becomes intimate before it is sexual. His hand at her back, the frame, “Give me something to push back against,” the insistence that “Being jelly doesn’t work” — it is all clothed, public, instructional, and still charged because it is about her learning how to be led without disappearing.

The peak for me is not the bar, honestly. It is the concrete pad under the field lights, the boom box, the ridiculousness of it, and then the sudden click: “The pattern came through his body before it arrived in her feet.” That swept me up. It is so bodily and so trusting. She stops guessing. She lets information come through contact. It feels like sex without being sex, and like the emotional shape of what Pace keeps giving her: not taking choice away, but making a structure in which choosing can happen.

Then the Rusty Nail turns the same pleasure poisonous. I felt Vee’s visibility with her, and then I felt the sharp little drop when she realizes Pace belongs to this whole room. The girl’s comment is perfectly calibrated to wound: “Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to.” I don’t think the woman is fully lying, but she knows exactly where to put the knife. My suspicion is not really of Pace here. The chapter earns Vee’s jealousy, but it also shows her misreading him through old shame. The bar woman’s correction matters: “Wasn’t him they were jealous of, honey.” That line made the whole room flip. Vee thought she was the rule and those girls were the exceptions; maybe they saw immediately that she was the exception and hated her for it.

I felt for Pace hard here. His face losing ease, his straight answer — “Yes. Sometimes. When it’s required.” — and then him setting the untouched beer down and leaving: that hurt. He does not defend himself with charm. He does not punish her. He just goes. That made me trust him more in this chapter, not less, though the larger hidden arrangement with Randi still sits under everything like a buried charge. He is good in the scene, and the book lets that goodness stand.

Vee’s self-punishment is painful because I understand every step of it. “Three dates, and she’d spread her legs like a slut” is ugly, but it is ugly in her own inherited voice. The chapter lets me feel how quickly joy can collapse back into the old vocabulary. By the end, when she lies in his washed shirt and realizes she punished him “for being the man she wanted him to be,” I believed the turn. It is not a neat rescue. She still has both things in her: shame and the memory of being led well.
