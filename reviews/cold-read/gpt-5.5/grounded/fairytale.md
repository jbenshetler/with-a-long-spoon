## Reader reaction

I come out of this chapter feeling cold, and not in a dead way: in that sharp, glittering way where everything is beautiful and the beauty is exactly the problem. “Fairytale” gives Randi the version of life that should fit her perfectly, and watching it fail to touch her is painful. The snow, the champagne, the white-tie ball, the sleigh, the handsome eligible man who can ski and dance and kiss and arrange everything: it is so complete it almost feels like a trap made out of wish fulfillment.

Randi moved for me here. I already felt her doubleness, but this chapter makes her loneliness much more naked. Her revulsion at breakfast, the invented boys for her father, the plain white-gold bracelet kept “out of the light” — all of that makes her feel like someone who has been trained to perform ease so well that no one has to notice she is choking. Her parents land horribly without melodrama: the father hearty and possessive in his approval, the mother all “edges and points,” and Randi surviving them by making up acceptable versions of herself.

John is almost unnerving because he is not bad. I don’t feel villain-suspicion of him. He is charming, capable, socially fluent, physically impressive, and he gives her a night that really is cinematic. But he is also all assumption. That moment at the hotel door — “he had never asked her where the night was going” — rang loudly because I have been living inside Pace’s asking. Randi’s own reaction complicates it: “She found, tonight, that she liked not being asked.” I believe that in the moment. I also believe the chapter earns the emptiness afterward. John can lead her, impress her, satisfy her body, and still never find her.

The swoon absolutely worked on me before it curdled. The lift meet-cute is delicious: “What’s your name, my dear wife?” and the instant invention of children is light, quick, sexy. The skiing has real pull because he does not need to prove himself: “He could have left her behind whenever he wanted, and didn’t.” The blue dress is a full fairytale object, and his reaction at the stairs — “both hands coming up and crossing over his heart” — got me. The sleigh in the snow is almost too perfect, but in a way I would have let myself fall for if the chapter wanted me to. It does want me to, briefly. Then it lets me feel how little perfect is worth when nobody underneath is touched.

The erotic charge is present but deliberately hollow. The strongest charge for me is not the sex itself but the lead-up: his competence on the mountain, the dancing, the kiss at midnight, the assumption at the hotel. The sex is summarized through aftermath, and that feels right because Randi herself experiences it as pleasure without meaning: “a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon.” That line is brutal. It makes the body’s satisfaction feel almost humiliatingly insufficient.

Trust versus suspicion: I do not suspect John of some hidden cruelty. I suspect the whole approved world he represents. And I trust Randi’s recognition here, even when I don’t trust her choices. Her decision not to tell Vee is selfish, frightened, and heartbreakingly understandable. “She could have Vee this way and keep the friendship both, as long as the word kept its cover” is the chapter’s most dangerous sentence for me, because it is where tenderness turns into strategy again.

The strongest ache is Pace and Vee entering the room without being there. “Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best” lands hard after the recent chapters, where Pace has failed Vee with words but still listens into her. And Vee, even more: “Something came off Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday that this room could not counterfeit one thread of.” That is the real confession of the chapter. Randi knows. She knows exactly what Vee is to her, even if she will not say it in a way that lets Vee answer.

The friction I felt is tiny and maybe intended: the fairytale sequence is so lavish it risks feeling unreal, but the title gives it permission. It is supposed to be almost storybook-perfect. The line “This is the best the acceptable world could do for her” snaps it into place. The title, “Fairytale,” first seems like the fantasy Randi is handed: mountain stranger, blue dress, ball, sleigh, hotel. By the end it means the lie people call happiness when it has the right scenery. It did not give the chapter away exactly, but it made me read the perfection with a waiting dread.

The book title, *A Polite Invitation*, feels sharper here. Randi is living inside invitations: the acceptable one from her parents’ world, John’s confident invitation upward to the hotel, and the unspoken invitation she refuses to make honestly to Vee. The series title *With a Long Spoon* keeps feeling like proximity to danger: wanting something you should approach carefully, maybe from farther away than desire permits. The packet’s closing beat, “Every yes was freely given. That was the problem,” feels very alive here, because Randi says yes to John freely and it reveals nothing; she is also preserving a situation where Vee’s future yes may not know what it is answering.

What I want next is Vee and Randi in the same room again, badly. What I dread is that Randi will come back from this colder and more determined to keep the shape of things hidden because now she has proof that the safe world is empty. My guess from here: this chapter makes Randi more dangerous, not because she loves Vee less, but because she knows she loves her more.
