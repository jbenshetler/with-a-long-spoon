## Reader reaction

I came into this chapter still suspended at the almost-kiss, and this gave me the aftershock instead of the kiss itself. That worked on me more than a direct kiss scene might have, honestly, because the center here is Vee rushing back into Cassie’s room with her whole body lit up. The erotic charge is not explicit, but it is very alive: “I made *him* stop. And I kissed him.” That “Me” lands hard. It is arousing less because of physical detail than because of agency, heat, and the thrill of her being astonished by her own wanting.

Vee feels incandescent here. She is all spill and warmth and disbelief. I love her in this state, and I’m frightened for her because she is already converting very little information into safety: “See. I know where he lives and everything.” No, Vee. You know “off six-fourteen,” past gravel, vaguely west of town. The chapter title makes that funny and ominous at once. It names the insufficiency of what she knows. To her it feels like proof; to Cassie, and to me, it is not enough.

Cassie remains my anchor. I trust her more every chapter. Her first response is joy, real joy — “Cassie’s whole face was open with it, glad, all the way in” — but then she immediately asks, “How much did he give you to drink?” That did not feel cynical to me; it felt like love with its shoes on. She believes Vee once Vee says, “Nobody did anything to me. I wasn't talked into it. I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose.” I liked that Cassie lets Vee have that. She checks the obvious danger and then does not steal the joy from her.

Pace is not physically present, which is interesting because his pull fills the room anyway. My feeling about him does not exactly move, but the danger sharpens. On the page, through Vee’s report, he did exactly the right thing: he accepted her no, began to walk away, and let her choose. That is seductive as hell. It is also exactly where the book’s tagline keeps biting: “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” Vee’s yes to the kiss, and now her yes to hiking, are emphatically hers. The chapter takes pains to let her say that: “I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose.” I believe her. That is why I’m uneasy.

The heat peaks for me at the confession of the kiss, not because I see the kiss, but because Vee’s voice changes around it: “the part wanted a different voice than the brass-cage voice.” That is a lovely, intimate distinction. The “brass-cage voice” is the chatter of recounting dinner; the kiss requires privacy even inside friendship. Then her whole-body spin is adorable and slightly heartbreaking: the room is “too small to hold what she had to do with it.” That line made me feel the size of her happiness.

The only friction is that the hiking invitation makes me tense in a way Vee does not share, but I think that is intended by the scene, not a failure of it. Cassie’s state-trooper joke is funny because it is also the sane question: “what to tell the state troopers when you go missing on a mountain with a man you met at a coffee shop?” The title, “Off Six-Fourteen,” absolutely recolors the chapter. Before reading, I didn’t know if it meant an exit, an address, a time, a bus line, anything. After reading, it feels like the first breadcrumb toward Pace’s private world: the long drive, the house, the trail, the place past gravel. But it also feels like a deliberately inadequate address, almost a non-address. “Off six-fourteen” is not enough to find her. That scares me.

The book title, *A Polite Invitation*, feels louder now. Pace’s invitation to dinner was polite; his “May I choose?” was polite; now hiking is another polite invitation, and Vee says yes quickly. *With a Long Spoon* still suggests eating with danger at a distance, or feeding/being fed with some necessary caution. The tagline is practically humming under this chapter. Nothing coercive happened, and that is the point. The trap is not that Vee was forced. The trap is that her freedom is being met so expertly.

What I want next: I want to see the kiss, but the book has skipped past it, so now I want to see hiking. I dread Pace’s house because I know from the jacket and earlier chapters that his house is not just a house. I especially dread Vee entering the space where Randi has already been so exposed, while Vee still knows nothing about Randi and Pace together. My guess from here: the hike will be another threshold, maybe the first time Vee sees where Pace lives “off six-fourteen,” and Cassie’s borrowed-shoes warning will sit in my head the whole way.

**Running memory — story so far:**  
The cover/jacket frames the book as *A Polite Invitation*, Book 1 of *With a Long Spoon*, tagline “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” The jacket says Randi/Miranda, Pace’s secret lover, and Pace, a young mathematician living alone at the end of a long drive, pick Vivienne Thorne across the quad and decide she will be third in their bed. Vivienne never knows there was a game; both Pace and Randi will fall as hard as she does.

In “The Bench,” Randi arrives at Pace’s warm house after lying to Brooke about a paper. Pace feeds her permitted honeycrisp apple slices, then reveals a hidden purpose-built erotic bench disguised as a low table. He undresses her, positions her, has her repeat safeword “Red,” spanks and edges her with careful checks, invokes Brooke and sorority judgment to break through her polished “please,” and makes her beg in a real voice. She orgasms intensely and cries. He gives aftercare with cashmere, water, and holding. In the bathroom, Randi realizes Pace saw past her face to her real self; terrified, she decides to put that self back behind the face. Pace believes he has helped her find her hunger; Randi silently decides she must figure out what to do.

In “Standards,” Randi returns to the sorority house Saturday morning in last night’s clothes. Brooke, Sloane, and Kinsey are in the common room. Brooke warmly interrogates her about the paper lie, Pace, his truck, grad school, and whether he teaches her course. Randi lies smoothly enough and escapes upstairs. In her single room, a Standards perk, she cannot settle, ignores two phone notifications, lies in last night’s clothes under an inadequate synthetic duvet, almost cries but does not, and waits for nothing.

In “The Pointing Game,” during sex, Pace asks Randi whether she wants to taste another woman. Her body answers with an orgasm. Later, calmer, she reframes the desire as “Only in a threesome. Randi hugs her, says “Love you, girl” and “See you later.” Vee walks home the long way, warm.

Pace enters, apparently by chance, and offers help. Vee introduces herself as Vivienne Thorne, “V.T.” Pace guides her through the problem using Euler’s formula, gives her back the pencil so the solution remains hers, asks about her econ interests, listens intently, and asks “May I call you?” She gives him her number. Randi encourages Vee afterward, while Cassie notes Pace looked at Vee like he forgot everyone else and at Randi like a great-aunt. Vee waits for her phone.

Cassie jokes about Vee not rolling a healer again. Vee goes with Pace to a too-bright froyo shop; he explains his flavor system, she takes too much without hearing her mother’s limiting voice, he pays, and they walk the long way in blue evening. He asks questions like her grandfather did. She confesses it is the nicest anyone has been to her in a long time. He answers lightly and tenderly: “Your tart’s holding up.”

In “Water Wings,” Vee and Cassie go to the rec pool. Vee wears an old green-and-white synchronized-swimming suit from when she was sixteen; it fits but gives her nowhere to hide. Guys in the bleachers make gross comments. Vee puts her face on; Cassie privately calls them tossers and jokes they will never know the love of a woman. Vee goes underwater and finds green cold silence, weightlessness, and a self before her body was a public problem. She grieves a little. Cassie sees her face wrong but does not ask; instead she splashes Vee into a water fight. Vee shriek-laughs, and the body that is such a problem feels left up on the deck with the rolled towel.

In “May I Choose,” Friday evening after the pool, with chlorine still in her hair and Cassie on the bottom bunk, Vee gets a text from Pace asking for Saturday dinner. He does not demand an immediate answer; Vee experiences that as room and care. Cassie notices her face, learns he asked for dinner, and, when Vee says other boys made her answer immediately so they knew where they stood, does not tease her. Cassie says Pace is not a boy but a man. Saturday, Pace comes to get Vee from her dorm lobby, showered, shaved, and dressed with quiet care. They walk to a small warm restaurant where Christine knows him. Vee is hungry from having skipped lunch while choosing dresses and borrowed shoes. The unfamiliar menu defeats her except for hummus; Pace asks, “May I choose?” Vee says yes, please. He asks if there is anything she won’t eat, then orders deliberately. Vee hears unfamiliar food names as soft promises. When food comes, she watches his hands tear bread and copies him. She eats bright green salad and smoky dark dip, realizing the hunger goes deeper than noon. She stops watching herself eat. He does not make her self-conscious. She loses the thread of his speech when he licks a drip of smoky food from his hand, and nothing bad happens. The evening goes soft; she lets him carry it. Mint tea in brass-handled glasses brings her partly back. He settles the bill with Christine. Outside, he takes Vee’s hand for the first time. Walking back the long way through quiet blue campus, he stops under a tree where a kiss becomes obvious. Vee states her rule: she does not kiss a man she has known a couple of days. Pace does not argue or press; he smiles and starts walking again. Vee stops him, lets the quiet hold, and says, “But… I’m making an exception.”

In “Off Six-Fourteen,” Vee comes back into her and Cassie’s dorm room still warm from the date, while the window is cracked and letting in a thin cold line because Cassie hates the building’s overdone heat. Vee has been talking since she got in, recounting dinner details like the tea in little brass cages. When she reaches the walk back and the tree, her voice changes. She tells Cassie that she said her rule about not kissing a man she has only known a few days, and Pace did nothing: he smiled, accepted it, and started walking on. Vee did not go with him. She stopped, made him stop, pulled him back, and kissed him herself. Cassie is genuinely glad but asks how much he gave her to drink. Vee says nothing, only tea, and insists nobody did anything to her, she was not talked into it, and she kissed him on purpose because she wanted to. Cassie believes her and says, “Look at you.” Vee spins in the small room, nearly hits the closet door, collapses into the bean bag laughing, and says, “Look at me.” She then tells Cassie Pace asked her hiking on a trail he likes. Vee has never hiked but said yes very fast. Cassie asks his name for the state troopers if Vee goes missing on a mountain with a man she met at a coffee shop. Vee says his name is Pace, he is lovely, and she is not going missing. She says he lives out west of town, off 614, past where it goes to gravel, and he will have water. Cassie repeats that “off six-fourteen” is the whole address and dryly challenges the vagueness. She asks if he is rich, because rich people hike and poor people walk. Vee does not know; she has not been to his house yet, but the not-knowing feels like something delightful she will get to find out. Pace told her to wear real shoes. Vee says she will borrow some. Cassie goes back to her reading but tells her to borrow good ones. Vee lies warm in the bean bag and does not feel the cold.

**How I feel:**  
Pace is intensely attractive and intensely suspect. His patience, competence, restraint, courtesy, and listening are seductive. He seems genuinely attentive to Vee’s safety and pleasure, and in “May I Choose” he earns her trust on the page by leaving room, choosing carefully, feeding without shaming, and respecting a boundary. In “Off Six-Fourteen,” Vee’s report confirms he accepted her no and let her initiate the kiss. But because I know he and Randi chose Vee in a game, every good act is double. The better he is, the more dangerous the trap feels. The hiking invitation raises the stakes because it points toward his private house/land/road.

Randi remains sympathetic, fascinating, and alarming. She is wounded by being seen, trapped by her face and Standards, and genuinely drawn to Vee, but she is also an accomplice in bringing Vee toward Pace without honesty. Her continued absence during Pace and Vee’s accelerating intimacy feels like pressure building offstage around her.

Vee has my full protectiveness. She is intelligent, observant, hungry for kindness, ashamed and defended around body/appetite, and now opening quickly because Pace’s careful attention reaches exactly where she has been deprived. Her “yes” to letting him choose dinner, her initiated kiss, and her fast yes to hiking feel freely chosen and emotionally real, which is precisely why I worry. Her joy in “Off Six-Fourteen” is beautiful and vulnerable.

Cassie is still my trusted anchor. She sees Vee’s patterns and protects without smothering. She does not know the hidden game, so her approval of Pace is limited, but her love for Vee feels steady. In “Off Six-Fourteen,” I especially trust her because she checks for alcohol/coercion, believes Vee’s agency, jokes but also registers that “off six-fourteen” is not a real address, and tells Vee to borrow good shoes.

Brooke remains socially formidable and untrusted. Sloane and Kinsey remain social weather. Randi’s mother and Vee’s mother remain sources of internalized shame; Vee’s grandfather remains a memory of gentle attention. Christine makes Pace seem rooted and known in a life outside campus.

Overall mood: sensual, tender, hungry, and anxious. The romance is working on me, but the title/tagline keep making every beautiful yes sound like a lock clicking.
