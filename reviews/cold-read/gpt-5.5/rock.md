## Reader reaction

This chapter feels like a lowering of the lights rather than an escalation, and it works on me almost entirely through quiet. I felt warm, then sad, then very protective of both of them in different ways. The erotic charge is not mainly in sex here; it is in being allowed close enough to hear Pace’s real name, to touch the scar, to sit under the quilt while the cold presses against the glass.

Vee is almost painfully open in this chapter, but not foolishly. She is paying attention. She tells him about Ohio, casseroles, church basements, her nurse mother, her jet-engine father, her distant Army brother, the name “Vivienne” meaning propriety or trouble depending on who says it. I like her even more because she can be funny without being mean: “Ohio has its mysteries. Cumin was not one of them.” But she also has that lovely instinct for mercy. When Pace says his real name is Peter and gives almost nothing about his mother, “He had taken his mother out of the answer. Vee let him.” That moved me. She is learning when to ask and when not to.

Pace / Peter deepens a lot here. I already knew he was young, brilliant, careful, and secretive, but “My real name is Peter” lands hard because it suddenly makes “Pace” feel chosen, made, forward-moving — and “Peter” feels like the boy underneath it. His childhood math story hurt: the teacher moving his desk into the hall for a year, making him do long division because he knew too much. Vee’s line — “They took the thing you loved, and made it the punishment” — is exactly right, and his “Yes. That is approximately what happened” is so contained it hurts.

The big shift for me is his first love. The page absolutely earns my unease. It is not me importing suspicion from the jacket; it is right there in the arithmetic Vee does: “Nineteen and fifteen. Two years.” The chapter lets me feel what Vee feels — that he is telling this as love, even luck, while something in it is uglier and more adult than he is willing to name. The line that stays with me is: “he had handed her the thing that had hurt him and named it luck.” That is devastating. It makes me understand him more, and trust him less in a particular way. Not because he is cruel here — he is not — but because he may have learned to call certain imbalances tenderness if they felt like rescue.

The math passage is beautiful, and it did not feel like showing off to me. It feels like the closest Pace can come to confessing his soul without saying “I was lonely” or “I was hurt.” The paintable volume with infinite surface, the coastline that gets longer the more carefully you measure, the ball and leaf and stars obeying the same sentences — all of that lets me see why Vee is entranced by him. “Most people walk around inside the poem and never get to read a line of it, and I got to read some.” That is gorgeous. It also makes him seductive in a way that is not merely sexual. He can make the world feel newly readable.

The scar returns, and this time the handling is better and more intimate than the earlier closed-off moment. Before, when Vee touched it during sex, he silently moved her hand away. Here, when she finds it again and asks, he covers her hand but does not remove it: “‘Not tonight,’ he said.” I loved that. It is a boundary, but not a rejection. “Only a door with a time on it that wasn’t now.” That line makes the chapter title, “Rock,” interesting to me. I hear rock in the fire, ridge, hard stars, the weight bar, the geological cold outside, maybe Pace himself as something solid and difficult. But I also hear the old scar and the thing under the skin: what was broken, struck, survived, hardened over. The chapter title does not give anything away; it sits under the chapter like weight.

Erotically, this is low heat but very charged. The opening image of Vee in his robe, tied badly and fallen open, with his hand settling over her breast “without pressing, without asking,” is intimate and arousing because it is so unhurried. Later the hottest moment for me is actually not explicitly sexual: her recognizing his lifting-focus as the way he is with her — “you’re all the way here” — and his hand simply settling on her bare hip. That is the chapter’s peak of erotic trust for me. It never goes slack exactly, though the long math explanation slows the sensual pulse; I still liked it because it made him more alluring.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, still feels tied to appetite, feeding, danger, and distance — dining with someone powerful, being nourished by someone you maybe should not fully trust. This chapter strengthens that because food opens the door into family, family into childhood, childhood into injury. The cover tagline keeps getting sharper: “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” Vee’s yes here is conversational and emotional. She chooses to ask. She chooses not to push. She chooses to say “Peter.” It is beautiful — and because I know he and Randi are still hiding the central fact, even this tenderness has a shadow.

What I want next: I want Vee to know about Randi and Pace. I dread it because chapters like this make the hidden arrangement feel more painful, not less. I also want the scar story now. Guess from here: the scar is going to matter emotionally, not just physically, and Pace’s history with being young among adults is going to echo his current relationship with Vee in ways he may not see clearly.
