## Reader reaction

This chapter feels quiet but exposed. No heat on the surface, just two women in a cafeteria after Thanksgiving, but my body read it as intimate because Vee is telling Cassie something close to the truth and still not the truth. The title, “Cropped,” lands very plainly at first — Vee cropped the induction photo before sending it to her mother — but by the end it means much more than the image. She has cropped Pace for her parents. She has cropped herself for Cassie. She is cropping the story everywhere to make it survivable.

I felt tender toward Vee here, and a little achey. Her parents’ pride is sweet: “Mom cried on the phone when I told her, and then she cried again at the table, just remembering it.” Her father burning the rolls “on purpose” gives them a real family shape quickly. But then the picture becomes dangerous because even the safe, waist-up version carries too much evidence. Her mother touching “her own collarbone” at the dress’s shoulders is one of those small bodily readings this book does well; it says mother, woman, recognition, concern, maybe envy, maybe memory, all without spelling it out.

Cassie is wonderful here. I trust her maybe more than anyone because she knows how to ask and how to stop. “So what is he,” she asks, “Not to your dad. To you.” That is exactly the question. Not gossip, not suspicion for sport, just: name the thing you’re inside. And when Vee can’t, Cassie accepts the closed door. The last line is perfect and painful: “Vee was grateful. She was also, a little, not.” That is the first cleanest statement of what I’ve been feeling about Cassie’s restraint: Vee wants to be protected by not being forced, but some part of her also wants someone to force the truth into daylight.

My feeling about Pace doesn’t really move, but the chapter refracts him through ordinary parental logic, and that makes him newly strange. “A boy doing a doctorate who does the right things with his daughter” sounds safe, almost ideal. Then Vee starts listing the things she leaves out — cooking, house, making the dress — and I understand why her father would start looking “for the catch.” Pace’s goodness is not untrustworthy on the page, exactly, but it is too much for conventional explanation. The line that really caught me was: “Honestly, my dad would take it better if I told him Pace takes that dress off me than that he made it.” That is funny, but it also names how intimate the making is. Sewing the dress is more bewilderingly possessive/tender than sex would be to her father.

Trust vs suspicion: I don’t feel Cassie is off at all. I feel her restraint is loving, though possibly dangerous because it lets Vee keep partitioning her life. With Pace, the text again earns both attraction and unease: the details Vee chooses not to tell make him sound almost mythically competent — young PhD candidate, dancer, homeowner, cook, maker of gowns — and “too good is its own kind of suspicious” is Vee herself saying what I feel. Not that he is false, but that the arrangement around him cannot be told cleanly. Randi is absent, and I felt her absence: Vee talks about the dress and induction photo, but not the heels, not Randi’s role in making that public self happen, not the kiss, not the nude photo Randi has seen. The cropping includes Randi too.

Erotic charge is low but present as afterimage. The chapter’s charge gathers around the dress: the cropped photo, the shoulders, the unspoken lower half, the fact that her family has only the safe version of an image I know was deliberately erotic below the waist. “The version they have stops at the waist” made the missing part feel loud. The heat is all in omission.

Reader friction: none in the bad sense. This is a small chapter, but it earns its smallness. If anything, I wanted more of Thanksgiving, but the chapter is not trying to be Thanksgiving; it is trying to show the edited report after. I bought that.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, still keeps hovering around appetite and danger-at-a-distance. Here there is cafeteria food, a family table, burned rolls, but the real “long spoon” feeling is how Vee handles dangerous nourishment from far away — giving her parents a cropped version, giving Cassie a partial version, keeping the full meal of Pace/Randi/desire away from anyone who might name it. The tagline, “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap,” feels sharper because Vee is not being forced to lie. She chooses every crop. That is what scares me.

What I want next: I want Cassie to keep asking, but I dread what happens if she asks the exact right question. I also want Vee to tell someone one full sentence without cropping it.
