## Reader reaction

This chapter lands quieter than the last one, but it has a very intimate ache. No sex on the page, no direct Pace, no Randi, but Pace is everywhere because Vee is trying to translate him into a family-safe story and realizing there is no version that fits. I felt that most in: “Honestly, my dad would take it better if I told him Pace takes that dress off me than that he made it.” That line is funny, but it also hurts. Sex is legible to her father; tenderness through craft is not. Pace making the dress becomes almost more exposing than him undressing her.

I feel very warm toward Vee here, and protective of her. She is not lying exactly, but she is cropping herself as much as the photo: waist-up for her mother, “the parts that fit” for her father, boyfriend with “a question mark on it” because even the ordinary word does not quite hold what Pace is to her. She feels more adult than her family can see, but not confidently adult; she is still half asking permission to name what she already knows.

Cassie is excellent in this chapter. Blunt, but tender by restraint. “Cassie didn’t laugh, which was the right call” made me trust her even more, and then “Is it that you can’t say, or that you don’t want to?” is exactly sharp enough without being cruel. I loved the final sentence: “Vee was grateful. She was also, a little, not.” That is the chapter’s whole emotional temperature. Cassie gives her room, and part of Vee wants to be spared, while another part wants someone to force the truth into the open.

The erotic charge is indirect but real. It is in the dress, in the cropped induction photo, in Vee’s parents looking without knowing what they are looking at, and in the fact that Pace taking the dress off her is somehow the less frightening truth. The heat peaks in the collision between sex, craft, and secrecy; it is not arousing in a graphic way, but it keeps the body present.

I have no new suspicion of Cassie. She feels cleanly, strongly herself. Pace is absent, and nothing here makes him feel off; if anything, the dress gets warmer because Vee understands more fully how impossible and precious it is. The unease is structural: Vee keeps having to crop the truth for everyone. Her parents get one crop, Cassie another, and nobody gets the whole picture.

The title, “Cropped,” is perfect and maybe a little blunt in the best way. It starts with the literal photo stopping at the waist, but by the end it means every edited version of Vee’s life: the induction picture, the boyfriend story, the “parts that fit,” the Pace her father can approve of, the self Vee can let Cassie see. *A Polite Invitation* still feels like an invitation into rooms where desire is made socially acceptable before it becomes dangerous. *With a Long Spoon* still feels like a warning about intimacy with something powerful and not fully safe. I have no cover tagline in the retained packet, so I can’t use one.

What I want next is for Vee to tell Cassie more, because Cassie has earned more than almost anyone. What I dread is that every cropped truth is becoming its own little betrayal, especially with Cassie, who keeps giving Vee chances to be honest.

### Chapter record

The chapter takes place at Sunday dinner in the athletic cafeteria, the night everyone came back, at a table near the windows under a high ceiling with a low murmur and the clink of steel on hard plastic. Vee and Cassie are both present in person; neither is an athlete.

Vee asks Cassie how home was. Cassie says it was good to see her parents. Cassie’s sister has a new baby, so the visit was “all baby, the whole time.” Cassie’s sister’s husband is “alright” and “a little bit of a dork.” Cassie says it was also good to leave because her parents still treat her like she is in high school, not overtly, but enough that she can tell they do not quite see her as an adult.

Vee says home was good. Her parents were proud of her honor society induction. Her mother cried on the phone when Vee told her, then cried again at the table remembering it. Vee’s father burned the rolls, on purpose, according to Vee. Vee says he does that.

Vee’s mother had printed the induction picture and kept it in her purse, taking it out to show people who had already seen it. Vee cropped the picture before sending it; the version her family has stops at the waist. Vee’s father kept looking at the dress, and Vee’s mother had thoughts about the top of it, especially the shoulders, though she did not speak them; she touched her own collarbone while looking.

Vee’s parents asked who took her to the induction, who gives a girl a dress like that, and who Pace is. Vee told them he was her boyfriend, but thinks she put a question mark on it out loud, as though asking them. Cassie does not laugh.

Vee told her father “the parts that fit”: Pace is a math PhD candidate; Vee and Pace went hiking; Pace taught Vee to dance on a square of concrete with a boom box before taking her to a hall. Vee says her father was happy, more than happy, because “a boy doing a doctorate who does the right things with his daughter” is something a father gets to be proud of.

Vee says her father looked at the dress again and went quiet, doing the math on its cost. Vee reads him as understanding that an expensive gift from a man means his daughter is in deeper than he had pictured. Vee thinks her father pictured Pace as older than he is, because to him a math PhD is a man with grey coming in, not someone barely older than Vee.

Vee did not tell her father Pace cooks for her or that Pace owns his house. She thinks a man who cooks for her and owns his house would make her father look for the catch, because “too good is its own kind of suspicious.”

Vee also could not tell her father that Pace made the dress himself, sewing it on a machine on his sun porch because Vee once mentioned seeing one in a magazine she could not afford. Vee says her father would take it better if she told him Pace takes that dress off her than if she told him Pace made it. She says sex is something her father understands, but sewing he never would. Cassie laughs briefly. Vee says her father would not think it was wrong, but weird, and that in some ways weird is worse than wrong because wrong is something he understands and rejects, while weird does not fit anywhere in how he sees the world.

Vee says the strange thing is that she is not ashamed of Pace making the dress; if anything, the opposite. Cassie has stopped eating and asks carefully, “So what is he,” clarifying “Not to your dad. To you.” Vee looks at her tray and thinks she has nothing that fits Cassie’s frame: not the right word for what Pace is, not the right story for how it went, and not the version of herself that stands up cleanly in the telling.

Cassie asks whether Vee cannot say or does not want to. Vee answers, “Both. Probably both.” Cassie nods, returns to her plate, and does not push. Vee is grateful, and also a little not grateful.
