## Reader reaction

This chapter hurts immediately. I felt it in my stomach more than my head: Vee arriving in her “good clothes,” trying to be presentable enough, adult enough, legitimate enough — “the things a person could wear to church or to meet anyone who mattered” — and then being met like a stranger at the door. The title gives itself to me only at the moment he says, “Can I help you?” She is not literally unknown to him, but he makes her into someone unknown, and that is worse.

Vee breaks my heart here. The clothes matter so much: bought with her mother at Christmas, carrying home and respectability and the version of herself that could be introduced, received, claimed. She has flown back on almost no sleep and driven to him with a plan, afraid of finding another woman, afraid specifically of “Sheri in his bed,” and the first mercy is that “there was no one behind him.” But even that relief turns hollow because the worse thing is not another woman. It is Pace standing there with no welcome.

My feeling about Pace moves sharply darker. I still believe, because of last chapter and because of the one-second break in his face, that he is not indifferent. When she says “Peter,” “His face broke,” and there is “a sob caught in his throat.” That is real. But it does not save him for me. It almost condemns him more, because he has the feeling and still chooses the cruelty of formality. “Can I help you?” is devastating. It feels like punishment, or self-protection so extreme it becomes punishment. I don’t know yet which, but either way he hurts her on purpose enough to keep the door mostly shut.

Trust versus suspicion: I don’t suspect there is another woman in the house; the chapter plainly answers that fear. “There was no one behind him.” I do suspect Pace’s closedness now in a much more serious way. Not that he does not love or want Vee — I think he does — but that he can turn that feeling off at the surface and make her bear the cost of it. The text earns that suspicion entirely through the doorway scene. He is “level and courteous and closed,” and the hand stays “on the edge of the door.” He is physically and verbally not letting her in.

The erotic charge here is basically absent in explicit terms, but the intimacy of the body-memory still hums painfully underneath. Her hand on his face is more charged than sexual: “the place she'd had under her hand a hundred times in the dark.” The single word “Peter” feels almost naked because it is “the true name, the one under the other.” That is the peak for me — not arousal exactly, but bodily intimacy turned into wound. The chapter is hot only by ghost-light, by remembering what access she used to have.

I had no boredom. It is very short and clean and brutal. The only friction is emotional, not craft friction: I almost recoiled from how perfectly aimed “Can I help you?” is. But I bought it because the chapter makes clear he is working hard to close himself, and because the face-break shows the feeling underneath. If anything felt like a line too pointed, it is “He had been entirely fine,” because of course from last chapter I know he hasn’t been entirely fine. But that is Vee’s perception in the doorway, and I felt how true it would look to her: pencil behind ear, book in chair, barefoot, working, no evidence of collapse.

The chapter title, “The Stranger,” could have spoiled the feeling a little if I had paused over it too long, because it tells me someone is going to be made strange. But it still landed. It points at Vee as the stranger at his door, Pace as the stranger wearing the face of her lover, and maybe the whole relationship made strange by the lack of a name. The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, keeps feeling like intimacy at a dangerous distance: you can eat, taste, accept, say yes, but the arrangement itself keeps something poisonous just out of sight. The tagline — “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” — aches here because Vee freely comes to him. No one forces her onto the plane, into the car, onto the step. But she is coming under conditions she still does not fully understand, and now even the yes of reaching for him is met by a door.

What I want next is almost desperate: I want Pace’s side of this exact moment, and I want him to suffer enough that the scene does not become only Vee’s humiliation. I also want him to fix it, but I dread that he won’t. My guess from here: this may push Vee toward Randi hard, because Randi is the one who answers warmly, calls her gorgeous, lets her be someone. And that scares me, because Randi’s warmth is already not innocent.

Sheri orders him to come dancing to a Brooks and Dunn cover band the first Saturday in February and says she does not care what “the redhead” has to say about it.

The kid server mistakenly puts the double burger and chocolate shake in front of Pace and the single before Sheri; Sheri corrects this by taking what is owed. Pace reflects that everyone wants something from him — to be wanted or to have a problem solved — but Sheri wants none of that. She simply likes him, gives him hell, and is not impressed by him. This is exactly what he needs.

The jukebox plays Elvis singing about being home for Christmas, pressing the seasonal bruise. Pace asks Sheri “How are things,” meaning the real things. She first deflects, then tells him there was someone named Paige, a junior in design school who made bird prints, laughed too loudly, and came from a loud family of seven. Sheri liked Paige and her family, but the relationship lasted only a couple months because Paige kept pushing Sheri to talk to her own people/mother at Christmas. Pace says people who have never lost anything think grief is a choice. Sheri tells him not to waste the good stuff on her and to save it for his redhead.

Sheri asks about Vee and teases Pace crudely about Vee’s body. Pace hesitates, and Sheri guesses whether he had his fill of Vee or whether Vee found out about “the brunette.” Pace says no, not like that. He explains that Vee wants “a declaration,” wants him “to say it.” Sheri asks whether that is so hard. Pace lists his proof of devotion: he is there every weekend; he gave Vee weeknights for school because she wanted them; when he has Vee, nothing else is in the room; he told her out loud she was the most important thing in his life. But Sheri says Vee did not ask him for reasons. She asks what he wants. Pace says, “I want Vee. I miss her.” Sheri tells him to say it. Pace says, “I can’t.” Sheri replies that not saying it matters more to him than having her, and that from her seat it feels pretty shitty. Pace does not argue.

Sheri then lightens the mood by stealing fries and talking about Randi, “that brunette”/“the sorority one” he brought dancing before Thanksgiving. She says Randi was easy on the eye but probably “a lot,” and that when Sheri hugged Pace hello, Randi looked at her like one more harem applicant and never learned her name. Pace does not answer, and Sheri drops it.

Sheri asks why Pace is so gone on Vee, since in two years she has never seen him act like this over a girl and he has never been a hit-and-quit man. Pace says, “Her name is Vee,” while Sheri keeps joking about Vee’s body. Pace says the air is different when Vee is in the room, that it crackles. Sheri translates this as good sex, and Pace admits yes, but says that is not what he meant. He says Vee sees things nobody else does: when he cooks, she does not just say it tastes good, she gets what it is for, “the whole idea under it. The philosophy of it.” Sheri mocks him — “The philosophy of your food. Lord almighty. Get over yourself.” Pace laughs fully for the first time that night. The chapter ends with the impossible thing sitting farther off for a while, the diner warm, and fogged glass keeping the cold outside.

In “The Stranger,” Vee acts instead of waiting. She has worn her good clothes on the plane — gray sweater, wool skirt, boots bought at Christmas with her mother — clothes fit for church or meeting someone who mattered. After a six-hour flight and a drive, she turns off the highway onto Pace’s road. His truck is in the drive, so he is home. She sits with the engine running, afraid now in a way she could not be while the plane’s forward motion carried her. During break, she had imagined the worst: Sheri in Pace’s bed, two people naked and belonging to each other. She gets out in the cold and knocks because the door is locked and she has not been expected.

Pace opens the door, and the first thing Vee sees is that no one is behind him. His house is quiet, lamplit, cool, with a book face-down over a chair arm. He is in weekend clothes — jeans, clean white t-shirt, barefoot despite the cold floor, good pencil behind his ear — and he has been working. To Vee, he seems entirely fine. She says, “Hello, Pace,” hoping his face will break open with grief or gladness. Instead, he gives a long breath, blinks, and says, “Hello. Can I help you?” He uses the level closed voice of someone speaking to a stranger at the door. Vee asks how his Christmas was; he says “Pleasant.” She asks if he did anything; he says “Yes.” He does not elaborate, does not ask about her, does not invite her in, and keeps his hand on the edge of the door.

Hurt overtakes Vee’s prepared composure. She steps into the space she has not been allowed, puts her hand to his jaw/cheek, and says “Peter,” the true name beneath the other. For one moment Pace’s face breaks: the cold goes out, raw grief rises, and a strangled sob catches in his throat. Then he pulls his face away as if burned, swallows the sound, and closes down again. Vee leaves without staying for more, gets to the car, drives down the drive, and after a mile pulls onto gravel at the county road because she cannot see or breathe. She sits between flat snowy fields in the running car, in her good clothes, and cries until nothing is left.

**Running memory — how I feel now:**  
Vee feels more vulnerable than ever: not naïve, but displaced, under-informed, and emotionally exposed. Her longing for Pace is bodily and domestic; her reliance on Randi is social and emotional; neither is safe because both are entangled with withheld truth. After “The Stranger,” she also feels actively humiliated: she risked coming to Pace as someone respectable and claimable, and he made her a stranger on his doorstep.

Pace’s silence still worries me, and now his capacity for coldness worries me more. He misses Vee and wants her; “The Usual” convinced me of that, and “The Stranger” confirms feeling under the surface when “Peter” breaks his face. But he can still choose the closed door, the minimal answer, the stranger’s voice. I trust his feeling and his bodily tenderness, but I mistrust his avoidance deeply. The longer he decides things “can keep,” the more his care turns into harm.

Randi remains lifesaving and dangerous. To Vee she is the sure returning thing, but I know from the jacket and prior chapters that she is not transparent. Sheri’s outside memory of Randi as possessive and dismissive of another woman sharpened my unease, and after Pace’s doorway rejection I fear Vee may move toward Randi for warmth and recognition.

Sheri feels like a gift: funny, wounded, earthy, blunt, and one of the only people who can tell Pace the truth without wanting anything from him. I do not currently see her as a real romantic threat; Vee’s fear of her was answered in “The Stranger” by the empty house, though Vee herself may not yet know Sheri’s real place in Pace’s life.

The overall mood moving forward: colder and sharper. The diner’s warm refuge did not translate into action; Pace has now met Vee’s brave return with a locked emotional door. Randi waits as the bright sure thing with shadows around her. The trap feels increasingly made of omissions, partial honesty, freely chosen hunger, and the devastating fact that a yes can lead someone all the way to a door where they are not let in.
