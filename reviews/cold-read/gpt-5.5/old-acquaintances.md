## Reader reaction

This chapter feels lonely in a very bodily way: cold sheets, too-short bed, lavender plug-ins, fabric softener, knees bent because she has literally outgrown the room. I felt Vee’s panic before I interpreted it. The worst part is not that Pace is absent; it is that the absence has no shape. No date, no promise, no name, no answer she can use to steady herself.

Vee moves me hard here. She is not melodramatic; she is trying to survive a blank. Her jealousy of an invented blonde is painful because the page shows exactly how it forms: no standing to ask, no public name, a missing Christmas week, the memory of Sheri, the stove, the food, the way Pace’s care could be happening for someone else. I don’t think she is being fair to him, necessarily, but I completely believe how she gets there.

Pace’s absence makes him feel both more loved and less trustworthy. Nothing on the page proves he has done anything wrong with anyone over Christmas, but the silence after “Believe Me” is damning emotionally. He left her “the room,” and what once felt respectful now feels like abandonment. My suspicion is not “he’s cheating”; it is that his refusal to speak lets Vee’s mind manufacture any catastrophe it needs.

Randi is the comfort and the danger. Vee’s text is so clean — an open hand, no demand — and Randi’s reply lands as warmth to Vee, while I carry the prior knowledge that it is cropped truth. Vee calls Randi “the only girl she could talk to about real things,” and that hurts because the realest thing between them is still unspoken and hidden from Vee. The chapter makes Randi feel like the sure thing of the new year, which is tender and alarming at once.

Erotically, this is charged rather than explicit. The peak is Vee trying to return to the fra diavolo night — sheet slipping, Pace seeing, garlic and pepper blooming — and then losing even that memory to the imagined blonde. That is devastatingly erotic and cruel: the beloved scene becomes a site of dispossession. The food/sex overlap is still working because it is not decorative; hunger and jealousy are the same ache here.

Friction: almost none. The line that made me stop was “The room kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed.” It is a little heightened, but it earned itself; the whole chapter is built around being lovingly preserved as someone you no longer are.

“Old Acquaintances” points first to New Year’s/Auld Lang Syne, but the chapter twists it: Vee is surrounded by old selves, old trophies, old friends who don’t text, old family rhythms, old shame, and none of them can accompany her into the new year. The book title and tagline feel sharper here. Every yes was freely given, but the trap may be that consent to private intimacy does not give Vee public standing, language, or security. *With a Long Spoon* still feels like a warning about eating from dangerous sweetness — care, luxury, desire, friendship — without being able to know what is in it.

What I want next: I badly want Pace to break the silence with something more than food and hands. I dread that Vee will lean toward Randi because Randi is scheduled, visible, and responsive while Pace is blank. Guess from here: the new term will make Randi feel like relief, not danger, precisely because Pace has left no next step.

At the mirror she saw her lipstick left on him and repaired her face; the smile still worked and meant nothing. She understood this was the best the acceptable world could do: a faultless man in a faultless room, safe life approved by her mother and house, touching only her surface. Pace on his worst night reached further. Pace found the hidden person and did not look away, though he could never belong in the life built to keep her hidden. Vee gave off something across an ordinary Tuesday table that this room could not counterfeit. Randi considered telling Vee plainly, but could not picture Vee’s face afterward and feared losing the hot easy friendship. She chose not to tell. She chose the “other way”: let the thing keep taking shape, let Vee arrive at it on her own, keep brunches as they are, have aliveness and friendship as long as she never says it out loud. Vee texted from Ohio near New Year, thinking about Randi and offering to talk or not. Randi sent back a true but cropped fairytale message about chalet and mountains, added the sincere “miss your face most though, gorgeous 💋,” turned the phone face down, and returned to John’s borrowed warmth.

  In “Old Acquaintances,” the same New Year’s exchange is seen from Vee’s side. Vee lies awake in her cold childhood bedroom in Ohio, under sheets smelling of her mother’s fabric softener and lavender plug-ins, knees bent because she outgrew the bed years ago. The house is asleep, the furnace in savings mode, the ball drop downstairs too lonely to watch. Her room preserves old Vivienne in high resolution — ribbons, synchronized-swimming trophies, clarinet, old friends’ faces — but does not know the woman now in the bed. Christmas days have been beige: ornaments, wrapping, tidying, casseroles, potatoes, good meat, Wheel of Fortune, and daily questions about her “young man” that she cannot answer in any way that fits this house.

  Vee checks her phone: nothing from Pace. She scrolls old warm messages and starts to type “are you—” but deletes it, knowing no message she sends will come back in the shape she needs. Pace leaves room, which she once appreciated and now hates. She looks at photos: laughing with Pace on his couch; herself in keyhole shirt and boots before the dance went wrong; the induction photo where the burgundy dress, slit, leg, and Randi-buckled heel made her feel wholly visible. She remembers sending Pace the whole induction photo, while cropping it at the waist for her mother downstairs, dividing herself by house.

  Vee looks at Randi’s feed — ski run, golden chalet, fancy dinner, bare shoulders, white-gold bracelet, solitaire, black mountains — and wants that whole visible life even though she knows from the PPP project what kind of money bought it. She texts Randi warmly, carefully, with no demand and no hint that she knows about Miranda Holdings: “thinking about you 💛 hope it’s beautiful out there. i’m here if you want to talk. or not.” Randi does not answer immediately. Vee calculates the time difference and imagines Randi in a lit public spectacle with a storybook man. What Vee envies is not the sex but the life lived out loud, in front of everyone. Her own best nights with Pace happen behind his door, and the one public outing went badly.

  Near midnight Randi replies with the fairytale text: “you ANGEL 😇 it’s honestly a fairytale out here — dad’s got us in this insane chalet, mountains for days, i’m never coming home. miss your face most though, gorgeous 💋.” Vee receives it as proof Randi is fine, glamorous, and still taking time for her. She thinks Randi is the only girl she can talk to about real things, though those real things are mostly Pace. Then she spirals over Pace: the doorway confrontation, his warm wrong answer, the quiet since, no word across break. Maybe it is over; maybe he thinks it is; maybe he thinks nothing happened. With no name for herself, she has no name for him and no standing to ask whose he might be. Remembering Sheri at Thanksgiving and Pace cooking for someone alone, she imagines him cooking over Christmas for an invented opposite of herself: blonde, tan, model-bodied. She tries to retreat into her best memory, the first sex/fra diavolo night at his table in the slipping sheet, but the imagined blonde replaces her in it. The bartender’s and Randi’s earlier reassurances no longer hold when Vee no longer knows she is the one. Midnight passes with small poppers and a dog bark. Vee imagines Pace’s arms around her, then cannot stop imagining whose he has them around instead. The new year has Randi penciled into it — class, brunches, texts, a face that will light up and call her gorgeous — and Pace as a blank. She falls asleep still reaching, in the bed her legs outgrew, while the cold room keeps watch over the girl who is gone.

- **How I feel**
  - Pace/Peter remains intensely attractive: careful, strong, patient, domestic, exact, sexually overwhelming, eloquent through acts, and believable in his sensory difficulty at Chili’s. Trust in his immediate practical care remains strong, but unease is sharper after “Old Acquaintances” because his silence over break leaves Vee with no standing, no schedule, and no answer; his habit of leaving room can feel respectful in warmth and like abandonment in cold. Randi’s comparison made his power more frightening and compelling: he reaches the hidden self more than the approved perfect man. Vee’s jealousy does not prove he has done anything wrong, but it proves the damage his not-saying can do.
  - Vee feels brave, hungry, alive, endangered, and painfully alone. She named the exact problem with Pace and refused warmth/food/touch as substitutes for words; now she is paying for that refusal in a cold childhood room that loves an old self. Her New Year text to Randi is kind, plain, and available; she does not know how much it means or how much is being hidden from her. Her imagined blonde is unfair but completely understandable from inside the blank Pace has left.
  - Randi is more sympathetic and more alarming. I feel the cage around her: father/mother/table/approved men/beautiful face. Her longing for Vee feels genuine, not just tactic. But she knowingly chooses not to tell Vee and to let Vee “arrive” without being asked directly. In Vee’s New Year, Randi is becoming the reliable bright thing — the face, class, brunches, texts — which feels tender and dangerous because Vee does not know the truth underneath that reliability.
  - John is attractive, competent, safe, and hollow only because Randi cannot be reached by him. I do not blame him much yet; he is the fairytale prince who assumes, performs, pleases, and still never finds the real person.
  - Randi’s father repels me through Randi’s revulsion, PPP shadow, and Vee’s awareness that his money bought the dream she envies. He is hearty and adoring on the surface but part of the approved table where truth cannot sit.
  - Randi’s mother feels cold, exquisite, restrictive, and cutting by presence alone.
  - Vee’s parents remain loving but category-bound. In “Old Acquaintances” I feel their care more concretely — heat turned down, work schedules, fabric softener, casseroles, Wheel of Fortune, repeated questions — and also how impossible it is for Vee’s changed life to fit inside it.
  - Cassie remains deeply trustworthy, perceptive, blunt, and tactful, though absent. She is still the person Vee should be able to tell and cannot.
  - Sheri now pricks at Vee as an example of Pace cooking for another woman, though the earlier context was loneliness/pity and cycling friendship.
  - Overall mood carried forward: the new year begins with two women on opposite sides of unsaid words. Vee has demanded a name from Pace and left when she did not get it; Pace has apparently gone silent through the break. Randi has recognized her desire for Vee and chosen not to name it. Vee, not knowing this, is starting the year with Randi as the sure visible comfort and Pace as a blank private ache. The book feels colder and more dangerous because the care, pleasure, beauty, family love, and friendship are all real — and all insufficient without the thing that must be said.
