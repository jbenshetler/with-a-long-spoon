## Reader reaction

This one hurts in a quieter, more ordinary way than “Fairytale.” After Randi’s glittering Gstaad chapter, landing in Vee’s too-small childhood bedroom is almost physically claustrophobic: “the cold lavender dark of a room that loved a girl who no longer lived inside her.” I felt that line in my chest. The erotic heat is almost entirely absent as action, but the ache of it is everywhere — not arousing so much as deprived. Her body remembers Pace’s kitchen and hands and sheets, and memory itself turns against her.

Vee is the whole chapter, and my sympathy for her deepens sharply. She is not just lovesick; she is displaced. Home loves “Vivienne,” the old version with ribbons, swimming trophies, clarinet, old friends, and a bed her legs outgrew, but it does not know Vee. That makes the chapter feel like a second “Bare”: she is cold, sleepless, and surrounded by substitute comforts that cannot answer. The fabric softener, lavender plugins, blankets, childhood objects — all of them are tender and suffocating at once.

My trust in Pace is wobbling harder now, because the absence is no longer just one painful conversation. It is “nothing since, not a word across the whole break.” I still don’t know what he thinks happened. The text is careful to keep me in Vee’s not-knowing, so I can’t fairly accuse him of betrayal. But his silence now feels cruel in effect whether or not it is cruel in intention. The line “He always left you the room — a thing she’d appreciated once, and hated now” is exactly the turn: his spaciousness, once consent and respect, now feels like abandonment.

Randi feels like comfort and danger at the same time. Vee’s message to her is generous and carefully non-demanding, and Randi’s reply lands as warmth: “you ANGEL… miss your face most though, gorgeous.” I understand why Vee clings to that. Randi is “the one sure thing the new year held.” But because I just saw Randi deliberately curate the fairytale and turn away from confession, I also feel the trap tightening. Vee thinks Randi is “the only girl she could talk to about real things,” while the realest thing between them — Randi’s desire, the hidden arrangement, the PPP knowledge Vee folded away — remains unsaid.

The erotic charge peaks in memory, especially when Vee reaches for “the best night she had”: Pace making fra diavolo after sex, the sheet slipping, her body “quiet and the evening having used her well.” That is charged because it has all the earlier Pace magic — food, sex, aftermath, being seen and not needing to fix herself. Then it curdles terribly when the imagined blonde replaces her: “She could not keep her own night.” That is the chapter’s cruelest emotional violation. Not Pace doing anything on-page, but Vee’s insecurity stealing even her own memory from her.

The friction for me is minimal; this chapter knows exactly what it is doing emotionally. If anything, the imagined blonde risks being melodramatic, but the text earns it by naming the state: “what you believe at midnight, cold and alone on the one night you’re not supposed to be.” That kept me with her rather than judging her.

The chapter title, “Old Acquaintances,” feels pointed at New Year’s — “old acquaintance” from the holiday song, people carried or forgotten as the year turns — but it also points at all the selves and people Vee cannot reconcile. Her childhood room knows old Vivienne. Her old friends haven’t texted. Her parents keep asking after “your young man” in old categories. Pace may already be slipping into the past, or may not be, and she cannot know. The title did not give the chapter away, but afterward it feels mournful: what should be kept, what should be let go, and who gets remembered into the new year.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, keeps gathering food and distance. Here food is love but not the thing: “the casserole and the potatoes and the good meat… had never once been the thing Vee was hungry for.” Pace’s cooking remains the central nourishment, but now she imagines him feeding someone else. The cover tagline feels sharper than ever: every yes was freely given, and now Vee is trapped not because she was forced, but because she genuinely chose, genuinely wanted, genuinely entered — and no one has given her the names or facts that would let her stand securely inside her own choices.

What I want next: I badly want to know whether Pace reaches out, whether Vee tells Randi anything real, and whether Randi uses Vee’s dependence on her steadiness kindly or dangerously. My dread is that Vee will turn to Randi because Randi is available, and Randi will be both sincere comfort and part of the concealed trap.
