## Reader reaction

This chapter lands softly, which almost makes it worse. I feel my guard go up because the date is so sweet, so ordinary, so almost comically wholesome — frozen yogurt, dusty plastic ivy, lemon cleaner, napkins, walking nowhere — and from inside Vee it feels completely earned. I like Pace here. I really do. That is the uncomfortable part.

The opening hit me right in Vee’s body: “Her stomach went before she did, the drop of a stair missed in the dark.” That is exactly the anticipation she has been sitting in since he left the coffee shop. She wants him to call so badly that she has to turn it into suspicion: “Is he desperate? Texting this fast?” I believed that instantly — the reflex of protecting yourself from wanting by insulting the thing you want.

Randi is warm and forward again, and I still don’t know how to trust her. “He looked like a man who wants you,” followed by “He wants to see you naked,” is funny and intimate and a little thrilling, but also grossly pointed because I know she and Pace already picked Vee for exactly that direction. Randi’s encouragement — “You should go” — feels like a friend hyping her up and like an accomplice moving the piece one square forward. Both are true on the page. Her line, “The rule’s for boys you’re not sure about. You’re not unsure,” is sharp, socially fluent, and persuasive in the way Randi always is. She makes Vee’s own desire the argument, which is precisely the danger this book keeps circling: nobody has to force Vee because Vee genuinely wants.

Cassie remains my anchor. Her joke — “As long as he doesn’t make you roll a healer again” — is funny and weirdly humanizing; it gives Vee a little past, a nerdy one, not just “beautiful chosen girl.” Cassie doesn’t stop anything, but she is present as a cooler witness. She knows Vee well enough to tease her about a whole semester of keeping “a business major alive in a video game.” That detail made Vee feel more solid to me.

Pace on the date is devastatingly attractive. The frozen-yogurt analysis is charming because it is both absurd and perfectly him: “Tart on the bottom holds up under the heavy ones. You put chocolate on tart and it’s an argument the whole way down.” He gives the same care to froyo that he gave to Vee’s math problem, and Vee notices that: “with the same attention he'd given her calculus.” He is funny without mugging for it, and when he says, “I’ve thought about most things,” I both smiled and recoiled a little. That is Pace’s seduction and Pace’s problem in one sentence. He has thought about most things. Including this. Including her.

The erotic charge here is not explicit, but it absolutely simmers. It peaks for me not at Randi’s “He wants to see you naked,” though that has heat, but when Vee builds the overfull cup and does not hear her mother’s policing voice: “Today there was nothing.” That felt quietly sensual — appetite unguarded, excess allowed, sweetness leaning and dripping, her not caring. The chapter is clothed and innocent on the surface, but underneath it is all permission. He pays before she can stop him; she grabs spoons and napkins; he holds the door with his back; they walk “the long way, not toward anything.” It’s not sexual action, but it is courtship as bodily loosening.

The tenderness works too, especially the ending. Vee says the embarrassing true thing: “This is the nicest a person has been to me in a really long time. And it’s froyo.” I felt protective of her there. It could have become too big, too sentimental, or too predatory. Pace does neither: “He didn’t pretend. He didn’t make it large either.” That is exactly what would make him feel safe to her. He lets the truth exist, then gives her a small practical mercy: “Your tart’s holding up.” And the image answers her fear: “the whole stack of it, still standing.” I found that beautiful. I also found it frightening because he knows how not to mishandle a fragile disclosure.

My suspicion is absolutely text-earned, but it is carried in from what I already know, not because this chapter alone makes Pace behave badly. On this page, he is patient, attentive, respectful, generous. But the line “as if he'd set something down exactly where he meant it to go” pricked me. Vee reads it as contained pleasure at making her laugh. I read it through Pace’s established pattern of placement and timing. The chapter title, “The Long Way,” is simple and lovely after reading: they literally walk the long way, “not toward anything,” but of course I feel they are very much moving toward something. It also points to Pace’s method — indirect, patient, scenic, never grabbing for the destination. The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, feels closer now: spoons are literally in Vee’s hand, a “fistful of napkins” with them, but the long spoon also feels like the indirect instrument of this whole seduction. No one reaches straight in. They stir from a distance.

What I want next is almost embarrassingly divided. I want more of this date because I enjoy Vee enjoying herself. I want Pace to keep being this careful. I want Vee to be cherished. But I dread how perfectly the trap is working. My guess from here is that Pace will continue to build trust through small, respectful, nonsexual intimacies before anything overt happens. And because every step feels like Vee’s own, I am more worried, not less.

**Running memory — how I feel:**  
Pace is intensely attractive and increasingly alarming. I believe his care, patience, erotic skill, intellectual generosity, and emotional tact. He is almost impossibly good at giving someone the kind of attention that makes them feel safe and real. But I trust him less because I know he has helped stage Vee’s selection and entrance without her knowledge, and because he knows when something is unfair and sometimes does it anyway. In “The Long Way,” he is at his most ordinary-sweet and therefore most dangerous: frozen yogurt, listening, restraint, and the perfect small response to vulnerability.

Randi has my sympathy, fascination, desire, admiration, and suspicion. She is not passive; she wants Vee, chose Vee, and keeps actively moving this forward. Her warmth toward Vee feels real, but it is not innocent. In “The Long Way,” her teasing encouragement is funny and intimate, but “He wants to see you naked” is chilling because she knows more than Vee does.

Vee has my strongest protectiveness. She is intelligent, observant, funny, hungry for attention, and shaped by shame around appetite and display. “The Long Way” makes her loneliness plain: ordinary kindness feels astonishing. Her overfull froyo cup without her mother’s voice felt like a real little liberation. I want her to have everything she wants and dread the hidden frame around those wants.

Cassie remains the person I most trust near Vee. She is dry, loyal, observant, and not easily dazzled. Her healer joke gives Vee history and makes their friendship feel lived-in.

Brooke remains formidable social power; warm but not safe. Sloane is observant/classifying; Kinsey is eager and socially pressuring. Randi’s mother and Vee’s mother both loom as sources of inherited shame and restriction, especially around food, bodies, need, and respectability.

Overall mood: the trap has become tender. Every yes still feels freely given, and that is exactly why I am worried.
