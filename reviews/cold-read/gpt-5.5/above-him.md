## Reader reaction

This chapter hit me very tenderly, and also with a low ache because of the cover’s promise. My body-read of it is warmth first: cool sheets, him lifting her at the door, food after sex, her saying “Peter” into his sleeping ear, then that top drawer. It is a quiet domestic-love chapter. It feels like Vee realizing she has crossed from visiting into belonging.

Pace/Peter, in Vee’s experience here, is almost devastatingly attractive. Not in the showy way — in the wordless accommodation way. The lift at the door is joyous; the sex is urgent but still centered on her; the salmon and dill and warm tomatoes continue the feeding/care motif; and then the drawer is the real romantic blow. He sees her mess in his ordered bathroom, says nothing, and by the next week he has rearranged the space so the mess is no longer mess but place. “He’d folded his whole ordered self down into a single drawer. He’d given her the top one. The easy one. The one your hand goes to first.” That landed hard. I believe, as Vee does, that he is saying love in action even while withholding the word.

Vee is so easy to love here and so vulnerable. I felt her happiness as physical relief: “happier than she'd known a person was let to be.” Her admitting to herself that she loves him feels inevitable, not melodramatic. It also scared me because she is building certainty out of gestures, and the central fact from the jacket — that Randi and Pace chose her together and told her nothing — is still not in her hands. So her “A man didn't do all of that for a girl he was only passing a season with” feels true inside the scene and also dangerous under the book’s larger shadow.

Randi is not present, and that absence matters. After the last chapter, where Randi’s gaze had entered Vee’s own arousal so powerfully, this chapter gives Vee a world with Pace again: food, sex, robe, bathroom, drawer. Randi is not named, and I felt a kind of relief from her brightness and pressure. But the relief is uneasy because I know she is not gone from the story. The drawer makes Vee more attached to Pace, more placed in his house, and therefore more vulnerable to whatever the hidden arrangement is.

Trust versus suspicion: on the page, Pace does not feel off in his direct behavior. The drawer feels genuinely loving, not manipulative in the immediate human sense. But because I carry the jacket and the accumulated knowledge that he and Randi started this as a game, every loving gesture has a second shadow. The text itself earns one tiny interpersonal uncertainty when Vee watches him see her bathroom sprawl and wonders, “Had he minded. She couldn't tell. He hadn't said. He never said.” That’s not sinister exactly, but it reminds me that his wordlessness, which is romantic here, also leaves her interpreting alone.

The erotic charge works, but it is more afterglow than heat. The first section is explicit enough in summary — “They hadn't made the kitchen. They hadn't made the hall” — and the line about him bringing her over “usually more than once before he took a single thing for himself” is hot because it is about attention and restraint. The couch after dinner has a warm second-wave charge. But the peak of the chapter for me is not sexual; it is romantic/domestic: the top drawer. That is the scene’s real climax.

I had very little friction. The chapter is brief and clean. If anything, the weight-loss paragraph made me pause — “her body quietly burning down whole weekends of him” — because it is sensuous and tied to appetite/freedom, but I also felt a flicker of discomfort at weight loss being a “bonus.” That discomfort belongs to Vee’s own history with body shame and clothing fit, so it did not feel false, just charged.

The chapter title, “The Top Drawer,” is beautifully literal and then suddenly huge. Before reading, it sounded like an object-title, maybe intimate, maybe domestic. Afterward it means being given first reach, daily access, a place in his house above his own things. It points toward belonging — almost a silent vow. It did not give the chapter away exactly, but once the drawer appeared, I knew emotionally where the chapter was going.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, keeps gathering food, feeding, distance, caution, and appetite. This chapter adds salmon, dill sauce, warm tomatoes, mustard dressing, Greek wine — but more than food, it adds domestic provisioning. The cover tagline, “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap,” presses harder because Vee’s yes to belonging is not coerced. She wants the drawer. She wants the robe, the scrunchies, the bag, the place. That is exactly what makes me dread the eventual revelation.

What I want next: I want Vee to keep this happiness, which means I’m afraid she won’t. I expect the drawer will matter — either because Cassie or Randi notices she is bringing things/staying over, or because Vee tells Randi and Randi understands exactly how deep Pace has let Vee in. My guess from here: Randi’s response to this kind of domestic claim may be complicated. This is no longer just sex or confession; Vee now has the top drawer.
