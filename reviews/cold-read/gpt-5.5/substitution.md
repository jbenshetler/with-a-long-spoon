## Reader reaction

I came into this chapter still hot and shaken from the bench, and the first relief is that this one is quieter on the surface: a coffee shop, homework, the humiliation of a problem that will not yield. But the quiet doesn’t cool the book down. It just moves the charge into looking, timing, and substitution — into who is helping whom, who is watching whom, and who knows more than Vee does.

Vee is so easy for me to feel with here. Her frustration with the problem is specific and alive: “three approaches crossed out, the margins crowded,” the pencil set down because she might break it. I like her pride in wanting the challenge problem, not because she has to but because she wants to be the kind of person who can do it. That makes Pace’s entrance land hard. He does not come in as a flashy romantic hero; he comes in as a disturbance in the room’s gravity. “Something in her chest went quiet” is exactly the right first signal. I felt the pull before I trusted it.

And I did feel the pull. Pace works on me here almost entirely because of restraint. The erotic charge is not sexual activity, but it is unmistakably bodily: his eyes on her face, his cuffs rolled back, her noticing his forearms, his hand taking the paper carefully, his asking for the pencil “with his eyes first.” That last detail made me melt a little. It matters because this is the book’s kind of consent in miniature: permission before contact, even with an object. He gives her the method and then “set the pencil back in her hand.” That is the swoon of the chapter for me. He could dominate the problem; instead he lets her solve it. It is courtly and precise and very hot because it preserves her agency while making her feel seen.

Vee trying on Randi’s introduction bit made me ache. “Vivienne Thorne. V.T. Yes, like the university.” It is charming, and it lands, but the tenderness of it is that she is borrowing poise from the girl who just made her feel chosen. Then she wants “her own name back” and says Vee. That little correction felt like a whole person surfacing.

Randi unsettles me more here than Pace does, but not because Vee sees it. Vee reads Randi’s texting as “some boy,” and I, from the recent chapters, know enough to feel the trapdoor under that. Randi’s one-word “Randi” to Pace is cold compared with the practiced sparkle she usually gives strangers. She doesn’t do the adjective joke. She withholds. Her hand around the coffee cup, bringing it up and not sipping, is so small and so loud. Vee explains it as boredom, because that explanation lets the scene stay simple for her. I don’t think it is simple. I feel Randi managing the very situation she helped create, and maybe discovering that watching it happen is not the same as imagining it.

Cassie is wonderful here, dry and almost brutally useful. “That cute guy in line is looking at you” is such a plain little detonation. She sees the room more cleanly than Vee does, and probably more cleanly than Randi wants her to. Her line about Pace looking at Randi “the way you’d look at somebody’s great-aunt” made me laugh, but it also sharpened the triangle. Cassie names the asymmetry without knowing, or at least without saying, what it means.

I have no suspicion of Pace’s conduct in this chapter on the page itself. He is warm, careful, interested, and genuinely good with Vee. But because I have the jacket and the recent chapters, the situation around his care feels rigged. That is the unease: not that his attention is fake, but that Vee does not know she has been placed in its path. The tenderness is real, and the substitution is real too. Randi substitutes Vee into the position she cannot bear to occupy with Pace; Vee substitutes Randi’s social grace into her own mouth; Pace substitutes the right mathematical frame for the impossible one. The chapter title did not give the whole thing away before reading, but afterward it clicks in several directions at once.

The math metaphor works on me. “Put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing that’s suddenly plain” is almost too perfect, but I bought it because it is also literally the lesson. It points at Vee and Pace, at Vee and Randi, maybe at the hidden structure none of them can yet measure directly. I did feel one tiny brush of authorial emphasis there, but not enough to throw me out. The scene earns it through the solved problem.

The erotic peak for me is not the number exchange, though “May I call you?” absolutely lands. It is the moment Vee realizes what kind of help Pace gave her: “He had given her the question and given her back the pencil.” That is the chapter’s heat. After that, the phone waiting facedown is delicious. I wanted it to light up too, while also dreading what the light will mean.

The book’s title, *A Polite Invitation*, feels more dangerous now because this chapter is all politeness: “Pardon me,” “May I call you,” careful nods to Randi and Cassie. The invitation is soft, correct, almost old-fashioned — and still part of a larger arrangement Vee cannot see. *With a Long Spoon* keeps promising distance from something tempting and possibly dangerous: you can approach, but you had better know what you are eating with. The packet’s closing beat, “Every yes was freely given. That was the problem,” feels painfully alive here. Vee’s yes to Pace’s help and then to the phone number is fully hers. That does not make the setup clean.

What I want next is the call, obviously. I want Vee to get the rush of being wanted by him. I also dread the first moment where Randi has to watch Vee want him without filtering that want through her.
