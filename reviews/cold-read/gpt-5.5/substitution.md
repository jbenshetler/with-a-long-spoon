## Reader reaction

This chapter made my stomach drop in a very quiet way, because on the surface it is almost absurdly lovely: Vee stuck on a math problem, a handsome-not-handsome stranger offering help, that clean intellectual click when he shows her the right substitution, the little bloom of being asked real questions. It’s charming. It works on me. And because I know Pace and Randi arranged Vee before she knew anything, the charm is also awful.

My feeling about Vee gets even more protective here. She is not stupid, not merely dazzled, not just socially hungry. She is genuinely bright, especially in the way she sees method: “the eye for *how a smart person handles a problem they could handle in any of several ways*.” That line made me like her sharply. She understands that Pace could have solved the thing for her, but chose to hand it back to her: “He had given her the question and given her back the pencil.” That is such a precise description of why his attention feels erotic and respectful to her. And of course it is also exactly why it is dangerous. He knows how to make someone feel uninvaded while entering the center of her.

Pace, here, is devastatingly attractive. I hate that he is. His entrance is almost too perfectly calibrated: he crosses the room looking only at her, makes the room “settle a half-degree,” asks “Can I help?” and then asks for her pencil with his eyes before taking it. The peak erotic charge for me is not overtly sexual at all; it is his rolled cuffs, his forearms, his hands on the page, and the fact that he does not show off. “They were not a math tutor’s forearms” is funny and hot and very Vee. The whole tutoring sequence has real charge because it is competence plus restraint. He does not merely know the answer; he knows how to make her feel like the answer remains hers.

But my trust in him keeps falling. Nothing he does in this chapter, taken by Vee alone, feels wrong. That is the problem. He is polite. He asks. “May I call you?” is lethal because it sounds like old-fashioned respect, and Vee hears the “may.” But I know from the previous chapters that his appearance is not innocent. He has not just happened upon her. The title, “Substitution,” gives me the math mechanism, yes — the old Euler thing substituted into the hard integral — but it also darkens the scene before I even finish it. Pace is substituting himself into Vee’s life through the acceptable opening of math help. Maybe Randi is substituting him for herself for a moment, letting Vee’s fascination shift. Pace lets her see his pride. Randi’s “yes” now has a name, a class schedule, and copper hair. Pace says, “Let’s go feed you.”

Fourth chapter, “See You Later.” The stats lecture meets in a tiered room on the second floor of the math building. Vee arrives early, as usual, with Cassie beside her. Cassie unpacks a granola bar and first the wrong textbook, then the right one. Vee opens her notebook and decides not to look at the door, though she repeatedly does or nearly does. She remembers meeting Randi the day before outside the dining hall with Meg from her floor. Vee had been mid-laugh and not thinking about anything when she and Randi collided; Vee had immediately read Randi as a dazzling “somebody,” the kind of person a campus orbits and the kind Vee would have given a lot to stand beside. Randi had smiled warmly, touched Vee’s arm, laughed at her joke, asked what she was studying, and said, “oh my god, we should sit together” when they discovered they shared stats. Vee walked back to her dorm not feeling her feet and spent the day wondering whether Randi meant it or merely said it.

In class, Vee keeps watching for Randi. Cassie notices and says, “You keep looking at the door.” Vee denies it. The professor’s microphone-clearing signals class is about to start, and Vee thinks the question is about to be answered by default: Randi isn’t coming, or didn’t mean it. Then Randi appears at the end of the row, having come in from the back, sidestepping past annoyed students with small apologies, looking at Vee with her whole face lit. Vee says “Hi” too brightly. Randi says she almost didn’t find her and calls the room a cave. She sits on Vee’s other side, the aisle side, so Vee is between Randi and Cassie. Vee notices Randi’s citrus scent, exact expensive clothes, and skillfully done face. Randi squeezes Vee’s forearm and praises her for having the right pen, joking she will copy off her all semester.

Randi warmly introduces herself to Cassie with a bit about her name: “Yes, like the adjective. No, it's not a coincidence. Yes, my parents had a sense of humor.” Cassie does not take the offered rhythm and says only “Cassie,” flatly. Vee smooths the hitch by joking that Cassie says about four words a day and must be read like tea leaves. Randi receives even Cassie’s flatness as delightful and says she needs that because she says four hundred words a day and only nine land. Randi’s warmth stays generous toward Cassie, but keeps returning to Vee, who feels chosen by the seating, the attention, and Randi’s bag touching hers.

The professor lectures on residuals for fifty minutes. Vee means to take notes but keeps losing the lecture because Randi is present at her right elbow, recrossing her legs, writing, leaning in to whisper whether something will be on “the thing,” pressing shoulder to shoulder. Vee whispers that it will be on the midterm and feels happy to be useful, “the one who knows things.” The professor’s voice becomes class-drone, and the math Vee is usually good at goes by like scenery because Randi is the better thing beside her. At the end, the professor says next time they will get into “latent variables,” factors one cannot measure directly and must infer from measurable ones. Vee writes “latent variables” and watches to see whether Randi is getting up.

Randi immediately says she is dying for coffee and commands/invites both Vee and Cassie to Chi Latte, the yellow one on Draper. Vee knows Chi Latte as the place with lines and photos, the place she once looked into but did not enter alone because it felt like walking into someone else’s party. Vee’s yes arrives full-sized in her chest; she waits one beat to fold down her eagerness, then says yes. Randi is delighted and tells Cassie to come too. Cassie declines because she has a thing/lab at noon. Randi offers to wait, but Cassie says she is good. Cassie looks at Vee, something brief crossing her face that Vee cannot name, then says, “Have fun” and “Goodbye, Vee.” The whole word “Goodbye” lands oddly to Vee, too formal/final for a coffee outing. Vee jokes that it is just coffee and she’ll see Cassie at home in two hours. Cassie does not fix the oddness; she leaves.

Vee and Randi walk to Chi Latte. Vee reads the sign properly for the first time: Χ ΛΑΤΕ, the Greek Chi plus latte pun. She has stood outside this joke before; now, with Randi holding the door, she feels let in. Inside, Chi Latte is yellow and white, bright and carefully styled, smelling of coffee and chocolate, with spoons ringing irregularly in cups as people stir sweetness. The place is full of girls. Vee reads the room in detail: matched leggings and cropped quarter-zips, scrunchies, made-up faces, underdressed worked-for bodies at the window. Seeing the underdressed girls, Vee thinks “Floozies” in her mother’s voice before she has finished looking. Underneath that judgment is longer looking: she notices stomachs, waistbands, bare skin, and feels a private true thought that she could wear those clothes and do them better because she has more and better than they do. But she is covered to the collarbone, by preference and by her mother’s internalized nice-girl warning about attention. She recognizes herself as both the most of what the room is selling and the one with the most fabric on, and she does not look straight at either fact.

Then Vee looks at Randi and the room goes quiet for her. Randi is not performing for the room the way the others are; she is simply Randi. Vee wants her helplessly from three feet away. Randi orders her an oat-milk lavender latte, says “Trust me,” and leads her to the high table by the window. Vee has seen girls perched there like they belong; now she sits there in one clean motion and becomes a girl at the high table at Chi Latte, up in the window and visible. She and Randi sit level, knees nearly touching, latte warm in Vee’s hands. The drink is good.

They talk. Vee learns Randi has her own room and senses that means Randi is near the top of her house, not just in it. Randi’s warmth toward Vee feels even more astonishing because of that status. Randi talks and listens; Vee later cannot reconstruct the words. She remembers only the shape: Randi being funny, a story about a sister and a parking ticket, Randi asking things and seeming to want the answers, a stretch about a guy, and one offhand flattering thing that goes into Vee like a key in a lock. Vee has hung on every word but kept the feeling, not the transcript. Being at the window with Randi, laughing, feeling clever and chosen, spoons ringing around them, becomes total. It is the best hour Vee has had in months.

When the cups are empty, Randi checks her phone and says she has a meeting, sounding genuinely sorry. Outside under the sign, she hugs Vee quickly and completely, smelling of cold citrus, with a hand flat between Vee’s shoulder blades. She pulls back holding Vee by the upper arms, face lit, and says, “This was the best. Love you, girl.” As she leaves, walking backward for two steps, she says, “See you later, okay?” Vee hears “See you later” not as a vague social phrase but as a fact, a future already scheduled by the queen. Vee says “See you.” After Randi leaves, Vee stands under the sign with her empty cup, spinning with the promise of “later.” She cannot retrieve any exact thing Randi said except “Love you, girl,” “See you later,” the hand on her back, and the fact that she has been chosen by exactly the person she would have chosen to be chosen by. She drops the cup in a bin and walks home the long way, warm all the way.

Fifth chapter, “Substitution.” Vee, Randi, and Cassie are studying at a coffee shop on Wilson. Vee has been stuck for an hour on an optional hard econ/applied-math challenge problem after finishing the rest of the set. Randi suggests she take a break; Vee says she wants to do it. Vee knows from her econ instructor that applied math often requires turning the thing in front of you into something you already know how to do, but she cannot see the needed transformation. Randi texts/scrolls on her phone; Vee assumes maybe some boy has her attention. Cassie, at the next table with headphones in, watches Randi with her flat patient look.

Cassie tells Vee that a cute guy in line is looking at her. Vee turns and sees a man crossing to their table, eyes on her face the whole way. Up close he seems ordinary-featured but somehow impressive as a whole, with a room-settling presence. He asks if she is having trouble with math and whether he can help. Vee, not wanting his looking to stop, accepts. He introduces himself as Pace. Vee automatically introduces herself by her full name, Vivienne Thorne, then borrows Randi’s earlier practiced name-joke rhythm: “V.T. Yes, like the university. No, my parents didn't plan it. Yes, they find it funnier than I do.” The bit lands, but she quickly adds that people call her Vee. Randi introduces herself to Pace only as “Randi,” without her usual adjective bit. Cassie waves and says “Cassie.”

Pace sits, rolls his cuffs back, and Vee notices his forearms and hands. She reads his clothes as oddly hard to classify: useful and unshowy, like a man who fixes things, but too good and well-cut for that simple category. Pace reads the problem carefully, asks what Vee’s professor taught as the trick for these problems, looks at her work, and says her work is right but the problem is built to send her in circles. He writes a horrible formula in the margin, says she could memorize it but should not, then makes a small crossed mark and reminds her of Euler’s formula, e-to-the-i-x. He explains that two difficult pieces, put together at right angles, become one plain thing. He gives the pencil back to Vee. She solves the problem herself in a few clean lines and feels private elation.

Vee realizes, bodily before mentally, what Pace has done: he has chosen the tutor’s path, not the mathematician’s path; he has not performed, not hidden, not solved it for her, but given her back the pencil so the solving stayed hers. Randi then asks Pace about why sample standard deviation divides by n-minus-one rather than n. Pace answers: Bessel’s correction, estimated mean uses up a degree of freedom, dividing by n would underestimate variance on average. Vee notices he answered Randi well and briefly, but answered her longer.

Pace asks Vee what she studies besides stats. She says econ. He asks what draws her to it, not merely whether she likes it. Vee gives a real answer: she likes that econ is about how people actually behave when they think they are being rational, the gap between what they say they are doing and what they are doing, and she likes applied math, taking real situations and finding structure. She talks for several minutes about a research project she has thought about but not articulated before because no one asked. Pace listens, asks good follow-up questions, does not check his phone, and laughs warmly once at something she says. Vee glances at Randi and sees her smiling like a friend watching a friend be charmed, but also holding/lifting her coffee cup without drinking. Vee explains it to herself as Randi being bored and wraps up.

Vee asks what Pace does. He says he is a grad student in math, finishing a PhD more or less. Vee is embarrassed that she warned a math PhD that her calculus was hard; he says she gave fair notice and he took it seriously. He checks his watch and says he should go, but asks, “May I call you?” Vee hears the formal “may” as a word from another vocabulary, one her mother might approve of, and she is nodding before her mouth catches up. She gives him her number. He repeats it back, pockets his phone, nods to Randi and Cassie, tells Vee “talk soon,” and leaves. The bell over the door rings.

Afterward Vee sits with her hand on the solved page and her chest racing. Randi says Pace was cute and Vee should go out with him. Vee says he only took her number and she may never hear from him again. Randi says it was cute watching Vee not understand what was happening. Cassie says Pace looked at Vee like he had forgotten anyone else was in the room and looked at Randi like somebody’s great-aunt. Randi agrees he looked at Vee that way and points out that he left without his coffee: he gave up his place in line, spent the whole time on Vee, and walked out empty-handed, which means he is going to call. Vee looks at Pace’s crossed mark, her own clean solution, and her facedown phone. She does not turn it over. She lets herself want it to light up.

**Running memory — how I feel:**  
Pace is intensely attractive and increasingly alarming. I believe his physical care, erotic skill, and tenderness in the moment, but I trust him less after “The Pointing Game.” He knows when something is “not fair” and sometimes does it anyway. He tells himself he is giving Randi something good and free, but he arranges the circumstances in which she can want, choose, and say yes. He is most dangerous when he is most confident that he understands what someone needs. In “See You Later,” his absence makes him feel even more structurally present: he helped set something moving that continues without him in the room. In “Substitution,” he becomes directly, painfully charming to Vee: respectful, competent, focused, and careful in ways that would feel safe if I did not know he and Randi had already selected her. His “May I call you?” is both genuinely attractive and part of the trap.  
Randi has my sympathy, fascination, desire, admiration, and increasing suspicion. She is not passive: she wants, chooses, assesses, maneuvers, and executes the meeting with Vee brilliantly. Her desire for women feels real, not implanted. But she also keeps having to translate unacceptable wanting into acceptable performance — girlfriend gift, sex-kitten play, bright game. Her polish is both power and hiding place. In “See You Later,” her warmth is intoxicating and frightening because it works so completely on Vee. In “Substitution,” she is harder to read: her bare “Randi,” unsipped coffee, and encouragement after Pace leaves suggest she is feeling more than she lets Vee see. I believe some of her warmth toward Vee is real; I also know it is not innocent.  
Vee / Vivienne Thorne has my strong sympathy and protectiveness, as well as attraction. She is warm, abundant, bodily, observant, and alive, but also vulnerable: she longs to be chosen by someone like Randi, reads status acutely, carries her mother’s shame around display and sexuality, and can be swept into a feeling so total she loses the words. “Substitution” makes me respect her intelligence and selfhood more. She sees method, agency, intellectual generosity, and the gap between what people say and do — which is heartbreaking because she is living inside just such a gap without knowing it. I dread for her because her yeses to Pace feel fully free and beautifully earned from inside her experience.  
Cassie interests me and I currently trust her more than most people in Vee’s orbit. She is blunt, sparse, not dazzled by Randi’s social rhythm, and her “Goodbye, Vee” felt like either warning or at least a refusal to pretend this is nothing. “Substitution” strengthens that trust: she notices Pace looking, notices Randi’s cup, and names the quality of Pace’s attention with comic precision.  
Brooke feels like a real and formidable social power: warm, polished, friendly, possibly caring, but also capable of interrogation through smiles and institutional authority. I do not know if she is malicious, but I do not feel safe around her.  
Sloane feels like social commentary in human form so far: amused, observant, classifying Pace through truck/status jokes while sounding complimentary.  
Kinsey feels younger or less powerful in the room, eager to join the warmth, but still part of the social pressure on Randi.  
Randi’s mother feels like a source of old pain around need, food, beauty, and refusal; Randi has had twenty-one years of not-going toward crying or need.  
Vee’s mother feels like another source of inherited shame, especially around clothing, sexual display, being a “nice girl,” and possibly approving old-fashioned male courtesy like “May I.”  
Overall mood: lush erotic intimacy has become a beautiful trap in motion. The book’s tagline feels fully active now: Randi’s yes is real, Vee’s yes to coffee is real, Vee’s yes to Pace’s help and phone call is real, and that is exactly what makes the situation frightening.
