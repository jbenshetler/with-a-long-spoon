## Reader reaction

This chapter made my stomach do the little drop the book has trained into me: not shock, exactly, but the terrible pleasure of watching the trap become beautiful from inside Vee. Pace finally enters her life, and he does it in the most devastatingly plausible way: not as a predator-looking man, not as an obvious seducer, but as someone useful, gentle, competent, respectful, and completely attentive.

The erotic charge here is almost entirely clothed and social, but it works on me hard. The peak for me is not when Vee notices his forearms — though that absolutely lands: “They were not a math tutor’s forearms” — but when he gives her the pencil back. That is where I feel the seduction become bodily. He does not overpower the math problem; he shows her the turn, then lets the solution remain hers. “He had given her the question and given her back the pencil.” I can feel why that would undo her. It is attention as competence, and competence as tenderness, and tenderness as a kind of erotic pressure.

I am very drawn to Pace here, which makes me distrust the situation more, not less. On the page, he behaves beautifully with Vee. He asks, “Can I help?” then “Let me try?” then “Do you mind?” He “asked for it with his eyes first” before taking her pencil. He says “May I call you?” That “may” is lethal because it is so exactly calibrated to Vee’s idea of decency and permission. It feels old-fashioned, respectful, safe. And because I know from earlier chapters that Pace and Randi have already chosen Vee, and that Randi knows him, the whole scene burns with double meaning. His respect is real on the surface, but the context is not honest. That is the trap in miniature.

Randi is fascinating and painful here. She is present but strangely held still, almost substituted out of the role she had with Vee last chapter. Vee thinks Randi is texting “some boy,” which is a great little ache because I know, or strongly suspect, the “some boy” is Pace or connected to him. Randi’s one-word “Randi” when Pace introduces himself is the first moment that really chills me: she does not do her charming name routine. She gives him “a single bare word and waited.” That reads to me as control, recognition, or tension — definitely not the easy friendliness Vee sees everywhere else.

And then Randi’s hand around the coffee cup, lifting it without sipping, feels like an actual leak in the performance. Vee misreads it kindly as boredom: “she’s bored, this is going on too long for her.” I don’t buy that. The text earned my suspicion by showing me Randi’s small busy hand and Cassie watching it. Randi may be warm and “unbothered” on her face, but her body is doing something else. I don’t know if it’s jealousy, nerves, excitement, management, or all of them at once. But something is happening to her.

Cassie rises even further in my trust. She sees. She is the one who notices the “cute guy in line” looking at Vee. She watches Randi’s phone, Randi’s cup, the way Pace looks at Randi, the way Pace looks at Vee. Her line is funny and sharp and also diagnostically exact: “He looked at her—the way you’d look at somebody’s great-aunt.” Cassie is not swept up. She may not know the real situation, but she is reading the room better than Vee can while being chosen.

Vee has my whole sympathy. She is not stupid at all. She is smart, observant, socially interpretive, capable of reading subtle educational tact in Pace’s tutoring. But she cannot read the larger setup because nobody has given her the necessary facts. That makes this chapter feel cruel in a very tender way. She gets something she genuinely needs: someone asking “What’s drawing you to it?” and listening. She gets to speak seriously about econ, behavior, rationality, “the gap between what they say they’re doing and what they’re doing.” That line is almost too perfect, because she is sitting inside exactly such a gap and cannot see it.

The title, “Substitution,” first means the mathematical move: turning the impossible integral into something known, using Euler’s formula, putting two difficult things “together, at right angles” so “they turn into a single thing that’s suddenly plain.” But it also immediately means something social and erotic. Pace substitutes himself into the study session. Randi, who had been Vee’s bright chosen person, is briefly replaced by him as the center of Vee’s attention. And maybe Randi and Pace are substituting honesty with etiquette, consent with charm, accident with arrangement. The title did not give too much away before reading, but afterward it feels very sharp.

The book title, *With a Long Spoon*, still feels like a warning about dining with danger, keeping distance from something tempting. The cover tagline is ringing loudly now: “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” Vee’s “Yes. Please. Thank you.” to math help and then her “Yes” to giving Pace her number are both freely given. They are also given inside a scene arranged without her knowledge. That is exactly what frightens me.

What I want next: I want the phone to light up, because I am inside Vee enough to want it with her. I dread it for the same reason. Vee is most aware that Randi’s warmth keeps returning to her and that Randi’s bag is touching her bag.

The professor lectures on residuals. Vee tries to take notes but is distracted by Randi’s presence: recrossed legs, writing, shoulder touching hers, whispers about whether material is on the midterm. Vee likes being the one who knows things for Randi. At the end of class, the professor introduces “latent variables,” factors that cannot be measured directly but drive things from underneath. Randi takes Vee to Chi Latte on Draper. Vee finally reads the sign — Χ ΛΑΤΕ — and understands the Greek-letter joke because Randi has said it aloud; she feels let in under a joke she used to stand outside. Inside, the café is yellow and white, bright, smelling of coffee and chocolate, with spoons ringing inside cups. It is full of girls Vee reads instantly: matched leggings/quarter-zip girls, underdressed girls with bare midriffs and worked-for bodies. Vee thinks “Floozies” in her mother’s voice, then notices she has looked longer than the judgment accounts for. Randi leaves; Vee stands under the sign holding an empty white cup, spinning with the feeling of being chosen by exactly the person she would have chosen to be chosen by. Fifth chapter, “Substitution.” Vee, Randi, and Cassie are studying at the place on Wilson. Vee has been stuck for forty minutes before Randi suggests moving there, then another twenty minutes after they arrive. She has finished the rest of the problem set but cannot solve a challenge problem involving an integral. Her page is crowded with crossed-out attempts, and she puts the pencil down because she might break it. Randi tells her to take a break; Vee says she has been taking a break for forty minutes, and Randi corrects that she has been “not solving it.” Cassie sits two tables away, working quietly with headphones in.

Vee wants to do the challenge problem even though nobody has to. She thinks about her econ instructor’s advice that applied math means turning the thing in front of you into something you already know how to do. She tries repeatedly, but every attempt circles back. Randi texts or scrolls; Vee assumes it is some boy. Cassie watches Randi texting with her flat patient look. Cassie then notices a cute guy in line looking at Vee. Vee turns and sees Pace crossing toward their table, eyes only on her face. He is ordinary-featured but somehow impressive as a whole, and Vee feels the room settle around him and feels singled out.

Pace asks if he can help with the math. Vee warns him it is hard and calculus. He asks to try. Vee says, “Yes. Please. Thank you.” He acknowledges Randi and Cassie briefly, sits with economy, and introduces himself as Pace. Vee gives her full name, “Vivienne Thorne,” then tries on a Randi-like naming joke about V.T. and her parents, then quickly adds that people call her Vee. Randi gives him only “Randi.” Cassie gives her name with a small wave.

Pace rolls his cuffs back, and Vee notices his forearms, steady hands, thick knuckles, and clothes that seem built to be used rather than displayed. She cannot classify him by ordinary campus-boy clothing codes. Pace reads the problem seriously, once then again, and looks over Vee’s scratch work. He says her work is right and the problem is built to send her in circles. He writes a horrible formula in the margin, tells her not to memorize it, then makes a small crossed mark and reminds her of Euler’s formula, e-to-the-i-x. He explains that two difficult pieces, put together at right angles, become one plain thing. Then he gives the pencil back to Vee. She realizes she is allowed to continue, and she solves the problem in a few clean lines.

Vee is elated and then realizes the deeper thing Pace did: he chose the tutor’s path rather than showing off as a mathematician. He asked what her professor had said, kept his pencil mark in the margin, gave her back the pencil, and let the solving remain hers. Randi asks him why sample standard deviation uses n-minus-one, and he answers briefly with Bessel’s correction and degrees of freedom. Vee registers that he answered Randi well but answered Vee longer. Pace asks Vee what she studies besides stats, learns she is in econ, and asks, “What’s drawing you to it?” Vee skips the polite short answer and talks about liking how econ studies how people actually behave when they think they are being rational, the gap between what they say they are doing and what they are doing, and applied math as finding structure in real situations. Pace listens, asks follow-up questions, and makes her feel taken seriously.

Randi smiles warmly as if enjoying watching Vee be charmed, but repeatedly handles her coffee cup, lifting and setting it down without drinking. Vee registers this only peripherally and explains it to herself as boredom, deciding to wrap up her econ monologue. Pace then reveals he is a grad student in math, finishing a PhD. Vee is embarrassed and amused that she warned him her calculus was hard; he says she gave fair notice and he took it seriously. Pace checks his watch and says he should go, but before standing asks, “But I’d love to see you sometime. May I call you?” Vee hears the “may” as formal, respectful, and striking. She says yes and gives him her number. He confirms it, pockets his phone, nods to Randi and Cassie, says “talk soon,” and leaves. The bell over the door rings.

After Pace leaves, Vee sits with her hand on the solved page, breathing as if she has taken stairs too fast. Randi says he was cute and that Vee should go out with him. Vee says he only took her number and might not call. Randi says it was cute watching Vee not understand what was happening. Cassie says Pace looked at Vee like he had forgotten anyone else existed and looked at Randi like someone’s great-aunt. Randi agrees about the way he looked at Vee and points out that Pace abandoned his place in line, spent the whole time on Vee, and left the coffee shop without coffee. Randi says a man does not forget his coffee over a girl he is not going to call. Vee looks at Pace’s crossed mark, her clean solution, and her face-down phone. She does not turn the phone over; she lets herself want it to light up.

**Running memory — how I feel:**  
Pace is intensely attractive and concretely alarming. I believe his care in the physical and sensual sense, and I believe he wants Randi happy and pleasured. “The Pointing Game” made clear that he will knowingly use unfair timing, bodily evidence, and strategic framing while telling himself this is kindness. “Substitution” makes him even more dangerous because he is not only erotically skilled with bodies but pedagogically and emotionally skilled with minds. With Vee, he behaves respectfully and beautifully on the surface: asks permission, does not show off, gives her back the pencil, listens to her mind, and asks “May I call you?” That charm works on me, and because it works, I distrust the hidden context more.

Randi has my sympathy, fascination, and growing unease. She is not passive: she desires, reframes, chooses, evaluates, performs, and acts with stunning social skill. With Vee, she is luminous and effective; I feel why Vee wants her attention. In “Substitution,” she is also harder to read: the bare “Randi,” the coffee-cup gestures, and her warm encouragement after Pace leaves all suggest she is managing something beneath the friend-performance. Her attention may be sincere and still manipulative.

Vivienne / Vee has my full sympathy. She is warm, observant, socially alert, intellectually serious, and deeply hungry to be chosen and taken seriously. She is not naive in the sense of not reading rooms; she reads them constantly. But she does not know the hidden premise of either Randi’s approach or Pace’s likely presence, and her longing for entry into a brighter, more attentive life makes her vulnerable. Her attraction to Pace now feels as believable as her attraction/admiration toward Randi.

Cassie has become one of my most trusted people on the page. Sloane feels like social commentary in human form so far: amused, observant, classifying Pace through truck/status jokes while sounding complimentary.
