## Reader reaction

This chapter worked on me in a quieter, more alarming way than the explicit sex did, because it is all about attention as seduction. I felt Vee get caught almost before I had time to object. She is frustrated, open, proud, embarrassed, eager; she wants the problem solved but she also wants to be seen solving it. Pace walks in and gives her exactly the kind of help that preserves her dignity and makes her feel more intelligent, not less. That is devastatingly effective.

My feeling about Pace moved sharply again. He is attractive here — very. The “ordinary” face, the controlled body, the economy of movement, the rolled cuffs, the steady hands: all of that landed. The peak of his erotic charge for me is not even overtly sexual; it is “He set the pencil back in her hand” and then Vee realizing “He had given her the question and given her back the pencil.” That is intimate. It’s an intellectual version of his sexual style with Randi: he does not simply take over; he arranges the conditions under which someone feels herself act. And that is exactly why I’m uneasy. Because from Vee’s side it feels like grace. From what I know, he is not a stranger wandering in; this is the man from the game, arriving inside a setup Randi helped create. The kindness is real, but the context is concealed.

Pace’s “May I call you?” got me too. I felt why Vee heard the “may.” It is polite, formal, old-fashioned without seeming fake, and it connects so directly to the book’s title that I almost winced. This is the polite invitation in miniature: he asks beautifully. He gets the yes. The yes is real. That is the trap.

Vee is heartbreaking here because she is so smart and yet so available to precisely this kind of attention. I loved her thinking about the math, about econ, about “the gap between what they say they’re doing and what they’re doing.” That line has teeth because she is sitting inside such a gap and does not know it. She likes applied math because it takes “a real situation” and finds “a structure in it,” while Pace and Randi are quietly structuring this whole encounter around her. Her intelligence does not protect her from being emotionally outmaneuvered; if anything, Pace wins her partly by respecting it.

Randi feels more complicated and more dangerous in this chapter. She is not dazzlingly active the way she was in Chi Latte. She is watching, managing, substituting. Her one-word “Randi” to Pace, after Vee tries on Randi’s own introduction bit, landed hard. It felt like Randi withholding the performance from him because she already knows him, and maybe because she is suddenly in a different role. There’s a little sting in Vee borrowing Randi’s social trick — “V.T. Yes, like the university” — and pulling it off in front of Pace. I felt Randi’s tension through her coffee cup: “Twice the cup came up to her mouth and went back down, and no sip happened.” Vee reads it as boredom, but I don’t. The text has earned my suspicion there. Randi is not bored. She is watching her lover court the woman she picked, and possibly discovering what that feels like.

Cassie continues to be my little anchor of sanity. She notices. She may not know the secret, but she watches the room correctly. Her line about Pace looking at Randi “the way you’d look at somebody’s great-aunt” is funny and sharp and also terrifying, because it helps sell Vee on the fiction that Pace is not there for Randi at all. Cassie’s flatness again cuts through things, but this time her observation may accidentally assist the trap.

Erotically, this is a 1 rather than a sex scene, but the charge is strong. It peaks for me around the forearms, the paper, the pencil, and the question “What’s drawing you to it?” The math tutoring itself is almost courtship. The chapter goes a little clinical only in the actual explanation of the integral/Euler’s, but even that is folded into character well enough that I stayed with it. I did feel the symbolic weight of “Put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing” very strongly — maybe almost too strongly — but as a reader I was pleased by it before I was suspicious of it.

The chapter title, “Substitution,” works on several levels for me now. On the surface it is the math move: turning the problem into something known, using Euler’s, finding the transformation. Emotionally, it points to Vee substituting Randi’s social bit for her own introduction; Randi and Pace substituting an apparently chance meeting for an arranged one; maybe Randi substituting Vee into the place of desire she could not safely own; and Pace substituting a tutor’s path for a mathematician’s path so Vee can arrive herself. It did not give the chapter away before reading, but afterward it feels exact and a little cruel.

The book title, *A Polite Invitation*, is glowing now. “Pardon me.” “Can I help?” “Let me try?” “May I call you?” Pace is all invitation, all courtesy, and that courtesy is precisely what lets Vee’s yes feel clean. The series title *With a Long Spoon* still feels oblique, though coffee spoons and sweetness are in the recent background; it suggests distance, delicacy, maybe feeding or tasting at a remove. The tagline keeps getting worse in the best way: “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.” Vee says yes to math help, yes please, yes to giving her number. None of these yeses is forced. That is exactly what scares me.

What I want next: I want to see what happens when Pace calls her. I dread how happy Vee is going to be. I also badly want Randi alone after this, or with Pace, because I want to know whether she felt jealous, aroused, displaced, proud, or all of it. My guess from here: this “substitution” has made Pace real to Vee in a way Randi may not have been prepared for.
