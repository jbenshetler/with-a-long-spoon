# Cold read (grounded) — Water Wings

*scene: scenes/water-wings.md · model: claude-opus-5 · memory: raw ch001..ch006 (pre-first-checkpoint) · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

This is the first chapter in a while that let me put my shoulders down, and then it turned out to be the one that hurt.

I love Cassie. I already half-loved her — the flat "Cassie," the ball cap, "Goodbye, Vee" landing a beat too heavy — and this chapter pays her off completely. The suit that "had a job to do," the "Who designed this? Genuinely. What man?", the refusal to be looked at and the total absence of any wound about it: "Cassie knew that, and it didn't bother her one bit." And then the two things that made my chest ache. She *had* the lungs — "used them all rec season" — and she could have pitched it up into the bleachers, and she didn't, because the thing Vee needed wasn't a fight, it was a hand. "Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman," said low, for two. And the splash: the cupped-hands trick that *shoots* it, ten feet, barely wets her, engineered for outrage and not for water. Cassie never asks, because "asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger." That's the most loving thing anyone has done in this book, and it's done without a single soft word. I trust her more than I trust anyone here.

The walk down the bleachers is nasty and perfectly built. The suit fits — "it gave her nowhere to hide" — and the towel rolled tight and carried *down at her side, not up against her*, which is a whole character in one gesture: she will not let them see her cover herself. "You won't need floaties with *those*!" and the laugh "that's really the guys applauding each other." That last clause is the truest thing about catcalling I've read in a while — it's not aimed at her, she's just the occasion. Fifty yards of held breath, and it goes out on Cassie's joke. I felt that release in my own throat.

The underwater passage is the best prose in the chapter and maybe the loveliest thing the book has given me. "The room switched off." The scull she doesn't have to ask for, palms feathering the figure-eight. "Nobody up there could see her. Nobody down here wanted anything." And the line that put a hand on my sternum: "She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem." Then she *grieves, a little* — and the page doesn't tell me for what, and I'm glad it doesn't, because I think she couldn't say either. That's the first time Vee has been alone with herself on the page, and it's underwater, soundless, with nobody selling her anything.

Which is where the dread came in, and I want to be honest that the dread is mine, not the chapter's. Vee is warm and hungry and so easy to be kind to. She has spent two chapters being chosen — the right pen, the high table, "love you, girl," the man who gave her back the pencil — and every one of those kindnesses arrived *engineered*. Randi pointed at nobody on that quad until she saw Vee laughing with her head thrown back, and Pace said "Her," and then "Go meet her." And here is Vee's actual solitude, and here is the one friend whose warmth has no design in it and gets declined at every turn — "I've got a thing," "You go." Cassie keeps stepping back and Vee keeps going toward the brighter thing. Nothing in *this* chapter is off. That's what made it unbearable: it's the only clean room in the house and I spent it counting exits.

Erotically this ran cool by design and I think rightly. The exposure is rendered as shame, not heat — the suit from sixteen, "more of her now," the mother's voice absent here but present as habit. The only flicker of charge is Cassie's "they will never know the love of a woman," which sat differently for me after "Goodbye, Vee" and the look Vee didn't try to name; I'm not resolving that, the page hasn't, but it's ajar and I noticed it.

Friction: almost none. If I push, "the walk of a girl who had *meant* to arrive" and "took it like an entrance" say the same thing twice in three lines. And the last paragraph explains the splash a touch more than it needs to — "You can't grieve and fight your best friend for a mouthful of pool at the same time" is a lovely sentence that would have been lovelier unstated. Small thumb, barely on the scale.

The title: "Water Wings" is the heckle — floaties, *those* — and then the chapter takes the insult and turns it over. She doesn't need water wings; she's the one person in the building who belongs in the deep end. And it's a child's object, which is exactly the Vee the water gives back: the one who loved this before she had a body to be a problem. It didn't give anything away; it recolored afterward, which is my favorite kind. The cover board — **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION** — is the one that frightens me. Sup with the devil, use a long spoon. And Vee has been handed a *polite* invitation, in the most literal sense: "May I call you?" That "may" is the spoon and she has no idea. No jacket copy or tagline was supplied to me beyond that cover line.

What I want: for Cassie to be at the next thing, and for Vee to invite her. What I dread: that Vee's next chapter is a phone lighting up, and Cassie standing in the shallow end saying *I'll be right here.*

**Cast present (in person):** Vee, Cassie. Mentioned only: none by name (the bleacher hecklers, the lifeguard, unnamed).

**Heat:** 1 — charge only at the edges; the body is a liability here, the exposure deliberately unerotic, with one sideways flicker in Cassie's line.

**Romance:** 2 — no declaration, but the splash instead of the question is real tenderness, and Cassie's care is the most intimate thing in the chapter.

**Motifs & images:** Water/weightlessness — first appearance, and immediately the book's cleanest refuge. The rolled towel carried down, not up — first, twin to Vee's cardigan over the better shirt at Chi Latte. "She put her face on" — repeat: Randi arranging the smile in Pace's bathroom mirror, Vee smoothing the wrongness of "Goodbye, Vee." Being looked at / the walk as performance — repeat from the Chi Latte high table and the quad. Held breath — repeat from Randi under the duvet, "the breath was held." A body scaled wrong for the clothes — the redhead the wind played with on the quad is this same body, seen from outside.

**Symbolism:** The painted dark stripe where the shallow gives out, and Vee crossing it while Cassie plants at the ribs — a line one of them will cross and the other won't. Water wings themselves: buoyancy offered to someone who doesn't need it, by people who've decided she does.

**Characterization:** Cassie deepened enormously and became a person I'd follow her own chapter about — competence (rec-season lungs), tact, unsentimental love. Vee deepened inward for the first time: we finally see her alone, ungoverned, grieving. Nobody flattened; nobody served the plot.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Ideal. Locker room, gauntlet, dive, splash — four movements, none overstayed; the underwater stillness earns the length precisely because everything around it is brisk.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** A deliberate held breath after the froyo walk, and welcome — first time the book has stopped courting someone and simply sat with a person. Momentum holds rather than builds, but it's storing charge, not sagging.
