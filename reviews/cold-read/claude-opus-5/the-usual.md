# Cold read (grounded) — The Usual

## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

I did not expect to like this chapter as much as I did, and I did not expect it to be the one that finally gets a hand on Pace's throat. Three chapters running I've been inside Vee's not-knowing, and it's been a beautiful kind of airless — the dryer room, the flannel taken off in the cold, the doorway she wouldn't cross. Then a double-wide on a county road with the jukebox dark, and a five-foot woman with two-color hair eating a third of his burger before he's picked his up, and the whole book exhales. The relief was physical. I'd forgotten Pace was allowed to be somewhere warm and easy.

Sheri. God, Sheri. She got upgraded from "Saturday miles" to a whole person in about four hundred words, and the way it's done is that she declines to be helped. "Part-time now that there was nobody behind her writing checks — she said it plainly and moved on before he could feel anything about it on her behalf." Then Paige, the design-school junior with birds and a laugh too big, and the seven-at-the-table house Sheri *liked being in*, and it ended because Paige kept picking at the not-talking-to-my-people. "Call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas." "Ironic. Isn't it." — and I don't actually know what's under that. Somebody's dead, or somebody threw her out, or both in the way that isn't either. The page doesn't tell me and Pace doesn't ask, and his one offering, "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice," is the closest thing to a thesis anybody's said out loud in this book — and it's aimed at both of them, the man who doesn't go back and the woman who won't call. With Elvis, of all people, coming up out of the machine about being home for Christmas, and Sheri not looking up: "Somebody always plays it." That's the chapter. I sat with that one for a while. Also — I'd read her remarks about women as weather, and now I don't. "I still think about it more than's decent" is appetite, and Paige recolors every sentence she's ever said about Vee's backside. I like her enormously and I'd like her kept away from all of this.

And then she does the thing nobody in this book has managed. He lays it out — every weeknight given, nothing else in the room, *most important thing in my life* — "laying it up brick on brick and couldn't stop, because every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something." And she says: "She didn't ask you for reasons." And: "What is it you want?" "I want Vee. I miss her." "So say it." "**I can't.**" Not won't. *Can't.* And Sheri doesn't let him have the softness of that: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her… from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." And he looks down, and there's nothing in it to argue with. I have been waiting forty-odd chapters for someone to price this man correctly, and it turns out it takes a woman who wants nothing off him. My feeling toward him moved, but not the way I expected — not to colder. To *sorrier*, and more frightened for both of them. He isn't withholding a word he has. Something is standing on it, and he didn't say what, and the page didn't tell me, and I'm not going to decide it's Daphne just because Daphne's the only wound I've been shown.

The thing that actually swooned me: "The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles." And Sheri flattening it — "You mean the sex is good" — and him holding the line, *that's not what I meant, but yes*, red-faced, going on anyway: she gets what the food is *for*, the idea under it. He is trying, out loud, to a friend, in a diner, to say what he can't say to Vee, and the best he can do is describe the weather. It's the most in love he's ever looked. "Lord almighty. Get over yourself" was the perfect answer and I laughed. Small ache underneath it: he hasn't spoken to Vee in weeks and he's sitting here missing her at a woman who'll steal his fries about it.

Two cold little facts I'm turning over. Sheri met Randi — Pace took Randi *dancing at the hall* before Thanksgiving — and Randi "looked at me like I was one more of your harem applicants. Never did learn my name." That's the first time I've seen Randi be plainly, casually cruel to someone with nothing to gain her, and it sat badly against the woman who wept into a mirror in Gstaad. And Sheri's "She find out about the brunette, then?" — "No. Not like that" — which means the thing is a known object out in the world now, with a shape, waiting. Every time someone else can say it in one sentence, the drop gets longer.

Trust: no new suspicion of anyone here. My unease is exactly where it was — in the architecture, not in a face — except that "I can't" added a new room to it. Friction: almost none; the chapter's short and earns its length, and the one beat that felt slightly arranged (the busboy putting the double in front of the man and the single in front of the tiny woman) is also the best small joke in the book, so I'll take it. The title, **"The Usual"**: the waitress writing before she asks, the two of them saying yes "like people saying yes to their own names" — being known so well the word isn't needed. Which is *precisely* Pace's defense to Vee, handed to me as a warm thing on the one night it's true, in the one room where it works. That recoloring is lovely and slightly devastating; it didn't give anything away, it re-aimed the chapter after the fact. **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION** (no tagline in my packet) still reads to me as *he who sups with the devil* — and tonight I couldn't tell you which one at that table is the devil, which may be the point. What I want next: Vee and Pace in a room. What I dread: Randi getting to Vee first, in January, warm and waiting, with the anthro class and the empty chair where a man used to be.

**Cast present (in person):** Pace, Sheri, the diner waitress, the young busboy who mis-delivers the plates. *Mentioned only:* Vee, Randi ("the brunette," "the sorority one"), Paige, Sheri's family/"my people," Sheri's mama.

**Heat:** 1 — a simmer at the edges only: "the air crackles," the long-legs joke, a man blushing about a girl he hasn't touched in weeks.

**Romance:** 2 — real, inarticulate devotion, but spoken to a third party and stalled at the exact word; no turn in the bond.

**Motifs & images:** Food as love and as language (repeat — the philosophy-of-the-food fight is the diner-grade version of the fra diavolo, the pizza, "crackers and a meal"); the **noise/quiet** axis — Chili's explicitly recalled, "here he could have heard her whisper" (repeat of ch "Under the Rug," now from inside his head); **the word that won't be said** (repeat, now on his side of it); being fed without asking — "the usual" as the good version of "May I choose?" (repeat, inverted); the jukebox/Christmas song (first); **going home / not going back** (repeat — "I don't go back," now doubled in Sheri); fogged glass with the cold kept outside (repeat of the warm house).

**Symbolism:** The standing order — being known well enough not to need the words, which is Pace's entire argument, granted to him here and denied to him by Vee. "I'll Be Home for Christmas" playing in a room containing two people who have no home to go to. The tabletop under glass: Elvis, a laughing family of four, a car on a turntable — preserved, amber, unreachable.

**Characterization:** Sheri deepened enormously and cheaply-earned nothing. Pace deepened by being *cornered* — his brick-on-brick accounting and his "I can't" are the most honest he's been. Randi thinned slightly, but only through a hostile witness's report, which I'll hold loosely.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Brisk and exactly right; the burger eaten slowly while he waits her out is the pacing made physical. Nothing dragged.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** A needed break in the pressure after three chapters of Vee's airless circling, and it still advanced the central deadlock more than any of them. Building.
