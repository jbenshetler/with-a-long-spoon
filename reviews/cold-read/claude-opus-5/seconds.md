## Reader reaction

Oh, that hurt. And it hurt in the exact place I didn't see the hand coming from — not from Randi, not from the arrangement, not from the thing I've been braced about since chapter three, but from Pace being *slightly off* by one word in a sentence he had every opportunity to get right. She built the thing for weeks. She checked it. "It was warm and true and it was not *I love you* — not in any shape he could take it for." She engineered a way to hand him her whole interior without triggering the rule, and he took it and gave her back — "I love how good we are together." *We.* The arrangement. The quality of the thing they have. And then, God: "What you do — what your body does — I've never." She told him in a green velvet booth that the miracle of him was being wanted *with the body attached, not the other way around*, and here, on the one morning she opened her chest, he handed her the body. She doesn't name that and I can't stop naming it.

What keeps me from settling into a verdict is the two breaths. "He took a second breath. She had not heard him do that before." And the hand that "had begun two different responses" and put both down. Something landed on him. The most exact man in this book, the one who reads a problem twice and marks a small cross in the margin, stalled — and then produced a sentence that is technically true and structurally evasive. I have my own ugly theory for why he can't say it, and it's sleeping in a sorority single across town. But the page will not give me that, and I like that it won't. Vee gets it right: "Maybe he understood and chose to say it like that. Maybe it had gone past him entirely." Both are terrible. One is worse, and I don't get to know which.

My feeling about Pace moved, and it's the first real movement since the Daphne conversation. Not toward suspicion of his tenderness — the held kiss on her hair, the deliberate rearrangement of his arm, the not-hurrying, all of it is still the genuine article, and he *wore two shirts in his own kitchen* two chapters ago because she asked. It moved toward something sadder: he may simply not be able to do this. He gives in objects. Drawers, dresses, scrunchies, worn flannel, pizza dough worked under the heels of his hands. The one currency that's only words, he does not have, or will not spend. And the cruelty of that is that Vee has been *living* on the objects and telling herself they're the same as the sentence, and this morning she found out they're not, and immediately went back to living on them anyway.

Vee — I ached for her in the laundry room more than anywhere. The dryer going round, the sentence coming back at her uninvited, "Her body did a small thing it did not have a name for, before she had decided to think about anything at all." Then the choice, made before she argues with it: *I'm not going to bring this up.* And "The cracker-and-meal would not return to her speech." That's a retirement. She made one beautiful thing and it didn't take, so she put it away forever, and nobody will ever know she made it. That's the loneliest line in the chapter. And the pile: "The place was not empty. There were other things in it already — small flickers, moments across the fall when what she knew about him had had to do small repair work to keep functioning." I hadn't been counting those. She has, evidently, without looking. That's the creak I heard in the outlier chapter, now under her own floor.

Erotic charge: near zero, deliberately, and I don't hold it against the chapter — but I'll be honest that after "The New Ordinary," which ran hot as anything in the book (the jeans fight, the bra unwinding off her like something off a spool, the sound she made "too loud and far too soon"), this is a cold porch on purpose. The only flick of heat is a bad one: his praise of what her body does, arriving in the wrong slot.

Friction: small. The coda over-tutors me a little. The broken syntax — "Hers had been about — she had been trying to say. And his had been about." — is genuinely good, that's a mind failing to complete a thought. But then it gets summarized twice in italics, "*She had said her best sentence. He had not said the sentence back*," and I'd already felt it; I didn't need the caption. Also "She had been received warmly, she would remind herself, in the small private way you remind yourself of evidence whose weight you are not sure of" is one clause past necessary.

**Seconds** — I read it first as the second coffee cup, then as his second breath (the tell), then as the second helping she asked for in the last chapter, plate held out. And then the one that's been sitting in my mouth since: *coming second.* Being someone's second. The title didn't give anything away going in — it looked domestic — and it recolored into something with a blade in it by the end, which is my favorite kind. **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION** has never landed harder than on a chapter whose emotional center is a woman saying *you fed me a meal*. Sup with the devil, use a long spoon. She's eating with her hands.

What I want next: for her to say it anyway, or for Randi to be the one she says something true to and for that to go somewhere neither of them planned. Guess from here, marked as a guess: the not-saying is now a locked room in her, and Randi is the only person she brings locked rooms to.
