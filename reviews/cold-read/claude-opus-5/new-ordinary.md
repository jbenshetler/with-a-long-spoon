# Cold read (grounded) — The New Ordinary

## Reader reaction

The bench. She threw her thong onto the bench. "It landed green on the long, unusually proportioned table that stood in the middle of his bedroom. She never looked to see where it went." I made a sound out loud. That's the whole book in one sentence and she walked right past it — the table with its top on, the thing that's furniture in daylight, sitting there in the middle of the room full of what it is. Randi's underwear lay on that top for weeks. Now Vee's is on it, thrown, careless, unlooked-at. I've been braced since the outlier record and this is the first time the dread has been *funny-awful* rather than solemn: the piece of evidence is the largest object in the room and she uses it as a hamper.

Everything else in the chapter is warm enough to hurt. The hallway strip is the best comic-erotic writing the book has done — the jeans "sensible in the car and the enemy now," the one-legged hopping with the denim inside out at her ankle kicked into the office door, "there was no dignity anywhere in any of it and she'd have burned the house down for him." And what he does with it: nothing. He stops her at the bathroom door with a hand at her waist, "no effort in it anywhere." He catches the back of her bra and *doesn't pull* — just holds it so she has to unwind out of it herself, "like something off a spool." She loses and knows she's lost and puts her mouth back on him anyway. That's the hottest kind of control in this book, the kind that costs her nothing and gets her everything: she's the one in a hurry, he's the one making it last, and the whole exchange runs on her wanting. I was completely swept.

And then her throat publishes her, too loud and far too soon — "the exact sound of a woman who had told herself a careful story about needing time to study and was being shown now, by her own throat, just how true the story had been." God, the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private. Five nights, her own doing, and the unreasonable corner that wanted him to *fight* for them. That's the ache under the whole chapter: he never asks, never argues, never charges her a cent, and the grace of it costs her more than a fight would have. That is a real, specific, unflattering thing about being loved by someone who never pushes, and I felt it.

The pizza went on maybe a beat long and I didn't care, because of where it lands: she tastes every flavor *separately*, laid out to walk down one at a time, "the difference between cotton candy and a bowl of cut strawberries." Her mouth has been asleep since she was born and now it's counting components. And the line I keep turning over — "He'd have made the pizza exactly like this for an empty house. The care was only the shape his attention took when it came down on a thing." She means it as the proof he isn't performing. It reads to me as the most unsettling sentence he's ever been given: his attention is the same instrument whether it's pointed at dough or at her. She can't say which got the larger share, and *doesn't mind*. I minded.

The flannel undid me anyway. I'd braced for the wrong hurt — his face when he thinks she's giving it back, the puzzlement and the start of it, and his decision not to ask, because "he was good at not asking things." Then she confesses the dying shirt: stale, then nothing, then washed, and "I need you to wear it for me. The whole weekend." He pulls it on over the shirt he's already wearing and stands there in his own warm kitchen in two shirts because she told him to. No note, never a note. She'd carry it away Sunday full of him and bring it back faint to be filled. It's the best romantic beat since the drawer, and it's also a small machine for staying: she has built a ritual that requires her to keep coming back, and the man supplying it is the man who has never once told her who else sleeps in that house. Both true at once. I melted and my stomach dropped in the same paragraph.

No new suspicion of anyone — nobody's *off* here. Randi isn't even mentioned, which after the library room feels like a held breath. What's off is the furniture, and Vee's ease with it.

"The New Ordinary" reads straight at first — this is what her life is now, the drawer, the drive, the door already open. Second thought recolors it hard: ordinary is exactly what the arrangement needed to become to be safe, and the bench has gone ordinary too. Ordinary is where a thing hides. *With a Long Spoon* — sup with the devil and you'd better have one — keeps promising a devil I still can't locate in any single person, only in the shape they've all made; and *A Polite Invitation* stays the coldest thing on the cover, because the politeness is real and the invitation is still open and Vee still doesn't know she was invited.

What I want next: Randi. What I dread: Vee asking, one ordinary evening, what that table is for.
