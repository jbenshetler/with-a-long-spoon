# Cold read (grounded) — hills-and-valleys

*scene: scenes/hills-and-valleys.md · model: claude-opus-5 · memory: ck-ch030 (no window) · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

I know this ride. Pace and Sheri out past his place, the cold in his fingers, her pulling away on the climb, "Two of them at once. How do you sleep?" — I've read this scene, and reading it again as "this chapter" gave me a jolt of déjà vu that took a page to settle. So let me say that first as plain friction: coming off the practice room, where Randi had just talked Vee wet in a chair with a story about a man's hand slipping, dropping back to a Saturday morning that happened *before* the apology pie felt like the book stepping backward on its own foot. Everything after this in my memory — the heels, "loaned out from the gods," the hand settling flat — has already recolored it. Which, oddly, made the re-read interesting rather than wasted. I'm not hearing the same chapter.

Because what lands now is how early he was already gone. "And then he was past the name before he'd decided to be" — that's the line. He doesn't tell Sheri that Vee is beautiful or funny or starving. He tells her that Vee ran her thumb down the grain where the arm met the leg, the joint he spent a weekend on, and *asked him how he'd gotten it to do that.* And that she pulled a math book off the shelf and didn't put it back like it had burned her. Those are the two things he chose. A man running a method would have reported the method's results; this man is reporting the two seconds where someone looked at the inside of him on purpose and wasn't frightened. "He heard himself going and didn't stop." Then Sheri, who has watched him across a handlebar for two years, says "Listen to you," and he genuinely doesn't know what there is to listen to. That's the most undefended he has been on any page I've read, including the ones with his hands on someone.

Sheri is a pleasure and she is also, in this chapter, the only adult in the book. She remarks on women the way other people remark on the weather and she means none of it unkindly; "hell of a backside on her, though, I'll give her that" is the sound of someone paying a compliment to a woman she watched glare at a floor all night and leave alone. I love that she kept an eye on Vee. Nobody asked her to. And the exchange that chilled me — "She know about the redhead?" / "She knows. She likes her." / "And the redhead?" / "Not yet." Four words. *Not yet.* He says it without flinching, as though it's a scheduling matter, and Sheri lets it be one. That's the coldest thing in the chapter and it goes by in a breath.

Then the counting, which I remember and which hits harder now that I've seen two more chapters of Vee not knowing: "she'd said her piece and was already looking down the road" — Sheri runs the arithmetic anybody could run, hands him "if that's not already complicated, it will be," and adds, generously, "but I bet it's worth it." He doesn't answer. He doesn't answer *twice* in two pages — once about Randi being finished or not, once about whether it's worth it. The man who has an exact sentence for everything, including Newton and coastlines, has nothing for either question. I don't read that as cunning. I read it as a person who has not looked at the thing yet and would rather pedal.

Where it went slack: the middle paragraph of her climbing. "The whole small engine of her standing on the pedals and driving the grade, the bleached length of her ponytail swinging across her back" — it's lovely writing and he's clearly looking, and I liked that the book lets him look at a woman he isn't going to touch. But the chapter is only a few pages and it's the one stretch that felt like scenery. The rest is so lean that the lushness stood out.

The title. "hills-and-valleys" is the road, and the going-quiet-on-the-climbs structure of the whole talk — they can only say things on the flats. It's also, I suspect, cheerfully filthy: Sheri has just given a verdict on Vee's shape, and a man who looks at a woman's body the way he does is riding a landscape named after it. It doesn't give anything away; it sits a little oblique and a little sly, which I liked. The cover board is all I have — **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION**, no tagline, no jacket copy. The spoon is the one that keeps saying *you sup with the devil at a distance*, and in a book where nobody has yet been cruel, I still can't tell who's holding the spoon or who's the devil — my current guess, marked as a guess from here, is that nobody intends to be and it happens anyway. And "A WARM RECEPTION" keeps being literal: the heat turned up hours before she arrives. I notice it every time it's mentioned and I don't like how fond of it I've become.

What I want next: forward. Back to where my memory actually ends — a woman standing in a hall bathroom with her hand held flat against her own knee, and a goodbye kiss her arms are slow to let go of. This chapter earned its existence by making Pace less legible in exactly the direction I'd given up on: not a man with a plan, a man with a problem he hasn't noticed.
