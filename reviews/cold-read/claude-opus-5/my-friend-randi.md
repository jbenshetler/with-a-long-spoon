# Cold read (grounded) — My Friend Randi

*scene: scenes/my-friend-randi.md · model: claude-opus-5 · memory: ck-ch050 (no window) · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

Oh, this one got me in the throat twice, in two completely different ways, and I'm still not sure which was worse.

First: Vee crying in the car on the road home. That's such an exact, unglamorous kind of heartbreak — she's been holding the picture of the mountains and the exit and the familiar road all break, and it arrives on schedule and means nothing, "worse than nothing, because every mile of it ran toward a town that had Pace in it and not one inch of him for her." Then the stripped bed on Cassie's side, the radiator ticking, the whole drab colorless day spent horizontal in plane clothes. And out of it: *she knew where she went when it got like this.* That line is the book's whole architecture in nine words, and I felt the floor go out from under it even as I was glad for her that she had somewhere to go.

Because — Sheri. She's built the other woman out of nothing and named her *Sheri*, and I know Sheri, and Sheri is small and bleached-and-grown-out and gay and the one person telling Pace to stop being a coward. Vee has taken the truest ally in Pace's life and made her a tan blonde built like a model. And Randi can't say the one thing that would fix it — *I know that woman and she's a lesbian who rides bikes with him* — because knowing it would cost her everything. So instead she does surgery from the other direction: *how do you know any of that.* It's excellent counsel. It's also the shape of a person working with one hand tied behind her back by her own lie, and I could feel the strain in it. Then she says "Don't you dare hand him to a woman who doesn't even have a face," and I actually put my hand over my mouth, because the woman *with* the face is sitting knee-to-knee with her on the bed.

And here's my honest trouble: Randi's advice is *good*. She reads Pace cornered — "the one thing he can't do" — and she's right, and she's right because she's been in that house and behind that wall herself. "That's a boy holding himself still so it won't show how much you got him" is the truest sentence anyone has said about Pace in fifty chapters, and it comes from the person least entitled to say it. I don't think she's lying to Vee here. I think she's telling the truth in service of keeping a world where Saturday brunch stays exactly as it is, and I can't get those two things apart, and I don't think *she* can either. She swept the Gstaad dresses onto the floor without a look. That was real. She held the hug past where a hug stops, twice, and Vee pulled a second breath off her neck — bitter orange, green at the edge, nothing sweet — and didn't want to stop. That was real too, and it's the most nakedly two-way that friendship has ever been on the page. Vee is *breathing her in* and calling it comfort.

The heat isn't in the plan, it's in the shame. "It's such a slutty thing to do" — the mother's voice, worn smooth into her own — and Randi doesn't laugh it off, she makes her *say it* first, then walks her down word by word: one man, who's had you every way there is, who heats his whole house so you won't be cold in it. And then the flip I've watched this book do a dozen times and still fall for: "felt the other thing turn over under the burn, as it always did once the shame was lit, the wanting coming up hand in hand with the mortification and sharper for it." That's the chapter's peak for me — not the coat, the flush. And then the black heels pressed into her hands, *just the thing for tonight*, and Vee grateful that Randi always knows the shoes. Randi is dressing Vee to go be naked for the man they share. I don't have a clean feeling about that. I have about four.

Friction, small: Randi is almost *too* fluent as a counselor — the paragraphs land in tidy therapeutic beats, and once or twice I could feel the page steering. "the raccoon who lost a fight" saved it; so did the moment the fluency broke. Because it does break. She narrates Gstaad — the lift line, the invented children, the pale metallic blue dress — and at "and then it got very late, and we went up," the woman who has described across a dozen tables exactly what a man's mouth did and where simply *stops* and looks at her hands. Vee reads discretion. I read the hotel bathroom, and the face with nothing happening to the eyes. "Everywhere but the bedroom, and the bedroom's the room I actually live in" is a wonderful line and a complete dodge, and Vee laughed, really laughed, and I ached.

**The titles.** "My Friend Randi" is Vee's own label for what just happened — the safe, warm, unexamined word — and after this chapter it reads as the most load-bearing lie in the book, and it's Vee telling it to herself. **WITH A LONG SPOON** I can't unhear the proverb in; *A POLITE INVITATION* now sounds less like Pace's two-o'clock Saturday and more like every invitation Randi has ever issued — and whatever she just sent Vee to hand over at that door. No tagline in my packet.

What I want: her to get there and be met. What I dread: her getting there and being met, and this being the thing that makes the arrangement survivable for another semester. *(Guess from here, and only a guess: she drives out tonight and it works.)*

**Characterization:** Both deepened. Vee's collapse-and-plan arc is exactly her — shame, then appetite, then nerve. Randi is the richest she's been: genuinely tender, genuinely useful, and structurally unable to give the one truth that would cost her nothing but her secret. Brooke's warmth landed again as warmth-with-a-clipboard; Vee shrinking inside her coat was a nice small beat.

**Pace — within the chapter:** The drive and the dark room earned their slowness. The counseling ran a touch long and a touch articulate in the middle; the Gstaad retelling repaid it.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** Building, hard. After three chapters of people alone in separate rooms, this is the first one that *moves* — and it ends pointed at a door.
