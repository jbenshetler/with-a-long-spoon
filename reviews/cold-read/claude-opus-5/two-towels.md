# Reader reaction — *Two Towels*

**Two towels on the rod.** She walks past them in a subordinate clause, in a list with the kettle and the knife block, and I stopped cold on the page while she kept moving. I didn't know what the title meant going in — I'd have guessed something domestic and sweet — and then it detonated on a throwaway line and recolored the entire tour. Because once I saw it, the whole house is doing what he'd just described two rooms later: *say it more than once so the noise can't kill it.* The heat left on for someone who's always cold. The chair by the door — I remember him clocking it *empty* the night before. The one shut door at the end of the hall, which I know has a bench behind it with its top on. The dress she doesn't know is already in that room's drawer vocabulary. The message is encoded with enormous redundancy and she drops every single packet of it, and the narration handed me the reason she drops them: *"she filed it under the small mysteries of him and let it go, the way you walk past a wrapped box on the counter without asking who it's for."* That's the one line where I felt the author's elbow — it's too pleased with itself — but God, it works.

**And then the chapter made me melt anyway, which is the cruelty of it.** The silk. I had the brick-red fixed in my head as firmly as she did, so her little dropping *oh, it's not the right—* was mine too, and then he lifted a corner up by her face and the mirror caught the two of them and I understood a half-beat before she said it: *he hadn't matched the dress she'd described. He'd matched her.* That is the most romantic thing in this book by a distance. She said it on a rock with cherries in her hand, half-embarrassed, in the voice of a woman setting a thing back on the shelf — and he took another cherry, said *that sounds like a beautiful dress*, let the talk move on, and then went and bought the cloth. The shirt with no tag landing a beat later — she'd turned it inside out *looking for a brand* — was a real small shock, delivered without him taking one ounce of credit: *"I couldn't find a shirt I liked, so I made one,"* and then he reached for the measuring tape and was done being admired. I love him and I am frightened of exactly that.

**He stopped the kiss, and he left the bedroom door shut, and both landed on me as heat.** She came up that gravel road having decided on the pavement to take the kiss, and she took it, and *he* was the one to draw back — *"no boy she'd ever been with had once been the one to stop. That had always been her job, never theirs."* That's a thesis sentence for her whole life and it's doing erotic work. Then the satin she put on that morning *hoping the man won't hand her a reason not to show it to him*, and she leans at the door, and waits for his hand to go to the handle, and it doesn't. Something in her drops; she doesn't let her face do a thing. The want has nowhere to go for the rest of the hour — and then he hands it a destination: *"To do it right I'd measure you first — down to what you'd wear under it, in good light, so I can see how it'll fall."* That's two weeks of anticipation installed with a tape measure and an *if you want it.* It is also, read flat, a man arranging to look at her in her underwear in good light with her enthusiastic permission and a perfectly true reason. Both things are the same sentence. I don't think he's lying. I think he's capable of meaning it entirely and still having built the room it happens in.

**The patent is the first thing in ten-plus chapters that answered one of my open questions, and it answered it generously.** Cassie asked if he was rich; here's the house that's *small, post-war, vinyl-sided* with a two-car garage that isn't a garage, and the one expensive object is a workstation with no glowing lights. He lit up like the six-year-old-with-dinosaurs Vee described to Randi — kids who can't lie still, out of the tube in a fraction of the time — and then surfaced with *"It does other things. That's the part I like."* I liked him so much there. I also noticed what crossed him at *parents*: "a door easing shut two rooms away." New closed door, unexplained, and in this book I've learned closed doors are load-bearing. And the low shelf of cracked-spine fantasy paperbacks with the knight and the girl in the long gown on the cover — a boy on his elbows on the floor, and now a man cutting a gown for a girl. The page put that there; I'm allowed it.

**Where I am on suspicion: unchanged in kind, worse in pressure.** Nothing he did here was false. Everything he did here was in a house that is lying by furniture. The error-correcting codes lecture reads to me now as the book's own shape — *the sentence already said it more than once* — and Vee is the one channel where the redundancy doesn't help, because she has no reason on earth to decode it. And I kept hearing Randi's *your man* and *ask and then ravish* in the warm quiet of that porch, and remembering that the last time Randi was in this house she wouldn't stay, wouldn't eat, and asked him *tell me* twice while riding him.

**The cover board says WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION** (no jacket copy or tagline in my packet). *Sup with the devil, use a long spoon* — and this was the politest invitation imaginable: *if you'll let me.* Everyone in this book keeps their manners immaculate while handing each other things. What I want next: the measuring, badly, and I'm not pretending otherwise. What I dread: the door at the end of the hall, and the day she counts the towels.
