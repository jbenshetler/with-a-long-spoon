## Felt read

Short as a breath, and I didn't want it to end. This is the second time in three chapters I've watched Vee come through a door still wearing the evening, and it works on me every time — the lowering into the desk chair "like a much older woman, both hands bracing," the legs filing a complaint, the flannel three sizes too big that she *lets Cassie look at* before either of them says anything. That's the whole chapter in its first two lines: she's not hiding. Vee, who wears a cardigan over the better shirt as an apology, came home in a man's shirt and sat down in it in front of the one person whose verdict counts.

Cassie is the reason this lands. "I had your blood type ready" is exactly her register — the state-trooper bit from Saturday, kept running like a family joke. And then she does the thing I love her for: she stops. "Cassie, who had been doing the dry thing, the file-revising thing, went still and stayed that way." "Cassie didn't say anything. Cassie was rapt." She gets out of the way of Vee's happiness. She doesn't take a swing at the shirt, or the man, or the mountain. She lets Vee have it, and then gives her the one thing she came for: "Look at you," said and meant — the same three words as the spin-in-the-bean-bag night, so now it's theirs.

The heat here is second-hand and I don't mind at all, because what Vee chooses to report is telling. "I think there's a whole situation under there" made me laugh out loud — the *reporting what I observed* deadpan on top of an hour spent behind that blue shirt. But the part she slows for is not the body. It's "he got out. Into the rain... getting soaked on purpose so I wouldn't have anybody looking at me," and then, cuff turning in her fingers, "I took my wet shirt off in front of him. On purpose. Me." The *Me.* is where I melted. This is a girl building a self out of two sentences: *I got up a mountain on my own legs* and *I did that on purpose.* Same grammar as "I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose" from Saturday. She's keeping score of her own agency now, and she's winning.

And then: "I kept the shirt. I didn't ask. He didn't ask for it back." I'd have taken that as the last line happily. It's the most romantic beat in the chapter and nobody touches it — a thing given without being given, kept without being asked for. "Keep the shirt" from Cassie is a blessing, and it costs her something I can't quite read.

Because in between there's the stone. "Vee," Cassie said finally. Soft. Not *Oh, Vee.* Just her name, like setting something down where it wouldn't break." And Vee says, "*I know.*" I don't know what either of them means. I've got two candidates and neither settles: Cassie is saying *you're gone on him* with a little fear in it, and Vee is agreeing — or Cassie is saying the thing she said in the lecture hall with "Goodbye, Vee," and Vee is answering a question she hasn't consciously heard. That second reading is doing all the work on me, because it's now the third time Cassie has let a heavy word stand without fixing it. Then she pivots straight to boots. She *always* pivots to logistics when the real sentence gets too close. I trust her completely and she is scaring me.

No new suspicion of Pace here — nothing in this chapter could generate any, it's all Vee's telling. But it's a strange ache to sit in her glow knowing whose girlfriend asked whether she'd like the bench, and to watch her hand the whole radiant account to the *one* person in the book who isn't in on it.

**The titles.** "Rye" surprised me — of everything on that mountain, the chapter is named after the bread. But that's right: it's the item she says first when she says *and then we ate*, "the way other girls said *and then we kissed.*" Dark, sour, seeded, nothing like the soft white loaf at home — a taste from a wider world, handed to her. The title says the food was the love scene, and I believe it. (The echo of *catching* somebody in a field of rye drifted through and I'll leave it as drift.) *WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION* still sits over all of this like weather: the spoon proverb means someone at this table is the devil and someone else is supping anyway, and every chapter of Vee's happiness makes me hold that line tighter. The "polite invitation" is the part that keeps getting worse — everything done to her so far has been done with impeccable manners.

**Next:** I want the induction dinner and the russet dress, which he set down and did not pick up and absolutely has not forgotten. Guess from here: that dress arrives, and it will be beautiful and it will be a bill.
