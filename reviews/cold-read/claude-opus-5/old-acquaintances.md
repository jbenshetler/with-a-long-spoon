# Cold read (grounded) — Old Acquaintances

## Reader reaction

This is the same midnight from the other side, and it wrecked me. I'd just watched Randi put her face back on in a hotel bathroom in Gstaad, warm only along the one seam where she touched a man who meant nothing, and now here's Vee in a twin bed in Ohio with her knees bent because her legs outgrew the mattress in eighth grade — and the text passes between them in front of me a second time. I already know that "you ANGEL 😇 it's honestly a fairytale out here" was typed by a woman lying next to a stranger she felt nothing with, who had just decided she wasn't brave enough to tell Vee the truth. And Vee reads it and thinks *she took the time, out of whatever amazing thing she was doing, to write back to me.* Two women alone at midnight, each certain the other is where the life is. I don't think I've ever been handed a lonelier piece of dramatic irony so quietly. No one on the page did anything wrong in that exchange. That's what makes it unbearable.

The room is the best thing in the chapter. "The room did not know her. It had known Vivienne — knew her still, in high resolution, and loved her." The ribbons, the little gold swimmers frozen mid-lift, the clarinet in the closet, the corkboard of girls who haven't texted in a year — a shrine, dusted, to a person who isn't in the bed. And her mother's name for her is *Vivienne*, which means the room and the mother are keeping the same girl. I felt the ceiling come down. And the food: the casserole and the good meat made because it's Christmas and it's love "and had never once been the thing Vee was hungry for, not then and not now; she just hadn't had the word for it then." That lands the feeding motif harder than any meal he's ever cooked her, because it's the first time it's been used against her home.

Then the cropped photograph, which I'd had as a small sad beat with Cassie in the cafeteria and which detonates here: "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it." She did that. Nobody made her. The leg out of the slit to the hip, the heel Randi knelt and buckled — the exact part where she became herself — scissored off before her mother could see it. She's been living cropped since September and only now can she see the cut.

And the thing that actually frightened me: she reaches for the best night she has, the sheet slipping at the table, the fra diavolo, the wine light — reaches for it *to be warm* — and it turns while she's inside it. The hair goes gold, the leg goes tan, and a blonde she invented out of spite is sitting in her chair in her memory while the garlic blooms. She can't keep her own night. That's the sickest, truest image of jealousy I've read in a while, and it's worse because she built the rival herself, out of the exact inversion of her own body: "a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's." Her mother's voice, wearing a new outfit. And the testimony that used to hold her up — the bartender, *wasn't him they were jealous of, honey* — goes through her hands, "because they had only ever held her up while she was the one." That's a precise, awful piece of psychology: reassurance is load-bearing only as long as you believe the premise.

Where I sit on Pace right now: for the first time I'm not defending him in my head. The silence across the whole break is his. "He left you the room. He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now." That reframes his entire courtly non-pushing as a kind of absence, and I think the book means it. The Sheri fact she folded away as pity in November comes back armed — *and about Christmas he'd said nothing at all.* I don't actually suspect him of a blonde; I suspect him of believing that not pressing is the same as being present. "Words are cheap. Believe what I do" was the coldest thing he's said in forty-odd chapters, and it's still ringing. My feeling has moved: less swoon, more impatience, and a real fear that he genuinely does not have the word to give.

Randi, meanwhile, I now love and dread in equal measure. Vee wishes she could talk to Randi about this. Randi is the one person who cannot hear it neutrally. And Vee's one certainty for the new year — "a class, a face that would light up and call her gorgeous and mean it" — is the trap closing, with everybody inside it sincere. That last paragraph, "holding both of them and setting neither down, and thought, *and what about Pace,* and nothing came after it," is the whole book in one sentence and it goes unanswered because she falls asleep. Perfect.

Erotic charge: near zero by design, and I didn't want any. The one flare is the memory of the kitchen — "the same building, then giving, that his hands worked on her in the dark" — and it's immediately stolen from her. The imagined arms at midnight that come and won't hold. This is heat as phantom limb.

Friction: almost none. The Gstaad envy paragraph is the one place I felt the author steering a touch hard — "and it did not matter that she knew what had bought the dream. She knew." — the repetition underlines what the scene already had. And I'd have believed the invented blonde slightly more if Vee hadn't narrated her own spite so legibly ("she believed it, lying there — what you believe at midnight"). Tiny. The prose is otherwise superb and plain.

**Old Acquaintances** — Auld Lang Syne, obviously, and it's the good kind of title: it hands you a New Year's cliché and then makes you count *which* acquaintances. The girls on the corkboard who stopped texting. Vivienne, who still lives in that room. Pace, who may have already been forgot. Randi, met by "accident" four months ago and already the most important face in her year. And *should* old acquaintance be forgot — the song's actual question, unanswered, which is exactly where the chapter leaves her. It didn't give anything away; it recolored on the last line.

**WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION** — he who sups with the devil needs a long spoon, and this is the first chapter where I've watched Vee eat at somebody else's table and be starved by it. The volume title's *polite invitation* keeps getting truer and nastier: every door she's walked through was opened for her courteously. No tagline in my packet beyond the cover board.

What I want next: the term to start. I dread the classroom door with Randi on the other side of it, and I want it anyway.

**Romance:** 1 — love at full volume with no one to receive it; the ache is the whole beat.

**Symbolism:** The bedroom as a museum to Vivienne — the room loves a girl who's gone, and it's *her mother's* girl. The cropped photo as self-censorship made literal. Auld Lang Syne as the chapter's unasked question.

**Characterization:** Vee deepened hard — this is the most interior she's been, and the invented rival is a genuinely new, ugly, credible register for her. Pace deepens by absence; his silence does more characterizing than his presence has lately. Randi doesn't appear and gains enormously from it.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Slow on purpose and it earns it; the forty minutes are the form. Only the Gstaad-envy stretch could lose a few lines.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** Building, and cleverly — after "Believe Me" and "Fairytale," this is the third cold bed in a row, which risks sameness, but the mirrored midnight justifies the repetition and turns it into structure rather than a rut. Momentum is now entirely in the unanswered question, and I want the term to start as badly as she does.
