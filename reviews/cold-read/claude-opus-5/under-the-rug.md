## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

That opening paragraph is the best thing in the book so far at naming what Vee's actually afraid of, and it's not what I expected: not *does he love me* but *is my feeling even real, or is it just what a man this good at it does to a woman*. "Whether all that steady, astonishing attention to her body was showing her the truth or keeping her from it." That's her asking the question I've been asking about him since chapter three, from the inside, by feel, without any of my information. It gave me a chill of pure sympathy. She can't see the arrangement, so the suspicion comes up through her the only channel it has — as doubt about herself.

Then she picks the fight, and the chapter does something I loved and hated: it makes her *unfair*. "You went cold on me after the Rusty Nail." She knows it's a lie as she says it — "some back room of her knew which of them had gone cold that night, knew who'd carried a pie up whose porch steps" — and she says it anyway "because she needed the line of it to hold." That's the most honest interior move Vee has made. And then, worse and better, she *forgives* him. "It sat well in her — *forgiven*, warm and large and easy to wear." She invents a wrong so she can be the one who pardons it, because forgiveness is a shape she can rest in and *I don't know what we are* isn't. I've watched her be brave over and over this fall; this is the first time I've watched her be dishonest, and it made her more real to me, not less.

Chili's absolutely killed me. Two separate griefs stacked: he can't hold his eyes on her because the room is coming at him all at once — and I believe him completely, I've had the boy whose desk got moved into the hall in my head since the glass porch — and she reads the flicker of irritation as *he's ashamed to be seen with me*, and the room "roared its agreement." And then the nachos. "It was to food what a billboard is to a painting." "He had ruined it for her, was the thing. Not just this." That landed like a stone. He's fed her into a palate she can't afford, into a life she can't get back out of, and none of it was cruelty. Every gift in this book keeps turning out to also be a door closing behind her.

Here's where the text earned my suspicion, and it earned it cleanly, without nudging: his explanation about loud rooms is true, I'm sure it's true, and it *does not answer the question she asked.* She said "we don't leave the house" and "will we ever be seen in public again," and he answered about noise. He is a man who cannot be seen around this campus with Vee, because Sloane already clocked his truck behind Hahn and Brooke already files his name and department with a smile. So his tenderest, most disarming line — "I'm listening now" — may be entirely sincere *and* the thing that keeps her home. That's the trap this book runs on, and it ran it on me again here: I felt the relief step off her chest and I felt myself relax with her, and only afterward noticed the question had been swapped out.

The sex worked on me. Specifically the long stay at her breasts — "his mouth working at her as though he were taking something there for himself — some ease, some comfort she had no word for and gave him anyway, gladly" — that's the only moment in the whole book where *he* needs something, and it's rendered as her gift, and it's hot precisely because of that. Then her sharp startled wish for him to quit being careful, to *lose* it; and the granting of it at the end, all his patience gone at once. And the devastating little beat at the crest: the word coming up in her again and "her body broke over the edge and took it back down, drowned it clean, as it always did, as she let it." Her own orgasm as the thing that keeps her from saying it. That's brutal and I believed it.

And then the afterward, which is the real chapter. "She belonged to him... But did he belong to her?" "What she had of him she could not have held up to anyone, could not have proved. It lived only here, in the dark, in this bed, and stopped at its edges." She's arrived at the exact true description of her situation by a route that has nothing to do with the facts. She doesn't know about Randi and she has still correctly worked out that she is a thing that exists only inside his house. The double ending — no big name, no small one, can't say *boyfriend* to her mother, can't lay him out for her father — is bleak in the good way. No rescue offered.

**Under the Rug**: perfect, and it doesn't give anything away before you read it, it recolors afterward. It's the pile from the laundry room ("The place was not empty"), now added to: the sentence he didn't answer, the lie she told about who went cold, the *forgiven* she awarded herself, the flicker in the truck she declined to turn over, and — though she'd never put it there consciously — the name MIRANDA on a screen she closed. She's a girl sweeping. The cover line *With a Long Spoon* is the devil's-supper proverb and it keeps getting more pointed: the spoon isn't long, she's eating out of his hand, and *A Polite Invitation* stays the most sinister three words on the board because everything about him genuinely is polite. Friction: almost none. The one place I felt the thumb was "the just-sex of it, proven in fluorescent light" — a shade too neat a summary for a woman mid-spiral.
