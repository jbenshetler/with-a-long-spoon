# Cold read (grounded) — Standards

## Reader reaction

**The felt read**

Four minutes of smiling and I was more tense than I was through the entire bench scene. That's the thing this chapter does to me. Last chapter was an hour and a half of a woman being taken apart with enormous care; this is a Saturday morning in a common room and it is *so much colder*, and I read it with my shoulders up. The door she closes quietly — "which was already wrong. Randi did not close doors quietly" — and I'm already braced. She's coming in in last night's clothes and the house is holding a spreadsheet and a coffee and a bowl of cereal, and all of it is a trap.

Brooke is the best new thing on the page and I do not trust her an inch, and the text earned every bit of that — I'm not being paranoid, I'm reading what's there. The not-standing. "Brooke was the chapter president and did not need to stand to stop someone in her own common room." Then the *seam*: she glances back at the laptop, clicks something, the paper is settled — and immediately, "*Pace.* That's actually his name?" That's not chit-chat, that's the second question of an interview, and Randi knows it, and she still can't stop it. "I kept thinking you'd said *Chase*" — filing the name, making her repeat it. Then "Engineering, isn't it," landing him a notch down without appearing to reach, and Randi correcting it upward (Math, PhD) like she's been hooked into defending him, which is exactly what a good interrogator wants. And then the real one, arriving pre-apologized: *you're not in one of his sections.* "The room held it." God. Brooke already knew she wasn't going to be scandalized. She wanted to watch the pause. And then the cruelest touch — Brooke ends it. "Sorry to grill you. Go." Randi had three lines assembled in her mouth and doesn't get to use one of them. "The conversation was over because Brooke had decided it was over." That's a woman being told, warmly, where she ranks.

What moved in me most, though, is Randi — and it moved because of what I saw in the mirror last night. I know what she decided in that bathroom: the face goes back on, forever, and the one he found is not coming out again. So watching her walk into this house and *deploy* the face at three of her sisters is almost unbearable, because it works. It works beautifully. She matches Kinsey's laugh pitch — "The matching was very fast. She had not had time to choose the pitch" — and calls Pace "cute," diminutive, harmless, a short-cute, and the man who knelt on the floor and unbuckled her shoes one at a time gets folded down to a white pickup behind Hahn and a word Kinsey can put away. I felt genuinely protective of him, offstage and unaware, and I don't think he'd even mind, which makes it worse.

And then upstairs, the chapter gets very quiet and very good. She can't sit. She walks to the vanity and doesn't sit, to the bed and doesn't sit, opens the closet with her hand half-raised and can't find what she came for. That aimlessness broke my heart more than crying would have. The bed "was made, because she had not slept in it." The room is cold and she pulls up the duvet and the prose stumbles deliberately: "It was not — it was a duvet." *It was not the cashmere one.* She brought that blanket to his house because she gets cold after, and here she is under the adequate synthetic one with her shoes on, and nobody had to say the word *his* for me to feel it. That's the ache of the chapter, and it's an ache about a warmth she has already decided to refuse.

Two lines went all the way through me. "*He's hard to say no to,*" the joke that landed perfectly in the kitchen, arriving "in a different key" — and the narration just stops there: "It was not a key she could sing in... beyond the words there was a quiet she had no way of crossing." I love that the book doesn't translate it. And the not-crying: "it did not feel like holding a door shut. It felt like there being no door." After a chapter full of doors — the deadbolt she turns first thing, the bathroom lock, Daphne's door he didn't respect — to land on a woman who has no door left to shut because she's been holding the breath so long it stopped feeling held. That's the scariest sentence in the book so far.

Friction: almost none. Two small thumbprints. "A woman who would one day be some CEO's third wife" is a fantastic knife, but it's delivered with narratorial certainty when it's really Randi's verdict — I'd have liked it a shade more clearly hers. And the closet palette, "bone to camel to oatmeal with the one disciplined oxblood at the end," is gorgeous but rhymes so neatly with the blue toenails-as-only-transgression that I felt the pattern being handed to me. Tiny. I'd take both again.

The title: **"Standards"** is the best kind — it looked like an abstraction and turned out to be a *job*. She sits on Standards, the board that judges conduct; the seat buys her a single room with a door she doesn't have to share, "which she had wanted for reasons she had told herself were about the work and were not entirely about the work." So: the girl who judges other girls' behavior came in at ten a.m. in last night's clothes and got judged, gently, by someone above her. It also means the standard she's held to, the standards she holds herself to, the buffed and portioned perfection — and it didn't give anything away before I read it. The cover board is all I have — *WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION*, no tagline, no jacket copy. The proverb it's leaning on is the one about supping with the devil, and after this chapter I genuinely don't know who's holding the spoon: Pace seems the least devilish person in the book. Brooke sups with a very long spoon indeed. "A polite invitation" now reads like Brooke's voice — warm as perfume, and the thing that stops you walking.

What I want next: Pace, and Randi with Pace wearing the new face, because I want to see if he notices. What I dread is that he does, and is kind about it, and that's what kills it. Guess from here, clearly marked: Brooke isn't done, and the section question wasn't idle.
