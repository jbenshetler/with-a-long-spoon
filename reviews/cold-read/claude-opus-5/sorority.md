## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

This is the happiest chapter in the book and I spent the whole thing with my stomach somewhere around my knees. That gap is the chapter. Vee walks in "fit to burst," late to everything and untouched by it, with her bed unmade as a "tattletale," and she gets to sit between her two girls in one room, and she is so pleased about it that she reads the two of them turning toward her instead of the board as the same fact — *both my friends, over a dress.* And I know one of those women turned away from the board because the night before she rode her boyfriend with her eyes shut and didn't say *tell me*, and ate a salad instead of the bowl he'd ladled for her, and said *it's selfish, Pace. How long are you going to take.* So when Randi sits in that aisle seat with "nothing at all on her face," I don't see a friend hanging on a dress. I see a woman doing arithmetic.

And then the professor puts Simpson's paradox on the board. *Each year on its own, the first man is the better hitter. Put the two years together and the second man is. Same numbers. Nobody touched one of them.* I don't think I've been handed anything in this book that landed on me harder — not as a puzzle, as a description of my own reading. Every scene of Randi's warmth taken on its own is genuinely warm. Nothing in it is falsified. Put them together and it reverses. It's doing to me exactly what it does to the room, and Vee spends forty honest seconds in the numbers and comes back up to talk about shoes.

The shoe store is the best comedy in the book so far and I loved it without reservation — the canoe off the end wall, "Do yetis shop here?", Randi coming back down the aisle a head taller in six-inch pink-rhinestone platforms, grave as a woman at serious work, turning her foot to the light and asking "Do these go with burgundy?" And the clearance flat: "this one's one brisk walk from becoming a sandal," which folds Randi over the bench into *the real laugh, the one that costs her something to give.* That's the swoon of the chapter for me — not the boutique, the big ugly store off the bypass where the ground is Vee's and she is the funniest woman in Virginia. I believed that laugh. I think Randi did too, which is the trouble.

Because then: "Which is exactly why you're not buying that." And I watched it happen with a kind of helpless clarity — the sensible tan heel, the debit card, the Thursday math, the whole architecture of a girl who pays her own way, dismantled in about nine minutes by a woman who told her the dress *wants to walk in late and stop the room cold* — which is the sentence Vee had been running behind her own eyes all week, handed back to her in someone else's mouth so it would be real. The credit card. "There was nothing under it. She knew that even while she did it." That sentence sat in me cold. Nobody lied to her. Nobody pushed. She chose it, and she will be paying for it next month, and the person beside her signed for a pair of loafers at several times the price without glancing down and wore them out of the store "like shoes were a thing you simply traded mid-afternoon." That juxtaposition is cruelty with no cruel act in it anywhere.

And yet — Randi down on one knee on the pale floor in her own beautiful clothes, waving the saleswoman off with "I've got her," lifting Vee's foot onto her knee and then *not* reaching for the box. Holding it. Turning it to the light. Looking at the frosted plum she'd already walked Vee into confessing at brunch. "Mm." One thumb across the nail of the big toe, and Vee's hand shoots out for her shoulder because the floor tipped. That's where this ran hottest for me, by a mile — a fully clothed public shoe-fitting, and it burned, because everything Randi has extracted over weeks of lunches arrives in one silent gesture that says *I know what these are for.* Randi looks up and smiles and says nothing at all. I had to put the page down.

My feelings have moved. Randi I now actively distrust in a way I didn't at brunch — "Portion" turned her, and this chapter confirms the turn without her ever doing one chargeable thing. The jealousy reading is the brilliant knife: Vee sees Randi go somewhere behind her own face at *he was up all night finishing it*, and decides it's jealousy of **him**, of never having had a man sit up a night over her — and wants it for Randi "so much it hurt." The page leaves it genuinely ajar; I think it may be jealousy of **Vee**, or of the sitting-up itself, or something I can't name yet, and I notice the dress Randi is reading the bias of, with her hand resting on the warm bare shoulder, was made by her own boyfriend. Cassie, meanwhile, broke my heart in four lines: "Your bed's made." "It's the same bed you made." She never looks at the board because *the friend was the only part of any of it Cassie had ever been able to see.* Her eyebrow goes up at the shoe date and comes back down. She's being outbid and she knows it. And Pace isn't here at all, which I felt — after "Portion" I want to know what he's doing with that covered bowl in the refrigerator.

Friction: almost none. "Slut" landing lower than Randi meant it to, and Vee's body keeping the night's account — the soreness up the inside of both thighs — is the one place the prose let me feel Friday without describing it, and it worked. The only beat I'd call thumbed is the professor's recap landing *after* the shoe date is set, a touch too neatly placed to miss; but it's so good a device I didn't mind being nudged.

**The titles.** "Sorority" — Vee's induction is an honor society, not a sorority, so the word points elsewhere: the three of them in a row, Vee "happy in the middle," a sisterhood of exactly three with one member who doesn't know the membership rules. And one of them is a professional at it: *four years of never being the one they waited on.* It's a warm title with a cold floor. The cover board — **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION** — I can't read "long spoon" without the old line about supping with the devil, and after a chapter where Vee was *ladling* the middle out of her own stories, and a previous chapter where Randi drew one spoonful across the top of a bowl she never ate, the spoon has started feeling like the book's instrument. "A polite invitation" is the whole method: nobody forces her, she is invited, and she accepts every time. No tagline in my packet.

What I want: the induction, obviously, and I dread it — guessing from here, I think Randi has just bought herself a front-row seat to a room turning to look at Vee, and I don't know yet whether she's doing it for Vee, for Pace, or for the thing she won't let anyone see. And I want someone to ask Vee how she's going to pay that card.
