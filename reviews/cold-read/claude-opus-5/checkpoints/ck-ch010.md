### Story so far
**Ch 1 — The Bench (Pace's house, evening; the woman is not named until the last pages).** A young woman lets herself into a too-warm house, bolts the door behind her, drops a monogrammed overnight bag, and kisses the man who lives there. He has sliced a honeycrisp thin, cut-sides up, because she'll eat it that way. She straddles him in a kitchen chair, feeds him apple between kisses, and hands him the evening: "What are we doing tonight." He leads her to the bedroom, where the long table she has been laying her underwear on has had its top removed: a bench he built, for her, for tonight — face cradle, chest shelf, handgrips, open middle, pivoting leg rests. She reads it, bares her throat, and says, "Show me."

He undresses her slowly from one knee, folding every piece onto a chair, lights up, and takes his time looking. On the bench he sets her word ("Red"), then spends an hour alternating spanking with touch and attention, then edging her past the point where she can bargain. Her social script — pout, turn up the heat, be *wanted* — has no purchase face-down. She breaks. The third round of *please* comes out in a voice that isn't hers-for-other-people, and he says "Good girl" and lets her come. It's uneven, enormous, and it makes her cry without her knowing she's crying. He says, very softly, "There you are." Then: "There you are, **Randi**."

Aftercare: blanket, water, fifteen minutes of being held. He says "You're beautiful" and she *flinches*. She asks for the bathroom, and — for the first time ever in his house — turns the lock. He hears her cry briefly and does not knock; he learned about doors from Daphne, long ago, at a cost. In the mirror Randi understands what happened: she has always been safe because nothing reached past the face, and tonight a man looked past it, found her, and everything after happened to *her*. She was more alive than she has ever been, and it is unbearable, because tenderness aimed at the real one can't be portioned. She decides — wordlessly, "quiet as a deadbolt going over" — that he can have the face and the one he found is never coming out again. She reinstalls the smile and goes back out. He is happy. He thinks he found her hunger and answered it and will answer it again. He asks her to stay. She says yes, and lies against him wondering what she's going to do about this. Across the room the bench waits.

Her cup gets away from her and, for once, no interior voice stops her. They walk the long way. He asks questions like her grandfather did and waits through the answers. She says the embarrassing true thing — this is the nicest anyone has been to her in a long time, and it's froyo — and he neither pretends nor inflates it: "Your tart's holding up."

**Ch 7 — Water Wings (the rec pool, with Cassie).** Cassie fights into an old navy suit she wears for its job; Vee is in the green-and-white one-piece from sixteen, from synchro, which now fits and gives her nowhere to hide. Vee walks the length of the bleachers with her face on and takes two catcalls; Cassie delivers the verdict low, for Vee only — "Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman" — and Vee's held breath goes out in a laugh. Cassie plants rib-deep and stays there. Vee crosses into the deep and goes under, and the room switches off: no noise, no being-looked-at, her old scull coming back to her hands, the one uncomplicated belonging that was never taken. She stays a beat past her lungs, and grieves a little. She surfaces with her face on wrong. Cassie doesn't ask — she shoots a low sheet of water across ten feet instead, and Vee comes for her, and they fight for a mouthful of pool until whatever came up from the bottom doesn't survive it.

**Ch 8 — May I Choose (Friday evening into Saturday night).** His text asks for Saturday dinner, names no place, and doesn't require an answer that night — and the built-in quiet is the thing that undoes her. Cassie reads her face, hears that the other boys always made her answer on the phone "so they'd know where they stood," and says: "That's not a boy. That's a man." Saturday, he comes to get her: showered, shaved, pressed broadcloth, old boots polished — nothing bought to impress, everything already looked after. She misses her stop in the elevator watching him. He walks her down a block she's never turned onto in two years. The restaurant is small, warm, and known to him; Christine seats them. The menu defeats her happily, and he asks, "May I choose?" She hands it over — "Yes. Please." — and the relief is bigger than the surrender should be. He asks only whether there's anything she won't eat, then orders in soft rounded syllables she can't repeat, and she sits wanting things she has no names for.

She hasn't eaten since breakfast (the afternoon went to dresses and borrowed shoes). The bread comes blistered and hot; she watches his hands and copies them. Tabbouleh, bright and sour; baba ghanoush, cool and smoke-dark. She stops watching herself eat and doesn't notice she's stopped. She notices he only ever uses the right hand. She notices the low background readiness for the bill to come due isn't there. She loses the thread of what he's saying and lets it go and nothing bad happens. Mint tea arrives in small glasses in little brass cages and brings her back up; the room has half-emptied around her. Outside he takes her hand — the first time — and it lands as something *resolving*, not beginning. Under a tree on the dark quad he stops and turns to her, and her old reflex arrives with its rule already in her mouth: "I don't kiss a man I've known a couple of days." He doesn't argue, doesn't lean, doesn't wait her out — he smiles like she's told him something fine and starts them walking. She doesn't go. The hand pulls taut between them and stops him. He turns back, attending. She lets the quiet run past comfortable, then: "But — I'm making an exception," and closes the distance herself.

**Ch 9 — Off Six-Fourteen (the dorm, that night).** It spills out of Vee before her coat is off; Cassie abandons her reading and gets on the bunk edge to hear it. The brass cages, the tree, the starchy rule, the fact that he simply let it go — and then the part that needs a smaller voice: *I stopped. I made him stop. And I kissed him. Me.* Cassie asks the dry floor-level question — how much did he give you to drink — and Vee says: nothing, there was tea, nobody did anything to me, I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose. Cassie believes her. Vee spins once, badly, nearly takes out the closet door, lands in the bean bag laughing at herself. There's a hike coming; she said yes instantly; she has never hiked in her life. Cassie asks his name for the state troopers. Vee counts off her dossier: Pace, lovely, lives west of town off six-fourteen past where it goes to gravel, has a trail he goes to, will bring water. "That's the whole address. *Off six-fourteen.*" Cassie asks if he's rich; Vee doesn't know, hasn't seen the house, and enjoys not knowing. She has to borrow real shoes.

**Ch 10 — Dear (after stats; Blacksburg).** Class lets out. Vee catches a look pass from Cassie to Randi and back, folded away before she can read it; Cassie leaves easy — "I'll see you at the dorm." Randi grabs Vee's wrist, can't recall Pace's name at first ("Paul?"), and demands a full report with a beverage. Vee explains Cassie's absence — practice, and the smoothie place "isn't really her place" — and lets the small thing that turns over in her lie where it is. At the Smoothie Bird, a bright matched room of identical girls, Vee's cardigan covers her like an apology and the window's high table sets her up on a shelf. Randi orders a bruise-colored beet-wheatgrass thing like a sentence handed down; Vee orders strawberry-banana and swaps in oat milk because Randi is standing there. Randi opens with her own catastrophe — a glossy Friday date who talked about Switzerland and lacrosse all night — landing the moral: a man who won't listen to you across a dinner table won't take care of you anywhere else.

Then Vee tells it: the block she never knew, the unreadable menu, and "May I choose?" — "May I. Not can I. Who even says that." Randi holds it up to the light: "You let him choose for you. And you liked it." Vee's "…Yeah" comes out small; she hurries to make it ordinary ("just nice, not having to be in charge"), and Randi lets her have it and goes no further. Vee keeps giving — the way he goes six years old about a bridge, how good it was just to listen — and then, flat and proud: "I kissed him. Two days I'd known him." Randi refuses Vee's dignified version ("I made an exception") and offers the truer, smaller one: *because you couldn't not. Because of him.* Vee agrees.

Brooke arrives with a taller silent woman who looks Vee over twice. Brooke's "We missed you Thursday" lays a whole private week on the table that Vee is no part of. Asked who the new friend is, Vee shrinks herself to "we have stats together" — and Randi says, "Vee's my dear friend," taking Vee's hand on the white tabletop in plain view and keeping it there. Brooke's smile doesn't change; she floats a dinner that means nothing and goes. Vee can't quite steady her cup and apologizes for something she can't name; Randi knocks a knee against hers — "You were perfect" — and the fright is answered. Then Randi has to go be a person; the hug on the sidewalk, cold citrus, hand flat on Vee's back, "Love you, girl." Vee walks home into the gold afternoon feeling picked.

### Impression
**Vee** — I like her enormously and I'm frightened for her. The pleasure of her is how *deserved* all of it feels: she is generous, quick, funny by accident, decent to Cassie, and starving in a way she's too well-mannered to name. The kiss under the tree was hers and it was glorious. I have no wish for anyone to take it back from her, which is exactly the problem.

**Pace** — I can't get him to resolve, and I've stopped expecting to. Everything he does *to* people is kind and everything he does *about* people is engineered. He waited outside a locked door because he learned from Daphne; he gave back the pencil at the exact right line; he left a water bottle out in advance. He also stood at a light pole and watched a woman he loves go acquire a girl for him, and was happy. I trust his tenderness completely and his intentions not at all, and I notice those two sentences fit together comfortably, which unsettles me more than either one alone.

**Randi** — the one I keep feeling sorry for against my better judgment. Chapter 1 gave me her whole architecture before I knew her name, and then made me watch her wall it up. She is running an operation on Vee, and she is also, plainly, falling into something she has no permission from herself to feel. The bottle cap under the thumbnail is the detail I can't put down. Wariness, yes — and real tenderness, which I suspect is the trap.

**Cassie** — the only person here I'd trust with a secret. Nothing she has done is for effect. She declined the name-bit, she kept the insult off the bleachers and gave Vee the joke instead, she splashed instead of asking, she didn't tease about the thing that mattered. The flat "Goodbye, Vee" is the loudest sound in the book so far and nobody heard it but me.

**Brooke** — pleasant, unhurried, never stands up. I don't dislike her. I would not want her asking me where I'd been.

Where trust sits overall: I have no reason to doubt Vee, Cassie, Meg, or Christine, and I don't. I have every reason to doubt Randi and Pace, and I'm charmed by both of them anyway — which is, I think, the honest answer.
