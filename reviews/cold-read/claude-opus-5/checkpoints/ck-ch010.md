Fifty minutes on residuals that Vee lets go by, because the better thing is at her elbow; the professor's last words are "latent variables," which she writes down and underlines. Randi commandeers them both for Chi Latte — Χ ΛΑΤΕ, the gold Greek sign Vee has walked under forty times without being in on the joke. Cassie declines: "I've got a thing." And then, flat and whole: "Goodbye, Vee." Vee laughs it off — *it's coffee, weirdo.* Inside, Vee reads the room: matched girls, two mostly-bare ones she labels in her mother's voice while looking a beat too long, and the private arithmetic that she is the most of what the room is selling and the one with the most fabric on. **Ch 5 — Substitution.** The coffee shop on Wilson. Vee is an hour into a challenge integral that keeps circling back; Randi suggested the venue, tells her to leave it; Cassie works at the next table with headphones in. Randi texts someone with absorbed focus; Cassie watches her do it with the flat *she's not serious* look. The bell rings. Cassie: "That cute guy in line is looking at you." Pace crosses the floor with his eyes on Vee the whole way, asks if he can help with the math. She borrows Randi's name-bit — "Vivienne Thorne. V.T. Yes, like the university" — lands it, then asks for *Vee* back. Randi gives him one bare word: "Randi." He rolls his cuffs, reads the problem twice like something new, asks what her professor said the trick was, writes the ugly closed-form in the margin and tells her not to memorize it, marks two short strokes at a right angle, names Euler's formula, and puts the pencil back in her hand. She solves it in a few clean lines and feels what he did a second after her body does. He answers Randi's Bessel's-correction question correctly and briefly; Randi's hands work her cup, lifting it to her mouth twice without sipping. He asks Vee "What's drawing you to it?" and listens through five minutes of a research project she's never articulated to anyone, because no one asked. He reveals he's finishing a math PhD; she dies laughing at having warned him her calculus was hard. Then: "I'd love to see you sometime. May I call you?" She hears the *may*, is nodding before her mouth catches up, gives the number, and he goes. Cassie: "He looked at her the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." Randi: he gave up his place in line and walked out of a coffee shop empty-handed — a man doesn't forget his coffee over a girl he isn't going to call. Vee leaves the phone facedown and lets herself want it to light up.

**Ch 6 — The Long Way.** Twenty minutes later it lights up: a number she doesn't know, asking about frozen yogurt. She looks for the flaw ("is he desperate?") because wanting is harder to sit in; Randi reads the text and says he looked like a man who wants her, and "you should go." Cassie needles about the ex she kept alive as a healer until two in the morning for a semester. Vee invokes the wait-a-day rule; Randi: "The rule's for boys you're not sure about. You're not unsure." That afternoon, under the neon and dusty plastic ivy, Pace explains his froyo system — tart on the bottom holds up under the heavy ones — and admits he's thought about most things. Vee's cup gets away from her and her mother's voice doesn't come, and she finds she doesn't care. He pays for both. They walk the long way, nowhere. He asks like her grandfather used to ask and waits out her sentences. She says the embarrassing true thing — the nicest anyone has been to her in a long time, and it's froyo — and asks him to pretend she didn't. He doesn't pretend and doesn't make it large. "Your tart's holding up."

**Ch 7 — Tannin.** That evening Randi comes to the house, locks the deadbolt, and is on him before he's standing, no hello in it. "Tell me." One glass of red, poured and left to open an hour before, because he knew she'd come. She straddles him on the couch, drinks, and kisses the wine into his mouth; he never pours himself any. She pulls his hands under her top and closes them on her breasts, and puts one hot word in his ear: "*Tell.*" He gives her the froyo, the walk, and the cup built like nobody had ever told her when to stop — and feels it land in her thighs before it reaches her face. "Did she eat all of it?" "Did she make you laugh?" "No. I made her laugh. She's easy to listen to." He keeps Vee's one true sentence back: it was hers. Randi goes level and quiet, the smile a beat late over a want too big for her face. He draws her down: "She's fun. You'll like her." "I already do," bright and easy — then she finishes the glass in one swallow, kisses the last of it into him, stands, and holds out her hand. He had not had a drop of it and could taste nothing else.

**Ch 8 — Water Wings.** Friday afternoon, rec pool, real suits required. Cassie wrestles into a navy suit older than the friendship and does not care how she looks in it. Vee wears the green-and-white one-piece from sixteen, from synchronized swimming, with more of her in it now and nowhere to hide. The only route to the water runs the bleachers, so she takes it as an entrance, towel rolled and held at her side: "You won't need floaties with *those*!" — then "Legs for *days*." Long blink, eyeroll. Cassie, low and only for Vee: "Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman," and fifty yards of held breath goes out of Vee at once. Cassie plants at rib depth and goes no further. Vee crosses the dark stripe into the deep and goes under, and the room switches off — cold from below, light in sheets from above, her hands finding the old sculling figure-eight, nobody able to see her, nobody wanting anything. She stays past when her lungs ask, and grieves a little. Surfacing with her face on wrong, she gets a low sheet of water shot across ten feet, and the splash fight takes whatever she carried up from the bottom. The body that was such a problem waits up on the deck with the towel.

**Ch 9 — May I Choose.** Friday evening, chlorine still in her hair, Cassie on the bottom bunk: his text. Saturday, dinner, no venue, no answer required tonight. The room he built into it undoes her — the other ones always made her answer on the phone, right then, so they'd know where they stood. Cassie, not teasing: "That's not a boy. That's a man." Saturday he comes to get her; she watches him across the dorm lobby before he sees her — showered, jaw faintly razor-blushed, pressed broadcloth, old boots kept polished, nothing bought to impress her — and misses her own elevator. He doesn't make her account for the smallness in her voice; asks if she minds walking; doesn't say where. A split block she's never turned down in two years; a warm doorway; Christine, who knows him. Vee hasn't eaten since breakfast (the afternoon went to dresses, Cassie's verdicts, borrowed shoes) and the hollow finds her at the door. The menu is one dog-eared page of words she doesn't know; she points at hummus and lets the rest defeat her, openly. He sets his menu down: "May I choose?" The relief of handing it over goes further than she'd have guessed. He asks only whether there's anything she won't eat, then orders unhurried, building it — soft rounded syllables she can't repeat, *baba ghanoush*, *tabbouleh*, promises she can't read aloud. The food comes crowding in, the blistered bread last and hot. She watches his hands first, tears bread as he does, and the bright sour green one goes down into a hollow older than noon. She stops watching herself eat and doesn't notice she's stopped. The dark smoky one, cool under its red-brown spice and pale nuts, folds her under. Always the right hand, never the other. The low background readiness for the bill to come due simply isn't there. She loses the thread of what he's saying and lets it go, and nothing bad happens, and the not-needing-to-follow is the most restful thing she can remember. Mint tea in brass-caged glasses brings her up; the room has half emptied and it's late. Outside he takes her hand — the first time — and it is too much for what it is, and feels less like a beginning than a resolving. Under a tree on the dark campus he slows and stops, and the old reflex arrives with its rule already in her mouth: "I don't kiss a man I've known a couple of days." He doesn't argue or lean or wait her out; he smiles as though she'd told him something fine and starts them walking. She doesn't go. The hand pulls taut and stops him; he turns back, attending. "But I'm making an exception." She kisses him small and light; he answers only as much as she brought and eases back a breath, leaving the next move hers. She stands in it, lets herself want it, then leans in and takes it — and this time he meets her all the way, his hand to her face, the mint in both their mouths. She grins inside the kiss and feels him smile.

**Ch 10 — Dear.** That night, through the two-inch crack of Cassie's window, Vee tells all of it, voice dropping for the tree: she said the starchy true thing, he let it go, and *she* pulled him back and kissed him. Cassie, dry-floored as ever: "How much did he give you to drink?" — *nothing, we had tea, nobody did anything to me, I did it because I wanted to and on purpose.* Vee spins once, nearly takes out the closet door, lands in the bean bag. He asked her hiking; she said yes too fast; she'll need real shoes, which she'll borrow. Cassie wants an address for the state troopers and gets: west of town, off 614, past where it goes to gravel. "Is he rich? Rich people hike." Vee doesn't know and isn't troubled — it goes in the pile of things she gets to find out. **Six days later:** stats lets out; Cassie, already up with her cap on, catches the look go past her to Randi and folds it away — "I'll see you at the dorm." Randi has Vee by the wrist and forty minutes: "How is it going with Pace" (after fumbling for the name — "Paul?"). Vee half-excuses Cassie's absence: practice, and the smoothie place isn't really her thing. Vee thinks it's fine to have your own people; something turns over underneath and she lets it lie. At the Smoothie Bird — matched girls, bare arms, Vee's pilled cardigan covering her like an apology, a high white table in the window where she's set on a shelf — Randi leads with her own "catastrophe": a Friday date, tall, dreamy, money, a candlelit French place, who talked about himself all night, Switzerland and lacrosse numbers; "a man who can't be bothered to listen to you across a dinner table is not going to bother taking care of you anywhere else." Then she turns the whole of her face on Vee, and Vee hands it all over: the unreadable menu, *May I choose*, the low soft names, the night going soft, "I stopped keeping track of myself." Randi holds it up to the light: "You let him choose for you. And you liked it." Vee: "…Yeah," then tries to shrink it to *it was just nice.* Randi lets her have it and goes no further, and Vee feels the letting-go as a kindness. Vee gives her more: how he goes six years old about a bridge, how good it felt just to listen. Then: "I kissed him. Two days I'd known him. I do not do that." Randi, near tender, re-writes it: "Because you couldn't not. Because of him" — and Vee's dignified *I made an exception* leaves her as the smaller truer thing. The hike delights Randi: a man who wants you to himself, hair down, no bra, ravished against a tree (Vee, flat, lifting one breast: "I'd black my own eye"); they laugh together on their shelf. Vee, quieter, hands running down her thighs, up the trail already: "He'd ask, though. Before he ravished me." Randi, a beat late and at Vee's own volume: "Ask and then ravish." Then Brooke arrives with a taller woman who never speaks and runs a slow circuit of Vee twice. "We missed you Thursday" lays a whole private week on the table that Vee is no part of; the smile takes her measure and stays warm. "And who's your new friend?" Vee makes herself small — a girl from a stats class, no one's business — to leave nothing worth holding against Randi. Randi: "Vee's my dear friend," and takes her hand on the white tabletop in plain view and is in no hurry to let go. Vee cannot name what goes through her, only that it cost Randi something Randi didn't appear to count, and that no one has chosen her out loud in a long time. Brooke: "We should all get dinner sometime," which is lovely and means nothing. Vee studies the money in Randi's brows until she catches herself. They go. Randi's phone calls her away; on the sidewalk the hug, the hand flat between the shoulder blades, "Love you, girl." Vee walks home with an empty pink cup, not a thing the room can look at but a girl who has been picked, and carries it the whole way.

### Impression
**Vee** — I like her so much it's a problem. She's funny on accident, smart in a way nobody has asked about, and so plainly starving that the first man who waited out one of her sentences has reorganized her week. The pool chapter is where I started actually caring rather than just watching: the entrance down the bleachers, the long blink, the grief at the bottom of the deep end. I want her to have the dinner and the hike and the hand. I am frightened for her in a way she is not frightened for herself, and the fear isn't of Pace's behavior — it's of what it will do to her when she learns she was *pointed at*. She has never once in these ten chapters been loved without also being arranged.

**Pace** — this is where I can't make the two halves meet, and I'd rather say so plainly than resolve it falsely. Everything he actually *does* on the page is careful: the sliced apple, the safeword asked for twice, the water bottle left out in advance, not knocking on a locked door, the pencil put back in a girl's hand at the exact line where the solving stays hers, the last inch of a kiss left where it was, and — the detail I keep returning to — keeping Vee's one naked sentence out of his report to his lover. I have no evidence of cruelty. What I have is that he lifts a top off a table before she arrives, that he asked a question in bed to see what it would open, and that he stood at a light pole and watched a woman be procured and found it went to the root of him. He is not a liar; he is a man who arranges things and then tells the truth inside the arrangement. My wariness is about scale, not sincerity: he may be the kindest person here and still the one who set this in motion.

**Randi** — she moved me more than I expected to be moved by her. The jacket let me expect a predator and the first chapter gave me a girl at a mirror who had just been loved past her own defenses and chose, alone, in a locked bathroom, to never let it happen again. The bottle cap under the thumbnail. The not-crying that isn't a held door but no door. She is doing something to Vee that I don't trust at all, and the wanting underneath it is the only unperformed thing she owns. I feel pity, and attraction, and a steady low alarm. The "dear friend" with the hand on the table may have been the truest sentence she has said in ten chapters, and she said it as a move.

**Cassie** — the one I trust without reservation. Everything she says is load-bearing. She is also, I suspect, in some quiet pain the book hasn't opened yet, and she said *Goodbye* with the whole word in it.

**Brooke** — friendly, seated, filing. Nothing hostile has happened and I don't like her.

Overall: suspicion sits almost entirely on the *architecture* — a plan nobody on the receiving end knows about — rather than on any one person's cruelty. Trust sits, oddly, in the small physical kindnesses, all of which have been real so far. Every yes has been freely given. That is exactly what's making me nervous.
