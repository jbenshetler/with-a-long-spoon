**The caring side (equally true)**

- He lined only the bodice and hips of the dress — a soft wall set into exactly the parts of her the dress would otherwise give away.

- Randi loves Vee — not as currency, with no private qualifier, the first time in her life. She knows what she feels, knows it is more than she has ever felt for anyone including Pace, and will not risk saying it because losing Vee is the one loss she is not brave enough to take. Vee reads "love you, girl" as sorority warmth.

### Story so far
**Before Vee.** Pace, 22, lives alone in a small warm house on gravel outside town, finishing a math PhD, licensing an MRI patent, building his own furniture and sewing his own shirts. He has been seeing Randi — a sorority Standards officer with a monogrammed bag, a Mercedes, and a face she has been using since she was thirteen. In ch 1 he undresses her in lamplight and puts her on a bench he built for her, spanks her, edges her past the end of her performance until she begs in a voice that is actually hers, and makes her come; she cries, and he says, "There you are, Randi." She locks his bathroom door — a first — looks at her own face, and decides that the woman he found is never coming out again. He holds her afterward, quietly delighted, believing he has opened her. In ch 2 she carries the night home past Brooke's pleasant interrogation and lies on her made bed in yesterday's clothes and does not cry.

**The game.** In bed (ch 3), Pace asks Randi whether she'd like to taste another woman; her body answers before she can, and she reframes the want as a gift to him: "Only in a threesome. With you." They walk the quad and he points at women; she rejects each. Then a tall, red-haired girl comes out of the dining hall laughing with her head thrown back, and Randi stops mid-stride and says, bare, "Yes." Pace tells her to go meet her; Randi engineers a shoulder-collision so clean the other girl apologizes, and comes back glowing: *Her name is Vee. And we've got a stats class together.*

**The approach.** Randi takes the seat beside Vee in stats (ch 4), charms her and Cassie, takes Vee to Chi Latte and leaves her spinning with "Love you, girl. See you later." Days later, at a coffee shop, Pace crosses the room to a stuck calculus problem (ch 5), shows Vee Euler's identity, hands the pencil back so the solving stays hers, listens to her talk about econ, and asks, "May I call you?" He texts within twenty minutes; they get frozen yogurt (ch 6). He takes her to a small Middle Eastern place, asks "May I choose?", orders in a language she can't read, and walks her home the long way; she says she doesn't kiss a man she's known two days, then backs him into a tree and does it anyway (ch 8). She tells Cassie every word (ch 9).

**Fall.** Randi takes Vee to a smoothie place and, in front of Brooke, calls her "my dear friend" and holds her hand on the table (ch 10). Pace takes Vee hiking (ch 11): the wrong boots, the moleskin, the summit over a silver lake, horseradish sandwiches, cherries, "we take nothing and leave nothing." Up there, half-embarrassed, she mentions a russet silk dress from a magazine she'll never afford, for her honor society induction; he says, "That sounds like a beautiful dress," and lets it drop. On the way down it pours; in the truck he hands her his only dry shirt and gets out into the rain so she can change. She knocks on the glass, calls him back, holds his eyes and unbuttons her wet shirt for him. He looks and does not move. A campus cop's siren ends the night. She keeps the shirt.

**The dress.** He invites her to his house at two on a Saturday (ch 13–14). She tours it — the furniture he built, the cookbooks and fantasy paperbacks and math texts, the patent that shortens MRI scans, the spotless shop — and on the sun porch finds a bolt of burgundy silk held up to her face. He matched the color to *her*, not to the magazine. She realizes he made the tagless shirt she's been sleeping in. He offers to build her the gown and needs to measure her (ch 15): shirt off, bra off, on a riser he made, in a house he heated hours early. She gets wetter and wetter and finally shuts her eyes in terror of being found out — and opens them to find him on his knees, chin tipped all the way up, waiting for her eyes, delighted. Something in her posture changes permanently. She starts spending the power; a pin pricks her; he kisses the blood away and says, low, "Keep still."

**Opening.** At lunch in a dark green booth (ch 17), Randi tells a story about being tied to a headboard and tickled, then draws Vee's fitting out of her piece by piece — "nobody measures over a bra," "all the best ones start at the left hip," "what did he *see*?" — then closes the door gently just as Vee is about to give up the secret. In the car she kisses Vee on the mouth for the first time; Vee explains it away as a French thing and scans the dorm windows for witnesses.

Vee buys a green satin set and paints her toenails frosted plum, and drives out (ch 18). They don't make it past the kitchen. He goes down on her standing; he asks, on his knees, "Would you be bothered if I was too hard?"; afterward he cooks fra diavolo while she sits at his table in his top sheet, and she folds her ruined panties in thirds and puts them in his hand: "Trade." She gives Cassie a cleaned-up version (ch 19) and Randi a fuller one over mimosas (ch 20).

The dress is finished (ch 21): she puts it on, finds the slit runs to the hip, takes off her underwear so nothing crosses the line, and strides down the hall to him. They have each other standing up, then fight the silk off her in a ridiculous, laughing relay before she tows him to the bed. Randi takes her shoe shopping (ch 22–23) — the yeti bit, the identical size sevens — and steers her past the sensible tan heel to a boutique where Vee puts the most expensive purchase of her life on an emergency credit card because "the shoes were what the dress was owed."

**Deepening, and the cost.** In Pace's bed (ch 24) Randi tells him she didn't expect to like Vee this much, that Vee's got it bad, that the girl hides in a cardigan; he says somebody taught her to be ashamed; Randi says Vee isn't ready and he says he wants her to get there on her own. By the fire with Vee (ch 25) he gives up Ohio-sized pieces of himself: Peter, 22, the year his teacher put his desk in the hall, college at fifteen, Daphne the assigned tutor who was nineteen — "It was the best two years I'd had. And then it was over" — and the scar on his thigh: "Not tonight."

He teaches her to three-step on a concrete pad with a boom box and takes her to the Rusty Nail (ch 26), where everybody knows him and a blonde tells Vee that *all us girls love him* and he's *so good about getting a girl home safe*. Vee throws it in his face in front of the room; he answers straight, "Yes. Sometimes. When it's required." She walks off; he sets down both drinks and leaves. The bartender tells her the truth afterward: he leaves them at the door, he doesn't bring dates, the girls were jealous of *her*.

She goes to Randi's sorority room (ch 27), gets coached — wait a day, go in sure, not sorry — and is lent cognac heels and a green-and-gold scarf, dressed and knelt to and kissed goodbye. Sheri, cycling with Pace (ch 28), clocks his new tone about "Vivienne" and warns him that two at once will get complicated. Vee apologizes on his porch (ch 29) with a store-bought "HUMBLE PIE," taller than him in the heels; he says, "Leave them on," and thanks her for telling him what the blonde said. The next morning, tickling at the sink, he swats her wet-handed and watches something enormous cross her face; she covers it with sex and won't name it, and comes twice while his hand holds the place he hit.

Randi takes her to an empty music practice room (ch 30) and mines the swat out of her, then tells her own story — over his knee, counting aloud, the hand that "slips" — until Vee has to leave for the bathroom and stops her own hand before it finishes.

**Peak.** At the induction (ch 31), with Pace in Berkeley and nothing under the burgundy, Vee takes the second step long and gives the room her whole bare leg on purpose; Cassie photographs it; Vee sends the uncropped frame to Pace, who will never know what else is in it. On a Friday (ch 32) she pantses him, loses a bet about whether she enjoys being spanked — he checks, with one finger, and holds up the proof — and is stripped slowly, spanked properly, and afterward given juice, her head in his lap, and the word *wonderful* until she sleeps. She finds a whole form of green scrunchies on his counter and decides to start bringing a bag. On Halloween (ch 33) she models the dress, then comes back down the hall in nothing but the heels: "And this is what I had on under it."

Over brunch (ch 34) Randi walks her all the way up — the bet, the bare bottom, the checking, the covers — then describes touching herself alone in a stranger's bedroom, and Vee has to go to the bathroom and stops herself again when Randi's face appears behind her eyes. She comes back to "Sticky fingers?" and a plate of pastries, and cannot tell whether she has been caught or offered a door. On the sidewalk Randi says Vee is her best friend in the world and kisses her; Vee's lip tastes of a glaze she never touched.

**The gaps.** Pace clears his top bathroom drawer and folds his whole life into one below it so she'll have the easy one (ch 35). Cassie notes she doesn't come home school nights (ch 36). Vee swims a hard set to take the words off "Do you love him" (ch 37). She lets Pace photograph her, first cropped, then all of it (ch 38), and hits a wall when she asks about his parents: "I don't go back." Sheri, alone, is coming for Thanksgiving. Over brunch (ch 39) Vee describes how much and how often and how *seen*, and Randi's eyes go bright and unfixed — a catch Vee reads as sentiment — and they sign up together for a spring anthro class.

In a glass study room (ch 40), the stats project — Cassie's PPP thesis, Randi's counterexample, Vee's model — turns up one huge outlier in Randi's home county, and the record opens on the name MIRANDA. Randi goes white and says, ragged, "Cheating bastards." Cassie, who can't see the screen, takes it as agreement. Vee sees everything, says nothing, hands Randi busywork, and never mentions it again.

**The crack.** Vee takes five school nights away and lasts five (ch 41); she gives Pace the washed flannel and asks him to wear it all weekend and give it back full of him, and he pulls it on over his shirt right there. Home for Thanksgiving (ch 42) she tells her father the parts that fit and crops the photo at the waist, and admits to Cassie she can't say what Pace is to her — both can't and won't. On the cold porch before dawn (ch 43) she gives him her best sentence: *I had been eating crackers my whole life, and you fed me a meal.* He takes two breaths and says, "I love how good we are together." She lets it go and files it somewhere not empty.

A bad night (ch 44): she asks whether they're only sex, he calmly takes her to Chili's, can't track her voice under the noise, and she reads it as contempt; the nachos taste of nothing because he has ruined that for her too. At home she accuses him of punishing her; he answers, quietly, "You know what happened after the dance," and then, "That restaurant — it's too loud… Somewhere quieter, I can. And I do. I'm listening now." She forgives him, and it isn't enough afterward, lying awake marked all over and still unnamed.

In her bunk (ch 45) she strips off his shirt, finds the warmth in it was only her own, takes it off entirely, and lies bare in the cold until morning. Then she drives out (ch 46) and doesn't cross the mat: *What are we? What am I to you?* He says she's the most important person in his life, asks whether any friend of hers has a relationship she'd rather have, and finally: "Words are cheap. Believe what I do." She says, "Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me." He folds his arms and says nothing. She cries, he reaches, she leaves.

**The break.** Gstaad (ch 47): Randi endures her parents, invents boyfriends for her father, is picked up in a lift line by John, skis with him, buys a dress for a white-tie ball, dances led so completely she never has to guess, is kissed at midnight under falling silver, taken up to a hotel room without being asked, and finds it means exactly as much as a sandwich eaten fast. She is warm only where she touches him. She thinks: *Pace on his worst night reached further than this.* And she thinks about Vee across a table on a nothing Tuesday, and knows what it is, and knows she will not say it, because she is not brave enough to lose her. Vee's text arrives from Ohio; Randi sends back the fairytale, true in every word, with one honest line at the end.

Ohio (ch 48): Vee lies in a bedroom that still belongs to a girl who no longer lives in her — ribbons, trophies, the clarinet — with the year running out, no word from Pace across the whole break, no name for what they were and no standing to ask, imagining a blonde in her seat at his table in the sheet. She counts Randi as the one sure thing January holds, and falls asleep still reaching for the thought that begins *and what about Pace.*

At a county-road diner (ch 49), Pace tells Sheri he misses Vee, that Vee wants a declaration, that he can't. Sheri: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." He has nothing to argue with. Alone (ch 50), hungover and sick, he walks back through it: the porch, the true answer that rose, the smaller one he chose; Daphne, the only woman he ever said it to, and how her leaving consumed him; the rule he built afterward that the words are the difference. A truck sounds on the highway and his whole body turns toward the drive. It doesn't slow. He breaks down over the table, then picks up the fork and eats anyway.

### Impression
**Vee** — I'm all the way in with her, and it aches. She's funny, quick, socially fluent, hungry in every sense, and has been talked out of her own body since girlhood; watching her stop apologizing has been the pleasure of the book. I trust her completely and I'm frightened for her, because she has handed her whole interior to the one person who was assigned to collect it. The tenderness I feel is heaviest where she's proudest.

**Pace** — I can't get him to resolve, and I think that's honest rather than evasive. Everything he does to Vee is careful, generous, and true; everything he did to *get* to her was arranged with another woman as sport. Both are him. Ch 50 moved me more than I wanted it to — a man eating prison food alone and crying at a truck that didn't slow. My wariness is specific: not that he's using her, but that he'll let his own fear cost her something irreplaceable while telling himself it's restraint.

**Randi** — she's the one I keep revising. For thirty chapters she read as the elegant predator; ch 47 turned her into the loneliest person here. I don't trust her — she's still lying by omission every Saturday, still working Vee open in ways Vee can't see, still the architect — and I feel real pity for her anyway. The bottle cap under her thumbnail, the face she puts back on with the mouth missing, the bracelet she keeps out of the light. She loves Vee and is a coward about it, and the cowardice is going to do damage.

**Cassie** — the only clean thing in the book, and the person I'd most want in the room when it comes apart. No reason at all to doubt her. Her being slowly edited out of Vee's confidences is its own small grief.

**Sheri** — unreservedly glad of her. She's the one voice that tells Pace the truth for free.

**Where trust sits:** Cassie and Sheri, fully trusted. Vee, trusted and in danger. Pace, believed but not yet vindicated. Randi, understood, pitied, and not for a second safe. And running under all of it: I'm enjoying the seduction exactly as Vee is, which is the uncomfortable part — the warmth is genuine, the pleasure is genuine, and none of that makes the arrangement less of an arrangement.
