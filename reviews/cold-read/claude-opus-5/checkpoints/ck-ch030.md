### Symbolism
- **The bench.** A purpose-built thing that becomes an ordinary long table the moment its top is set back on, sitting in the middle of the room "full of what it was… waiting for the next time." What it does to Randi is the book's opening statement of method, and she walks past it the next morning without looking.
- **The face.** Randi's actual face, and the thing she has lived behind since thirteen: "what was not her could be given away all day and cost nothing." Being seen past it was the most alive she has ever been and the one thing she will not survive twice.
- **Euler's formula.** Two things that won't give on their own, set at right angles, becoming a single plain thing you know what to do with. Offered as a math hint. It reads like the shape of the whole arrangement.
- **Simpson's paradox and the two outliers.** Same numbers, opposite verdict, nobody touched a figure; and the outlier that isn't a bad sensor but enters through the part that carries forward. Both delivered flat, by a bored man with a laser pointer, over Vee's head while she is not listening.
- **Latent variables.** The factors you can't measure directly and have to infer from the ones you can — the last thing the professor says before Randi takes Vee for coffee (ch 4).
- **Error-correcting codes.** Say it more than once so the noise can't kill it. Pace's cheerful explanation of redundancy sits oddly next to a book in which the same gesture is performed twice, on two women.
- **The dress made *to* her, not *for* her.** Randi supplies the preposition and Vee's throat closes on it. Cut on her body, lined only where it decided not to give her away, finished so no seam of effort shows — the visible form of everything being done to her.
- **The shoe that holds.** Being given no say in where her foot goes makes her surer of it, not less (23) — the same discovery as the dance frame she fought as "too much rule" and that then carried her across a floor (26).
- **The coastline.** As long as you have patience for. He says it about measurement.
- **Leave no trace.** A courtesy on a mountain; also, precisely, the standard the arrangement is being held to.
- **The pool.** The one place her body was never a problem, lost when it stopped being the right shape for the sport — weightlessness as the thing she is trying to get back to by another road.

### Story so far
**Ch 1–2 — the bench, and the morning after.** A young man's too-warm house off a gravel road, Steely Dan low in the front rooms. A black-haired, blue-eyed sorority girl lets herself in, bolts the door behind her, straddles him at his kitchen table and eats the apple he sliced thin for her. He walks her to the bedroom, where the long table she'd been laying her underwear on has had its top removed and is a padded, leg-spreading bench he built for her, for tonight. He undresses her on his knees, takes his time, gets her onto it, takes her word (*red*), spanks her, and then edges her past the end of her own repertoire until she begs in a voice that isn't the one she uses for waiters. She comes hard, cries without knowing she's crying, and he says: *There you are, Randi.* Afterward he wraps her in the cashmere blanket, holds her, tells her she's beautiful — and she flinches. She locks his bathroom door, looks at her face in the mirror, and decides: he can have the face; the woman he found is going back behind it and is not coming out again. Then she walks back out smiling, and stays the night. He goes to sleep pleased, thinking he's found her hunger and will answer it as often as she brings it to him. — Next morning she comes into her sorority house in last night's clothes and Brooke, chapter president, stops her from a seated position and pleasantly extracts everything: the paper that isn't due till Thursday, the name *Pace*, math PhD not engineering, not her section. Four minutes, mostly smiling. Randi goes up to her single, lies down in her coat and shoes under a thin duvet, and does not cry.

**Ch 3 — the pointing game.** In bed, mid-afterglow, Pace asks Randi whether she'd like to taste another woman, and her body answers before she can — she comes instantly, astonished. Asked again in daylight, she moves the wanting somewhere survivable: *only in a threesome. With you.* He says okay, and takes her for a walk on campus. He points at women; she picks. Too expected; you'd get both or neither; a *maybe* on the library steps; a hard *no* on the room-running blonde. Then a tall redhead comes out of the dining hall laughing with her head thrown all the way back, and Randi stops mid-stride and doesn't look away. "Yes." He tells her to go meet her; she drifts thirty feet and makes the collision look like the other girl's fault, and comes back glowing: *Her name is Vee. And we've got a stats class together.*

**Ch 4–5 — Vee's side of it.** Vee, who has been fine for two years, has been chosen by exactly the person she'd have chosen to be chosen by. Randi comes to stats and takes the seat beside her, is dazzling to Cassie too and keeps returning to Vee; then walks her to Chi Latte, sits her up in the window, and Vee loses the entire transcript of the best hour she's had in months and keeps only the feeling. *Love you, girl. See you later, okay?* — Days later, at the coffee shop on Wilson with Randi and Cassie, Vee is stuck on a challenge integral. A pleasant, unremarkable-looking man crosses the room with his eyes on her the whole way and asks if he can help. He reads the problem like it's new, asks what her professor said the trick was, writes the ugly formula in the margin and tells her not to carry it, marks a small cross — Euler's — and puts the pencil back in her hand at the exact line where she can finish it herself. Randi asks him one stats question; he answers it briskly and turns back to Vee. He asks what's drawing her to econ and listens for five minutes. He's finishing a math PhD, which mortifies her. *May I call you?* He leaves without the coffee he'd queued for.

**Ch 6–10 — the courtship.** He texts within twenty minutes; Randi tells her not to wait, and she doesn't. Froyo, the long way home, "this is the nicest a person has been to me in a really long time, and it's froyo." A pool afternoon with Cassie — catcalls on the bleacher walk, Cassie's low *tossers*, and Vee going under into the one place her body was never a problem. Then dinner: he asks for Saturday and leaves her the room to answer whenever. He arrives showered and shaved in polished boots, walks her to a tiny Middle Eastern place where Christine knows him, and asks *May I choose?* — and orders a whole table of soft unreadable names while she sits inside the warmth and stops keeping track of herself. Mint tea in little brass cages. He takes her hand for the first time. Under a tree on the way back she says she doesn't kiss a man she's known a couple of days, and he lets it close without argument — so she stops walking, pulls him back, and makes an exception. She tells Cassie everything, including that nobody did anything to her: she did it on purpose. — Meanwhile Randi takes her to a smoothie place, tells a story about a lacrosse bore, extracts the *May I choose* detail and says, slowly, "You let him choose for you. And you liked it." Brooke appears with a silent tall friend; Vee shrinks herself to nothing; and Randi takes her hand on the tabletop in front of them and says *Vee's my dear friend.* Vee walks home picked.

**Ch 11–13 — the mountain, and the invitation.** The hike. Wrong boots; he kneels on the trail, bares her heel and patches it, relaces the boot, and apologizes for not asking about boots at the lot. The summit opens onto a valley and a silver lake and burning ridges, and he watches her face instead of the view. Rye and horseradish sandwiches, cold water from one bottle, cherries — she spits a pit and he picks it up and lays it on wax paper without making her small. Over the cherries she tells him about the honor society induction and a russet silk dress in a magazine she can't afford, and puts it back on the shelf; he says *that sounds like a beautiful dress* and lets it lie. The rain comes down on the descent. In the cab he hands her the only dry shirt he packed for himself and gets out into the rain with his back turned. She watches him through the glass, knocks, crooks a finger, and — holding his eyes — unbuttons her soaked shirt and lets him look. He doesn't move; the gladness in his face is the whole answer. She notices he's shivering, puts his hand against her warm belly, and tells him to drive. A campus cop's siren takes the end of her sentence at the dorm door. She keeps the shirt. She tells Cassie; she tells Meg and Kayla over dinner, along with the fact that they've kissed exactly once, twelve days ago. Then his text: come to my house Saturday, two o'clock. No other information at all.

**Ch 14–16 — the fitting.** She misses the driveway. The house is small, old, and warm — too warm; she files it. He built the furniture; the joints have no screws in them; the shelves run fantasy paperbacks, cookbooks, and math. He licenses a patent that gets sick people out of MRI tubes faster, and lights up like a six-year-old explaining it. A garage that is a woodshop cleaner than her kitchen at home. And on the sun-porch table, a bolt of burgundy silk he holds up beside her face — not the magazine's red; a color mixed for her. He made the untagged shirt. He'd like to make the dress, if she'll let him, and to do it right he has to measure her. She says yes. Jeans, shirt, then — *the bra too, it changes the line* — and she stands on a riser he built, bare to the waist in flat afternoon light, and he doesn't ogle; he admires. He measures top-down, touching and withdrawing and writing it down, and she gets wetter and wetter and cannot stop it, and the pale satin she chose that morning with hope goes dark and obvious. When he knee-walks around to the front of her she shuts her eyes and braces for the laugh she's been braced for her whole life. He's waiting with his chin tipped all the way up so he can meet her eyes, smiling like he's been handed something. He never looked down. Her posture rearranges itself. After that she starts spending the new power — a hand up his wrist, a lean, and finally her bare breast pressed to his cheek on purpose. He goes red, nearly loses it, gets stuck by his own pin, kisses the tiny bead of blood, and says *keep still* a beat too late, choosing the dress over the easy yes. She tells Cassie all of it, including the heat turned up hours before she arrived.

**Ch 17 — the green-velvet booth.** Randi drives her out in the Mercedes to a dim restaurant where the walls eat sound, orders wine and salmon without reading the right side of the menu, and tells a story about being tied to a headboard with a scarf and tickled until she went off like a rocket — and then, gently, that a little helpless can be a very good thing, done right. Vee, who has rehearsed a clean version, gets walked out of it one soft sentence at a time: *nobody measures over a bra, sweetheart, it's just how it's done* (so there's nothing to be ashamed of, so she can say it); *were there any challenges?*; *it was cold in the house*; *cold. Hmm.* Vee laughs, cornered, and gives it up: it was hot. And then, right as Vee starts to hand over the one thing she has told nobody, Randi says *you don't have to tell me* and closes the door, and the hush is simply over. At the curb, Randi takes Vee's jaw and kisses her on the mouth, with a second press after the first. Vee explains it to herself as a French thing, then panics about who might have seen.

**Ch 18–20 — the first night, and the trade.** Friday. She buys a green satin-and-lace set and a bottle of frosted plum, paints her toes hours early, and drives out telling herself none of it means anything. He's in an apron with everything prepped and the Chianti already breathing. *Are you hungry?* — *Famished.* She kisses him first and asks to see his bedroom. He undresses her slowly, finds her already soaked, asks *would you be bothered if I was too hard?*, and goes down on her standing up while the shame runs the whole length of it and, for the first time, doesn't close her. They fuck with the lights giving her nowhere to put her face, and she stops being taken and starts meeting him. Afterward he cooks the fra diavolo and she sits at his table wrapped in his top sheet and eats. Later she fetches the ruined green panties, lets the sheet drop, folds them in thirds with the spot centered, and says *Trade.* She drives home bare, and can't take her skirt off in front of Cassie, and gives Cassie the whole story with the middle ladled out, and has never been so happy. At brunch with Randi she gives a fuller version, and Randi finds the toenails, the bought underwear, the whole campaign — and instead of teasing says *you knew how that night was going to end before you got in the car. So why are you telling me like it's a confession?* And Vee says: *I wanted it. And I took it. For once.* Second kiss at the curb; she meets it this time.

**Ch 21–23 — the dress, and the shoes.** He asks her over midweek and can't wait to show her: the gown hung in the sun-porch window with the light coming through it, a full-length mirror newly hung, the riser moved in front of it. She cries a little. It goes on like water finding its level, lined where it decided not to give her away, and there's a slit to the hip — so she takes her underwear off so nothing crosses the line, and strides down the hall to show him. They get most of the way through it standing up before remembering the silk, and the ensuing comic relay (neither able to let go of anything they're holding, him cleaning her own slickness off her fingers with his mouth) ends with the dress safely back on its hanger and both of them wrecked on the bed. He falls asleep; she lies awake imagining walking into the induction late and letting the room turn. — In stats, with Cassie on one side and Randi on the other, the professor explains Simpson's paradox while Vee glows; Randi books Saturday for shoes. Then the dorm room: Randi comes up, reads the silk with two fingers — *charmeuse, and a good weight* — and says, "He didn't make this *for* you. He made it *to* you." They wreck the discount store with laughter, Vee finds the sensible tan heel that goes with everything, and Randi kills it: a dress a man made *to* you doesn't want to go with everything. At the boutique Vee can't say she can't afford it, and doesn't have to — Randi kneels, holds her frosted-plum toes a beat too long, and buckles her into a pair of cognac heels that hold her like a hand. Vee puts back the debit card and pays with the emergency credit card. Randi buys herself loafers for several times the price without glancing at the total and wears them out.

**Ch 24 — the debrief.** Randi, afterward, on Pace's chest, Sade downstairs. She didn't think she'd like Vee this much; Vee got past her before she could help it. She retells the shoe store with Vee cast as the one caught blushing, and says *that was kind of hot.* She says Vee's got it bad, and that it's a little pathetic, and that she loves it. She warns that Vee isn't ready and he'll want to take his time; he says he wants Vee to get there on her own. He names the shame Vee carries; Randi says he's showing her she doesn't have to. Then she says, low and undefended, what Vee keeps under the cardigan is criminal — and he lets that stay hers rather than pick it up. When he describes what Vee does to a room, Randi goes still in a way that takes holding, says *yeah, she is,* and then rides him.

**Ch 25 — Peter.** By the fire, under a quilt, the cold on the other side of the glass. Ohio, casseroles, the unopened saffron, the nurse and the jet-engine father, *Vivienne* for love in one mouth and trouble in the other, a brother she loves and doesn't know. Then his: mother home, father at a chemical plant, and nothing else — and *my real name is Peter.* Twenty-two; SAT at fourteen; college at fifteen; a seventh-grade year spent doing long division at a desk in the hall because he asked his math teacher questions she couldn't answer. *They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment.* He talks about the shape with finite volume and infinite surface, the coastline, Newton's three sentences for the ball and the leaf and the stars, and she stops seeing the stars as decoration. He tells her about Daphne: assigned to him as a tutor, nineteen to his fifteen, two years, gone when she turned twenty-one — laid out in good order with every reason in its slot, and called the best two years he'd had. Vee holds the ugly word behind her teeth and says only *you were so young.* She finds the long old scar high on his inner thigh; he covers her hand with his and says *not tonight,* and doesn't move her hand.

**Ch 26 — the Rusty Nail.** He asks her dancing and teaches her the three-step on a concrete pad by the dark gym, with a taped-up boom box: give me something to push against, close the space, don't look at your feet, don't think — feel the lead. She fights it and then it gives, and she's dancing. At the bar everyone knows him; he taught the beginner class two semesters; the women hug him long. She's good on the floor until she starts counting. At the bar a blonde tells her *he's such a great lead, all us girls love him, he's so good about getting a girl home safe at the end of the night* — and Vee, knowing exactly what's being done to her, does it anyway: asks him in front of the room whether his *services* include getting the girls home safe. He answers straight — *yes, sometimes, when it's required* — and his face goes. She tells him she'll handle herself and walks off. He sets his untouched beer on the bar and leaves. She stays until the lights come up, watching the girls she's calling floozies, and an older bartender tells her the truth: he leaves them at the door, he doesn't bring dates, he brought one on a Thursday — *wasn't him they were jealous of, honey.* Vee takes a cab she can't afford, puts on his shirt that no longer smells like him, lies down on top of the covers, and doesn't sleep. By dawn she knows what she's going to do.

**Ch 27 — coached.** She walks to the sorority house in the cold with bare legs and an apple. Brooke lets her in — *oh, you're the stats friend* — under a foyer of composites. Randi comes down in a robe, no makeup, and takes her upstairs. Vee tells all of it. Randi supplies the frame: you don't get that jealous over a man you don't care about; you were so far ahead you couldn't see the race and ran it anyway, and he saw that, and that's the only thing that matters. Then: *not today.* Tomorrow night, so it reads as chosen, not panicked. Don't go in sorry — go in sure. He won't text; he doesn't sulk; he'll leave you the room. Then dress-up: top off, a slow appraising circuit, *he doesn't stand a chance*; the closet Vee wants to live in; *you'd be my very own shoe whore*; three pairs tried on under a long skirt so Vee can't see any of it, a hand under the hem bracing her thigh, a thumb behind her knee; the cognac heels; a green-and-gold silk scarf tied at her throat and then untied so Vee can learn the knot with Randi's hands over hers — *that's the whole difference between dorm mixer and dinner in Paris.* Goodbye kiss in the foyer, and Vee notices, a half-beat late, that she didn't brace.

**Ch 28 — the ride.** Pace and Sheri out past his place on bikes. Sheri watched Vee sit alone all night and leave alone, and says so, and says she's got a hell of a backside. Pace talks about Vee longer than he means to. *Listen to you.* Sheri asks about "the pale sorority chick"; *Randi. That's not finished.* Two at once, Sheri says. If it isn't complicated already, it will be. He doesn't answer.

**Ch 29 — the apology.** She changes into the heels in the car, brings a supermarket apple pie labeled HUMBLE PIE, and finds herself an inch taller than him in the doorway — starts to fold down and unbuckle, and he lifts the pie away, stands her back up, resets the scarf on her shoulder, and says: beauty like yours made for the gods and loaned out; he's not going to insult the loan by complaining he has to look up. She delivers the speech she built. He accepts it and then asks the one thing she built it to avoid — *but what happened?* — and it all comes out, the blonde's exact words and the fact that she knew and picked the lie up anyway because part of her was standing there waiting for one. He takes her face in both hands and thanks her for telling him what the woman said. She stays two nights. Sunday afternoon, in his undershirt and nothing else, she hangs off his back at the sink arguing for soaking; he tickles; she swats him; he swats back — one open wet hand, flat, where the shirt has ridden up — and something goes up through her so hard and fast her whole face changes, and he sees it. She covers it with her mouth and her hands and talks the entire way to the bedroom so no quiet can stand a question up. She rides him with her face hidden in his neck, lighting the sting with every stroke. Then his hand moves a few inches and settles flat over the exact place and bears her down, and everything she's been holding up comes off. *"Please."* — *"This?"* — *"Please."* Neither of them names it. She comes apart on it. Down the hall the pan keeps soaking. Monday she wakes late with her hair a wild country and no time and cries about it a little; he gathers it at her nape, kisses behind her ear, and says *you're always beautiful.*

**Ch 30 — the practice room.** Stats: two kinds of outliers, and the second kind comes in through the part that carries forward. Randi's knee against hers under the desk. This time it's Vee's two fingers on Randi's wrist — *walk me out?* — and Cassie gathering her things and saying *see you in the room* to the side of Vee's head. Randi thinks of the soundproof practice rooms, where people used to. Vee tells her about the heels and the gods, and then skids past the middle and lands on "we were being stupid, he sort of swatted me back." Randi will not let it go: *a game of slap-and-tickle. Did you deserve it?* — *probably* — *his hand was wet.* And Vee is back in the kitchen, voice dropped, saying it went right through her and he saw. *I bet he did.* Then Randi hands her the vocabulary: her own man, the mouse that roared, over his knee, skirt up, panties down, counting out loud, humiliating, *and God, it made me buck* — and, toward the end, his hand would slip, and by then it's very welcome. On *welcome*, Vee's body arrives at a conclusion, and she goes to the hall bathroom, bolts the door, finds herself soaked from nothing but a woman talking in a chair, catches her own hand and holds it flat against her knee. She comes back; Randi is warm, already up, already leaving, as if she'd meant nothing by it. The goodbye kiss is so worn-in now that Vee barely clocks it — except that her arms are slow coming down, and she notices it the way you notice a held note after the song has moved on, and puts it down to being tired.

### Impression
**Vee** — I like her enormously and I'm frightened for her, which is an uncomfortable pair to hold. She's funny, generous, socially fluent, smart in a way she keeps apologizing for, and she has been starving so long she reads plain decency as a miracle. Watching the shame convert into heat rather than shutdown has been genuinely moving. And every yes has been hers — that's the part that's going to hurt.

**Pace** — I can't find the lie, and that's what unsettles me. Everything he does is kind and most of it is kind at real cost to himself: the shirt, the rain, the returned pencil, the closed door he wouldn't open, the night he stayed hard and let it be about her. He is also, demonstrably, running a method, and I have watched him run the identical method on two women. He talks about Daphne with every reason in its slot, which is what a man does when he's holding a full glass. I trust his tenderness completely and his self-knowledge not very much at all.

**Randi** — the one I most want to be wrong about. The bench broke her open and she sealed it shut by morning, and everything since has been her spending herself on Vee while telling herself it's a game. "She got past me before I could help it" is the most honest sentence she's said out loud. She wants Vee and won't say so; she's lying to Pace by omission about her own interior; she shapes what she reports. Suspicion sits highest here — and so does the ache.

**Cassie** — no reason to doubt her at all. She's the only clean instrument in the book, and the fact that she's being quietly edited out of Vee's confidences is the loss I feel most.

**Where trust sits:** nobody has yet done Vee harm on the page, and nearly everything done to her has been done well. What makes me wary isn't cruelty — there hasn't been any — it's that the two people she loves most have a shared history she doesn't know exists, and the day she finds out, every good thing in this book turns over and becomes a different fact, the way the professor's numbers did.
