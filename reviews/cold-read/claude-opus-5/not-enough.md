## Reader reaction

**Not Enough** — reader reaction

---

God, that hurt. And I didn't expect it to, not this way. I've spent forty-odd chapters unable to find the seam in Pace, and this chapter hands it to me and the seam is just — fear. Not a scheme, not a cold operator's calculation. A man who said the words once, at sixteen or so, to the only person who'd ever shown him where to stand, and then she left and there was no one to tell that she was gone. That line took the top of my head off. "*There had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone. Daphne was the person he would have told.*" That is the whole of him in two sentences, and it recolors the Daphne conversation on the glass porch completely. I read his good-order recitation back then as a wound he was defending. It was — but the thing he was defending was the *rule* he built out of it: say it and losing them takes your life; don't say it and loss stays survivable. "He had taught himself that the words were the difference." That's a superstition dressed as arithmetic, and it is so exactly the kind of error a man who thinks in proofs would make and never audit.

And he knows. That's what wrecked me. He isn't confused. "*He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him.*" He watched his own answer land and fail in real time — "*He had watched the answer reach her and fail*" — which means Vee's laundry-room agony, the *maybe he understood and chose to say it like that, maybe it went past him entirely*, has an answer, and the answer is the worse of the two. He understood. He chose the smaller truth "because it was safe, and because he was afraid, and because at the moment she had asked him to be brave he had been weak." Sitting with what she doesn't know and I do is the specific misery this book keeps putting me in, and this time it isn't the arrangement, it's just two people and one sentence.

My feeling about him has moved more in this chapter than in any since the fitting. Not toward exoneration — toward something more uncomfortable, which is *pity*. This enormous competent man mopping his own sick out of the hall, standing in a shower that doesn't help, eating vacuum-sealed chicken and broccoli out of a bowl because his body needs fuel and his mouth wants nothing. The food is the tell and the book knows it: the man who taught Vee what a meal is, alone, eating prison food. He cooks like that *for other people*. By himself he refuels. "It went down because he told it to."

The truck did me in. I saw it coming a half-second before he did and it landed anyway — his head lifting before he knew he'd moved, his body "gathering itself toward the narrow cut in the hill," the engine holding steady, not slowing. And then he bends over the table and *sobs*. I have never seen him lose one degree of composure. Not with Randi crying on the bench, not in the rain, not at the Rusty Nail when Vee gutted him. He sobs until he can't hear the truck and for a few breaths after, and then picks the fork back up. "He was not hungry. He ate anyway." Those four words are the best ending in the book so far.

Here's my one cold spot, and it's a real one: **Randi does not appear in this chapter. Not as a thought, not as a name, not as a flicker.** He is alone in a house with a bedroom that has a bench in it and a whole other woman with an overnight bag, and she does not cross his mind once while he grieves. Sheri asked him at the diner, "She find out about the brunette, then?" and he said no — so it's live in his head somewhere. Its total absence here isn't a hole I want filled; it *is* the information. Whatever Randi is to him, she is not in the room where the loss is. I don't know whether that's tenderness toward Vee or a kind of blankness toward Randi, and I'd rather it stayed open than got explained.

The friction: "*Whiskey River* was a liar" is the one line where I felt a nudge — the Willie song and the joke about it are a little tidier than the rest of the chapter's plain reporting. And the Daphne section runs slightly closer to summary-statement than I'd like ("He had taught himself that the words were the difference") — the page names his mechanism for him where everywhere else it lets the mop and the bowl do it. Small. I didn't mind much. The unnamed woman who called the bowl prison food was a lovely, costless piece of the past; that she ended "with no cruelty on either side and no part of the world altered by it" is exactly the survivable loss his rule was designed to produce, and it reads as indictment without a word of editorial.

**Not Enough** — the chicken and broccoli that has everything a body needs and nothing his mouth wants. The smaller truth. All those bricks he laid up for Sheri, every one of them true and none of them the thing. Vee's *I'd just like to know*. It's the same shape four ways, and the title didn't give anything away before I read it — it clarified after. And *With a Long Spoon* / *A Polite Invitation* still sits over all of it like a threat I can't cash yet: sup with the devil, use a long spoon. Right now the long spoon looks less like a warning about Randi and more like what Pace has been holding between himself and everyone since he was seventeen. (No tagline in what I was given.)

What I want next: I want him to drive out to that dorm. I want it badly and I don't trust that he will, because he's a man who leaves you the room. **Guess from here:** the words come out eventually and Randi is somewhere in the frame when they do, and that's the detonation.

**Cast present (in person):** Pace, alone, throughout. Mentioned/remembered only: Vee, Daphne, the unnamed ex who called it prison food. (No Randi, in any form.)

**Heat:** 1 — only the remembered barefoot walk down his hall, and that's rendered as loss, not want.

**Romance:** 3 — he knows he loves her, names it to himself, and names exactly why he wouldn't say it; a turn in the bond even though she isn't there to receive it.

**Motifs & images:** Feeding (repeat, everywhere — inverted here: fuel with no pleasure). The first bite and her face opening (repeat, ch 38/39). Crackers and a meal (repeat, from the porch chapter — quoted back and understood). Cold/warm house (repeat — the house is *cold* now; he keeps it warm only for her). Music playing low and unnamed (repeat — piano and a horn, and Vee has never heard one start). The narrow drive and the covered stoop (repeat, from the arrival ritual). Doors (the bathroom door he shuts on himself). Frozen portioned food — first sighting, and it rhymes ugly with Randi portioning herself apple slices in ch 1.

**Symbolism:** The bowl — complete nutrition, zero pleasure, the exact opposite of what he built Vee's whole appetite on; his own rule served back to him on a plate. The passing truck: the thing that comes near and does not turn in.

**Characterization:** Pace deepened enormously, and in the direction I'd least predicted — downward, into ordinary human cowardice rather than design. First time I've seen him break. Daphne, in memory, went from anecdote to load-bearing.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Tight. The domestic opening earns its slowness; the Daphne middle is the one stretch that tells slightly more than it shows; the truck and the last three lines are perfectly timed.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** Building hard. After Vee's New Year's ceiling and Sheri's "that feels pretty shitty," this is the answering panel and it lands the run's emotional low point without repeating a beat — and it's the first time the POV has been alone with him in his own grief.
