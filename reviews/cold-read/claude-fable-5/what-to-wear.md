# Cold read (grounded) — What to Wear

*scene: scenes/what-to-wear.md · model: claude-fable-5 · memory: ck-ch010 + raw ch011..ch012 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

What a pleasure this one is — and what a quiet dread it opens under the pleasure, without ever once touching it on the page.

The felt thing first: this is Vee among girlfriends, and it's a Vee I haven't fully seen before. Not the Vee braced under her mother's voice, not the Vee sitting up on Randi's high shelf being perfect — Vee "laughing with her whole body, loud and easy, the kind that made the next table look over and then want in." That's the girl Pace pointed at on the quad, and it lands on me with a pang: the book is showing me she has a life, other tables, other friends — Meg from the collision day, this new bright Kayla — and a boy named Theo she turned down so gracefully he still waves. She isn't a lonely mark. She's a whole person with options, which makes what's being run on her worse, and makes her happiness more her own. Both at once. The chapter never says any of that; it just seats her in the gold light and lets her be loved.

The retelling of the truck runs a beat I adored: "She could hear the story getting better in the telling and did not care." That's so honest about how new love works — and I notice what she keeps and what she skips. The shirt, the standing in the rain, the cop: all told, and told well. The taking off of her wet shirt in front of him — the thing she gave Cassie almost whole in "Rye" — never surfaces here. Kayla and Meg get the gallant version; Cassie got the true one. That gradient of confidants (Cassie the whole truth, the dinner table the shine, Randi — remembering "Dear" — the version Randi draws out of her) is doing lovely quiet work about who Vee actually trusts, and I didn't feel an authorial thumb anywhere in it.

New fact that genuinely moved me: Pace is a powerlifter. It retro-fits everything — the forearms, the economical mover, "I think there's a whole situation under there" from last chapter — without ever having been dangled. And "he writes for six hours and stops. 'My writing's no good after that'" is exactly the man: discipline as identity. Vee giving them his flat certain voice, proud, is her falling in real time.

Then the phone. "Would you come to my house Saturday. Two o'clock." My stomach did the drop the girls' didn't, because I know what's in that house. The bench is in that house. The cashmere blanket. Randi's perfume has been in that house. Kayla, joyously oblivious, says the truest line in the chapter — "He wants you for the afternoon. Bring a change of clothes" — and she means it as girl-talk scandal, and I hear it as the plan advancing, and the chapter lets both readings sit in the same sentence without underlining either. That's the book at its best: the dread is entirely mine, imported from what I know; the page itself is nothing but warm.

One more line that snagged, in a good way: Meg's gentle "Everybody's different at the start. I said it about Danny. Word for word… Sometimes they even are." A real friend's real caution, offered kindly, and "Vee let it go by. It didn't catch on anything." Of course it didn't. That's the tragedy in miniature — the true warning arrives and slides off, because he is different, just not in the way anyone at this table could guess.

Suspicion: none of anyone on this page. Kayla and Meg are exactly what they seem. My unease is all offstage — Randi hasn't appeared in two chapters, and I keep feeling her absence like a held breath. Is the Saturday invitation his alone, or coordinated? The page doesn't say, so I hold it open — but "the stated plan is a threesome" is sitting in my memory and two o'clock on a Saturday at his house is a door.

Erotic charge: mild and deliberately displaced — the heat here is retrospective (the truck story re-lit) and anticipatory ("I have read it a thousand times," the quick light stupid heart she lets herself have). "It's making me lose my mind… climbing the walls over a man asking me what I want for dinner" is the chapter's little furnace: her arousal is now consciously about the asking itself, which she can hear "sounding like nothing" and can't explain. She's naming the mechanism without seeing it.

Friction: almost none. If I reach — the transition into Kayla's "So what's Pace do, then?" is a hair abrupt after Theo, but that's how Kayla talks, so it may be character, not seam. It's a talk-chapter after a talk-chapter ("Rye"), and it risks redundancy retelling the truck a second time — but the second telling is *about* the telling, and the new material (friends, powerlifting, Theo, the text) earns it.

The titles. "What to Wear" is perfect and double-jointed: Kayla's literal scandalized question ("What did he say to wear?" — "He didn't say"), and underneath it the russet silk dress Vee set back on the shelf on the mountain, the cardigan-over-the-better-shirt life she's lived, the flannel folded on her pillow "at this exact hour." What she wears is the whole grammar of who she's allowed to be. And *A Polite Invitation* — the invitation just literally arrived, eleven words, polite to the point of blankness. "Two people have planned the best year of her life" has never felt closer to the surface. The long spoon: she's about to sup at the source.

What I want next: Saturday, obviously — I've read the text a thousand times too. Guesses from here: the house visit is the consummation the book has been walking toward, and I half-expect some trace of Randi in that house — a smell, an object, the bench under its lid — that Vee will pass by without knowing. I dread the bench most. I want her afternoon to be as good as she deserves, and I no longer believe those two things can both stay true forever.
