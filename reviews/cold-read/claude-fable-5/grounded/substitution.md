# Cold read (grounded) — Substitution

## Reader reaction

So here it is — the meet. The thing the jacket promised from the first line, the thing "The Pointing Game" armed and "See You Later" set ticking, and I spent the whole chapter split cleanly in two: one half of me inside Vee's chest, warming like she warms, and the other half watching Randi's thumbs move on that phone and going cold. "Randi was texting — thumbs quick, eyes down, the private, absorbed focus of a girl writing to someone who had her attention. Some boy, Vee thought, amused." Reader, I did not think *some boy*. And then the bell over the door tinkled, and Vee didn't look up, and I did — I looked up for her, because I've been ahead of her since the quad, exactly as the jacket said I would be, and it works on me like a hand pressed slowly on the sternum. The dread is not that something bad happens in this chapter. Nothing bad happens in this chapter. That's the whole trick of it.

And I have to be honest about the other half, because this is where the book is dangerous: the seduction *worked on me too*. The tutoring scene is one of the best pieces of quiet erotic-adjacent writing I've hit in the book so far, and there's no sex in it at all. "He picked up her pencil — *asked for it with his eyes first,* it reached her before anything else." The formula he writes and then tells her not to memorize — "Nobody should have to carry that around" — and then the little crossed mark, Euler's, the thing she's known for years and never once used, and "He set the pencil back in her hand. // She hadn't known she was allowed." That last line undid me. It's the whole book in seven words. This is exactly what he did to Randi on the bench — found the thing already in her and handed it back to her as hers — and watching him do the pedagogical version of it, keeping himself out of the solving so the solving stays Vee's, I felt the pull Vee feels *and* recognized the technique, and feeling both at once is a genuinely strange, rich reading experience. Her long unspooling analysis of *what he had done* — the tutor's path, not the mathematician's, "the path of a person who wasn't performing" — I bought completely because it's her econ eye, the gap between what people say they're doing and what they're doing, which she names as her whole intellectual love two pages later without ever aiming it at the two people at her own table. That's the merciless part, and the book didn't have to underline it. It let me do it.

Randi in this chapter is the thing I can't stop turning over. She's almost silent, and every small thing she does is loud. "Randi," she says — one bare word — after Vee has just *performed Randi's own name-bit*, the "V.T. Yes, like the university" swerve she tucked away at the lecture hall. Vee wearing Randi's line, to Pace, while Randi sits there and yields it without a flicker — I actually put my hand over my mouth. And then the cup: "Twice the cup came up to her mouth and went back down, and no sip happened." Her other hand in her lap. Vee's brain "helpfully provided the explanation *she's bored*," and mine helpfully provided several others, and the page confirms none of them — nerves, arousal, jealousy, the strain of watching the thing she set in motion actually move — and I'm going to leave that exactly as ajar as the page leaves it, because after Randi's bathroom-mirror chapter I know better than to think I can read her from the outside. What I do know is the thumbnail-and-bottle-cap from "The Bench," and the no-sip cup rhymed with it in a way that made my stomach dip. And her closing move — "A man doesn't forget his coffee over a girl he isn't going to call" — is delivered as girlfriend-analysis and lands, for me, as a saleswoman closing. She's tending the fire she lit. Warmly. That warmth being real *and* instrumental at once is the exact thing the jacket warned me about, and the chapter never once needs to say it.

Cassie. Oh, Cassie. "That cute guy in line is looking at you" — Cassie is the one who turns Vee's head, the flat honest instrument innocently pressed into the machine's service, and that hurt in a small quiet way. And then Cassie watching Randi's cup "without watching it," and Cassie's parting shot: "He looked at you like he'd forgotten there was anyone else in the room... He looked at her the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." Cassie is the only person at that table doing honest observation, and she's *right* about the data and can't possibly have the model. Her "Goodbye, Vee" from last chapter is still ringing in me, and every scene deepens my sense that Cassie sees the shape of something and has no name for it yet. Whether Pace's great-aunt look at Randi was performance — of course it was performance; they were in each other's bed that morning for all I know — Cassie clocked its *quality* without knowing it was a lie. She unsettles me in the best way.

Trust and suspicion, plainly: I have no suspicion of Cassie. I have total, earned, page-built suspicion of the *situation* — not manufactured, I watched them build it two chapters ago. Pace I remain unable to simply condemn, which is its own discomfort: the "May I call you?" is real courtesy, the room he leaves her is real room, and it is all happening inside an encounter she was never told was rigged. "She heard the *may*." So did I, and I heard the book's title in it — this is the polite invitation, syllable for syllable, and every yes Vee gives from here will be freely given, and that, per the jacket's last line, is the problem.

The title: "Substitution" is doing so much I laughed a little when it settled. The calculus move itself — turn the thing you can't solve into a thing you already know. Vee substituting Randi's bit for her own voice. Randi, back in "The Pointing Game," substituting owner for appetite — "not a woman who hungered for another woman... but a girlfriend offering her man a gift." And Pace's little speech about Euler's: "On their own these two are a struggle... Put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain." Two things at right angles becoming one plain nameable thing — I felt that reach past the math toward every pairing in this book, and I don't think I'm inventing it; the page put it in a margin and handed me the pencil.

Friction: nearly none. If I squint, Vee's meta-analysis of Pace's tutoring runs one beat long, but the "The thought did not finish" device kept catching her (and me) before it curdled into essay. The chapter earns its length; the stuck-integral opening that felt like throat-clearing turns out to be the whole metaphor.

What I want next: I want the phone to light up and I hate that I want it, which is, I suspect, the intended condition of reading this book. Guesses from here: the phone lights up; the lunches "too intimate to name" begin; and somewhere Randi is going to have to feel something she can't file — the no-sip cup says the filing is already straining. And I keep thinking about Cassie's "Goodbye, Vee," the whole word, the word for a longer parting. I'm afraid she was right.

**Cast present (in person):** Vee, Randi, Cassie, Pace — all four, one table and the next one over, the only scene so far with all four principals in a room. Mentioned only: Vee's econ instructor/professor, "some boy" (Vee's guess), her mother (implied in "a word her mother might use").

**Heat:** 1 — no sex, but a real simmer: the forearms she looks at "a half-second past where she would have chosen," the wondering what he does with his hands, Randi's unexplained hand in her lap. Clothed, charged, deniable.

**Romance:** 2 — the tutoring sequence is a genuine falling-for-him set piece; "She hadn't known she was allowed" and the *may I* land as real tenderness, complicated only by what I know and she doesn't.

**Motifs & images:** The pencil, set down/broken/handed back (first sighting as motif, and it carries the chapter). The bell over the door, twice — entrance and exit (first). Cups and sipping — the no-sip cup rhymes with the spoons ringing in Chi Latte and, darker, with the bottle cap under Randi's thumbnail in "The Bench" (repeat, transformed). The name-bit performed by Vee (repeat from "See You Later," ownership transferred — a literal substitution). Phone facedown — Randi's in "Standards," now Vee's in the last line (repeat). "Turn it into a thing you already know" as the family crest of Randi's move in "The Pointing Game." Economy of movement — Pace "nothing in the movement spent that did not need spending" echoes "the small economy of two people" opening "The Bench" (repeat). Being chosen/being seen — "she felt, absurdly, chosen," echoing Vee's "chosen, plainly" under the Chi Latte sign (repeat).

**Symbolism:** Euler's identity — two intractable things at right angles becoming one plain thing — is openly offered as more than math; the page invites it and I take it, though *which* two things it names I leave open. The unsolvable integral that "keeps coming back around" as Vee's own circling want. The polite "May I" as the volume title made audible.

**Characterization:** Everyone deepened. Vee's intelligence finally gets to be on the page as *appetite* — for the problem, for the man, for the borrowed poise — and her self-blindness ("she's bored") is character, not convenience. Randi deepens by subtraction: what she doesn't do (the bit, the sip) says more than a monologue. Pace is consistent to the point of eeriness — the same technique on Vee's math as on Randi's body. Cassie, in a handful of lines, is the book's conscience-in-waiting. Nobody thinned.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Beautifully managed. The stuck-problem open runs just long enough to make Pace's entrance feel like relief rather than plot; the tutoring is the right length; the exit and the three-women postmortem is brisk. No drag.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** Strong build. "The Pointing Game" set the trap, "See You Later" bonded Vee to Randi, and this closes the triangle — three chapters, three distinct registers, no repeated beat. Momentum is real: the machine is now fully assembled and the book knows I know it.
