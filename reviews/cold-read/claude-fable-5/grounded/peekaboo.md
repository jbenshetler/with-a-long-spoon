# Cold read (grounded) — Peekaboo

*scene: scenes/peekaboo.md · model: claude-fable-5 · memory: ck-ch020 (no window) · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

This one got me. I want to say that plainly before I say anything else: I read "Peekaboo" with a stupid smile on my face and a lump in my throat at the exact same moment Vee had hers — "She hadn't decided to cry and she was crying, a little, the dumb happy kind that always beat her to it." The dress hung in the window with the fall sun coming through it is one of the loveliest images the book has given me, and the line that broke me open is the one that breaks her: "he'd taken all that trouble and then taken the further trouble to hide the trouble, so the only thing left for her to see was the part that looked like no trouble at all." That's Pace in one sentence. It's also — and I felt this land a half-second after the tears — the whole book in one sentence. Hiding the trouble is what he *does*. The sentence works as pure swoon and as a description of the deception she's living inside, and the chapter never nudges me toward the second reading. It just leaves it there, glowing, like the silk.

How I feel about the two people actually on the page: Vee keeps deepening in exactly the direction I've been rooting for. The mirror scene is her best solo passage yet. "*Floozy.* The word flared up automatic and her face went hot — and she stayed anyway, made herself look, and somewhere behind her ribs the looking stopped being shame." That's the mother's voice arriving fused to the heat, same as at the truck, the fitting, the first night — and this is the first time she's beaten it standing still, alone, with no one watching to perform for. Then the underwear. Her first thought is to keep her steps small so the pale edge won't show; the second thought "came up hot behind the first and didn't care what showed." She strips herself bare *on purpose, to be looked at*, alone in a room, and lets the shame cross into wanting "almost before it was shame at all." After eighteen chapters of watching her ladle out edited middles, watching her do this in private — authoring her own exposure with nobody to author it for — felt like a real milestone, not a repeat of the fitting. And then she *strode*. "The length of her given and taken back and given." God.

Pace — the boyish grin undid me. "Not sly, not holding a thing over her — *grinning*, helpless with it, the way a kid can't keep a surprise in his mouth one more second." He breaks a kiss that was "already past stopping" because he cannot wait another minute for her to see the thing he made. He steps out for her to change without being asked and without making anything of it. He was up all night finishing it. If I knew nothing else about this man I would be completely gone on him. I know what else I know. And here is where I'll be honest about the one cold thread in an otherwise warm chapter, because the page did earn it: the pedestal "he'd built for her to stand on" has been moved in front of "a full-length mirror that hadn't been there before — newly hung, the screw heads still bright." He builds staging for his women. He built Randi a bench with a hidden purpose under a plain top; he's built Vee a riser and now a mirror, an apparatus for being seen, in the same sun porch. The bright screw heads are just a lovely detail of freshness — and they're also the seam of effort showing for once, and I noticed I was checking for seams. That's on me and on the book both; nothing Pace did in this chapter was anything but generous. That's the trap working.

Swoon, specifically: the "wait, the dress" beat is my favorite thing in the chapter, maybe in the whole run since the flannel. Mid-passion, one of them stops, and he hangs the gown from the door "with the two-handed care you'd give something alive before coming straight back to her." It cost them a minute "of a thing neither of them had wanted to pause, and she wouldn't have given the minute back." That's characterization as romance — of course the man who kissed the bead of blood off her pin-stick pauses to save the dress; of course she loves him for it. (I notice I nearly wrote "loves." The page hasn't said it. Neither has she. I'll leave it ajar, but the word is standing just outside the door.)

Erotic charge: high and mostly clothed, which is interesting after "Famished." The peak for me is the mirror sequence — the gown coming down her "like water finding its level," the slit falling open "calf to the jut of her hip," her removing the underwear so "nothing crossed it" — and then the leg hooked around him with the slit falling all the way open. The sex itself is elided: "after that there was nothing left for either of them to decide," cut to after. A tiny part of the reader in me who came for the explicit wanted that on the page — Famished spoiled me — but I can't call it a flaw; the chapter's real sex scene is the mirror, and the ellipsis let the dress stay the center. It worked.

Friction: almost none. If I squint, the paragraph about the pumps at the back of her closet runs a beat long for what it does — though "on this they'd have read like an apology, and this wasn't a dress you apologized for" pays it off, and I suspect the shoes she doesn't own are a door left open for someone (a guess from here: Randi, who owns exactly such things, will supply them, and I dread how much I'll enjoy that scene).

The ending is where the chapter quietly turns the knife it never shows. Her induction daydream — standing in the middle of the light while the room turns to look, "and the looking didn't cost her a thing. It just felt like what she was for" — is triumphant and it made my chest hurt, because "what she was for" is a sentence with a second reading she can't see. And the last line: she lies in his arm, watching the dress go dark on the door, "getting away with something." She means the happiness — too much luck, unearned, hers anyway. But she is the one thing in that house that something is being gotten away *with*, and the book just let her say it, smiling, asleep-adjacent, in his arms. No one else on the page. Randi nowhere in the chapter — first one in a long while with no Randi and no Cassie at all — and her absence hummed for me the whole time, because this dress, this fitting, this tailor all connect back to her ("nobody measures over a bra").

The title: "Peekaboo" is doing three things at once and I like all of them. The slit — given and taken back and given, open and shut, the game the dress plays with her leg. The surprise — his kid-grin, the thing he couldn't keep in his mouth, hands over the eyes and *there it is*. And underneath, quieter: hiding the trouble so only the no-trouble shows; the plan playing peekaboo with her in every scene. It didn't give anything away going in — I expected something coy, maybe a striptease — and it recolors nicely on the far side. The book's title keeps sharpening too: this chapter is the polite invitation at its most literal — a door opened, a gift made, every step hers, "she didn't ask him to leave, and he didn't make anything of going." Every yes freely given. The tagline's "that was the problem" is the shadow the sun porch light throws.

What I want next: the induction, in the dress, with the mother in the room and possibly Randi in the room, and I both want it badly and am braced for it — that daydream reads like a promise the book intends to keep, and the book has been keeping its promises in ways that cost somebody. Guesses from here: the shoes come from Randi; the induction is where Vee's separate worlds (mother, Cassie, Randi, Pace) finally stand in one high room; and something in Randi cracks further when she sees Vee in a dress Pace built with his hands — the man who builds things *for* his women, and what he built Randi was a bench.
