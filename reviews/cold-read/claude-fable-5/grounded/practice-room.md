# Cold read (grounded) — The Practice Room

## Reader reaction

Felt read

This chapter did the thing this book does best to me: nothing "happens" — two women talk in a closet — and I came out of it with my pulse up and my stomach in a knot at the same time, and I could not tell you which feeling was in charge.

Start with the reversal, because it landed on me before anything erotic did. "Now it was Vee's two fingers on Randi's wrist... towing the most interesting person she knew toward the door." For weeks it was Randi cutting Vee out of the herd; now Vee does the cutting, and Cassie is left talking "to the side of Vee's head" with her granola bar and her wrong textbook. That half-sentence hurt. Cassie has been the truest person in this book and she just got demoted in real time, mid-sentence, and Vee didn't even hear herself do it. And the sting underneath: I heard Pace say, two chapters back in the low lamplight, "I want her to get there on her own." She's getting there on her own. That's what walking to the door under your own power looks like when someone has built the corridor.

Then the practice room, and God, the practice room worked on me. The soundproofing — "the building went away. Not muffled. Gone. The kind of quiet you could hear your own blood in" — is such a good move: the book keeps giving Randi and Vee smaller and more private boxes (the alcove booth, the single room, now a cell), and Vee's own blush at what freshman-year people "used them to. You know" tells us what kind of room her body thinks it's in before her head allows it.

Randi's interrogation is the most nakedly visible I've ever seen the machinery, and it still swept me along, which is exactly the trap the jacket warned me about. "So. Did you deserve it?" — and Vee, laughing too high, agrees, and only "a moment late" realizes what she's agreed to. Randi's smile brightening at that was the cat at the mousehole. And then the ex-boyfriend story. Reader, I know what I know: I was on the bench in chapter one. Skirt up, counting out loud, the hand that "slips" — she is feeding Vee her own actual life with Pace, gender-flipped and wrapped in a fictional "slight little thing" who "apologized to the furniture," and Vee takes it in as girl-talk while her body takes it in as instruction. "Sometimes it's the mouse that roars" is Randi doing what she has always done — telling the truth as a lie. It is horrifying and it is genuinely hot and the book made me hold both without letting me put either down.

The erotic charge peaks not in the story but in Vee's body receiving it: the nipples drawing up "to the point of aching, standing where anyone could see, where Randi could see," the crossed arms she "tried to make look like settling." And then the bathroom, which might be my favorite passage in the chapter — her hand knowing the way, and her catching it and pressing it flat to her knee: "To do that would be to say yes to it, and she would not say yes to it." Say yes to what, Vee? The sentence refuses to name its object and that refusal IS her. Aroused past soaking by a woman talking in a chair, and the one thing she can still control is whether she signs her name to it. She doesn't. The unfinished "Randi. Have you ever—" belongs to the same family: "the word she needed wasn't a word she owned yet." I ached for her there. Owning words is what this whole book has been teaching her, one at a time — "lover," "I took it" — and Randi is visibly the one handing them over.

The outliers lecture: yes, the professor-over-her-head gag again (latent variables, Simpson's paradox, now this), and yes, it's a touch on the nose — "The second kind comes in through the part that carries forward. It's in everything that comes after" is practically a title card. But I've come to enjoy this running joke as the book's dry chorus, and the chill it gave me was real: which kind of outlier is Randi? You can't lift her out. She's in everything that comes after.

The ending got me most quietly. The worn-in goodbye kiss Vee "barely clocked" — the normalization complete — and then her arms slow coming down. "She hadn't meant to hold on... She put it down to that." That last line is the whole book in miniature: the accurate observation, then the plausible wrong explanation, freely chosen.

Trust and suspicion: my suspicion of Randi is at its highest yet and every degree of it is earned on this page — the cat-stillness, the extraction, the deniability ("as if she'd meant nothing in the world by it. And Vee couldn't tell whether she had" — neither can I, and I notice the page keeps that door ajar on purpose: how much of this was appetite rather than plan). And still: "so pleased to see her that Vee felt it land, warm all through" reads as real pleasure to me. Both keep being true. Pace isn't in the room and I missed him, which is its own strange datum.

Titles: "The Practice Room" is perfect and only fully arrives at the end — a soundproof cell where music students rehearse and freshmen fooled around, where Randi rehearses Vee in a script Vee doesn't know exists. Practice for what performance? That's the dread. It gave nothing away going in; it recolored everything coming out. A Polite Invitation and the tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — have never sat closer to the page than "Did you deserve it?" / "Probably I deserved it." A yes, freely given, obtained.

What I want next: I dread and want the same thing — the moment Vee's want for Randi runs out of explanations. Guesses from here: the shoes never got handed back (she pulled them out and I don't think they left the room with Randi), and that will matter; and Cassie saw more from her seat than the page said.
