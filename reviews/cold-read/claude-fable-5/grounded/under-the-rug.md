# Cold read (grounded) — Under the Rug

## Reader reaction

This one hurt, and it hurt in the exact place "Seconds" left tender. She offered her best sentence on the porch and got "I love how good we are together" back, and now the book gives me the aftermath in full: the word right there in the dark, "simple, wanting out," and her deciding — again — not to risk it. What comes out instead, "at the wrong size for the room," is one of the truest things I've read in this book: "Is this what we are now?" too loud, a daytime voice in a murmuring room, and then her lying still and "listening to herself not having whispered." I know that exact human failure — meaning to say nothing and saying the sharp thing instead — and the page renders it without one ounce of authorial cushioning.

How I feel about them right now: Vee I love and I flinched for her all chapter. She is being unfair and she half-knows it — the page is scrupulous about this: "It wasn't true. Some back room of her knew which of them had gone cold that night, knew who'd carried a pie up whose porch steps after — and she said it anyway." That's her, carrying HUMBLE PIE up his steps in the cognac heels, and she weaponizes the memory backwards because "she needed the line of it to hold." I don't like her less for it. I like her more, because the book lets her be mean out of need and then shows me the need: "she needed him, right now, more than he looked anywhere near needing her, and the needing made her mean." That's the interior life I read for.

Pace — this chapter moved something for me, and it's complicated in both directions. The Chili's scene is the first time I've watched him fail at anything. His eyes slipping to the screen, the bringing-back that suddenly "had a cost in it," the fork squared to the table edge, the flicker of irritation he hides by dropping his eyes — and then his own account: "Not the sound of it. The whole of it, all at once. In a room like that I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you." I believed every word of that, and it recolors the whole courtship — the tiny quiet restaurants, Christine's, the velvet room, the concrete square with a boom box, the house. She reads the restaurant as proof she's just-sex; I read it as a man genuinely overloaded, and I watched her misattribute it in real time ("The just-sex of it, proven in fluorescent light. The room roared its agreement"). The gap between what I saw and what she concluded is the chapter. And yet — the ledger I carry that she doesn't makes his not-saying darker than she can know. He gave her "every last thing but the one." Is the withheld word temperament, Daphne-scar-tissue, or the fact that there is another woman and a game, and the word would be a lie or a detonation? The page holds all three open and I'm holding them open with it. What I can't dismiss anymore: whatever the plan is, the man who lost his control tonight — "shaking, undone, hers" — was not performing.

Swoon: the sex at the end worked on me completely, and it worked because the chapter earned it emotionally first. Her wanting him rough — "wanted, with a sharpness that startled her, for his control to give" — and then getting it, after she comes: "he got over her and into her almost in one motion... like a man who'd run clean out of the strength to wait." Her astonished second climb, "her body answering his with no say from her in it" — that's the hottest passage in a while precisely because it answers the chapter's question in the body: he does lose himself to her. And then the book, mercilessly, has her lie awake doing the accounting anyway: "He'd given her all of it and not the name." The cooling sweat, his spend still in her, "marked all over with where he'd been" — gorgeous, and used as the platform for the ache, not as a cure for it.

The nachos destroyed me a little. "It was to food what a billboard is to a painting" — and then the turn: "He had ruined it for her, was the thing. Not just this." The feeding motif has been the book's love language for forty chapters, and here it goes bitter: she can never go back to fine, and fine used to be plenty. That's the jacket's "an appetite that can only give her the best of herself by taking it," landing on a skillet of orange cheese.

Trust vs. suspicion: my one earned unease inside the chapter is her "forgiven" — the page flags it as a garment she puts on ("she told herself the thing that let her go... warm and large and easy to wear") and then strips it four pages later ("It did not feel like plenty now"). That's not suspicion of him; it's watching her self-deception cycle at speed. Of Pace, my standing double vision holds: the tenderness is real and the situation is rigged, and this chapter deepened both sides at once. No new suspicion of anyone else; Randi doesn't appear and I felt her absence — Vee has no one to bring this to except the woman who reports to him.

Friction: almost none. If I squint, the close narration naming her self-deceptions ("some back room of her knew") walks right up to the line of doing her thinking for her — but it never crosses; it reads as honesty inside her, not the author over her.

The title: "Under the Rug" is exactly the pile from the laundry room — "She put it where she put things she did not know what to do with. The place was not empty." The chapter ends with her adding the biggest thing yet to it: the whole nameless status of her life's best thing. It also plays sourly against "swept clean through" in the afterglow — swept, yes, and where. The book's title keeps sharpening: "Okay. Where do you want to go tonight?" is another polite invitation, another door held open with nothing to push against — and the tagline's "every yes was freely given, that was the problem" now applies to the yeses she gives herself: forgiven, plenty, fine.

What I want next: I dread the collision of this new grievance with Randi's brunch table — Vee arriving with "he won't name us" is exactly the confession Randi can do the most with, in either direction. Guesses from here: Randi will name it for her — give her a frame, maybe "he loves you, he just can't say it," maybe something that draws Vee further toward herself. And the not-saying, I now suspect (guess only), isn't coldness — it's that the word is mortgaged. I want to keep reading with a kind of low dread that feels exactly like caring.
