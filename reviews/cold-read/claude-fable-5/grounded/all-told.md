# Cold read (grounded) — All Told

## Reader reaction

This is a small chapter and I loved being in it, which is its own kind of alarming, because it's the trap in miniature: Vee "happy in the middle," one girl on each side, and I spent the whole scene warm and smiling and only afterward noticed the professor had told me exactly what I was looking at.

Start with the joy, because the chapter runs on it. Vee floating in late to everything and "it didn't bother her a whit" — after twenty chapters of debit-card arithmetic and menu-pricing and the mother's voice, watching her be sloppy and underslept and "overfull" is genuinely delicious. The made bed as "a tattletale in the cold morning light for anyone who cared to read it" is a perfect little image, and the chapter immediately pays it off: Cassie read it hours ago and let Randi have the detective work anyway. That's Cassie all over, and this chapter is quietly a Cassie chapter for me.

Because the beat that moved me most isn't erotic at all. It's Vee surfacing from forty honest seconds of note-taking to realize "Cassie had never gone down into them at all" — that Cassie has been turned a few degrees in her seat this whole time, watching not the dress story but what the dress did to her friend, "because the friend was the only part of any of it Cassie had ever been able to see." And then the plainest exchange in the book: "Was it a good night?" — "Yes. Oh, yes." — and something eases in Cassie's face and she picks her highlighter back up. That's the whole friendship in four lines. I have said in earlier chapters that Cassie is the one person here I trust without reservation, and this chapter didn't just confirm it, it made the trust ache a little. She's been standing watch. She stood down for exactly one highlighter's worth of relief.

And on the other side: Randi, being Randi, which is to say irresistible and — now that I know what I know — working. "Tell me everything. Leave nothing out" is her whole method said out loud as a joke. "You lucky slut," low and delighted, tips the register over — and lands "lower than Randi meant it to," on the night's soreness Vee's body has been keeping account of, which is the chapter's one flush of real heat, private, under the clothes, in a lecture hall. Then Randi finds "the one snag in the gold of the day" — no shoes — and I want to be careful here, because the line that chilled me is doing double work: "Randi had reached in and found it." On the page it's a friend spotting a problem. In my reading it's also exactly what Randi does — locate the gap and fill it, instantly, with herself: "That's not a problem, that's a *date*. You and me, Saturday." Even the phrasing — "a heel height Vee would need and hadn't known to need" — is Randi's entire relationship to Vee in one clause. Vee needed shoes even in Peekaboo (the worn pumps that would "read like an apology"); Randi has now made herself the answer to it. It went through Vee warm, and she didn't say no. Of course she didn't. I wouldn't have either. That's the book.

And Cassie's eyebrow goes up, and comes back down. "Cassie had never once in her life cared what was on anyone's feet." I don't know what that eyebrow holds — the page doesn't say, and I won't fix it — but it's the third or fourth time something has crossed Cassie's face around Randi and been folded away, and I am starting to brace for the chapter where it doesn't fold.

Now the professor. Simpson's paradox, delivered "in the flat unbothered voice of a man reading the back of a cereal box": each year on its own, the first man is the better hitter; put the years together and the second man is. "Same numbers. Nobody touched one of them." This is the stats class doing its oracle thing for the third time — latent variables when Randi first sat down, error-correcting codes in his den, and now this — and I'll be honest about both halves of my reaction. Half of me registered the pattern as a pattern: I can now feel the lecture coming when Vee walks into stats, and a device you can feel coming has lost a little of its charge. But the other half of me got a genuine cold drip down the back of the neck, because this one cuts closer than the others. Taken separately, every piece of Vee's life is true and good: Pace's tenderness is real, Randi's warmth keeps leaking past performance, every yes was hers. Put them together — aggregate them — and the picture reverses without a single fact being touched. "Nobody touched one of them." Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over. Same numbers. I actually put the page down for a second on that.

Which is what makes the title land so well. "All Told" — Randi's demand ("tell me everything"), Vee's compulsive telling, the book's running question of how much of the middle she ladles out. But "all told" is also the accountant's phrase: *summed up, in aggregate*. The chapter is literally about what happens when you put the numbers together. That's the best title double of the run so far, innocuous going in, loaded coming out, and it gave nothing away in advance.

The last line is quietly devastating in a way I can't fully parse yet and don't want to: Vee walking into the bright cold noon "happy and starving and on time, now, for nothing, the dress three blocks back in a closet, zipped in its bag, still shown to not one living soul." Happy and starving — the book's oldest pairing, still fused. And the dress unshown — the most finished, most *her* thing she's ever owned, and the only people who've seen her in it are the man who made it and a mirror. Everything truest about her life right now is zipped in a bag or edited in the telling. On time for nothing.

Friction: almost none. It's a breather chapter and knows it; at this length it earns itself. The only thumb-on-scale flicker is the lecture device's familiarity, noted above — and even that is played deadpan, uncommented, with Vee bored by it, which is the right way to do it. One small chronology wobble I had to reread: Randi's "It is a Friday… On a school night" — so the Peekaboo afternoon ran through Thursday night. Fine once I placed it, but I did have to place it.

What I want next: the shoe trip, and I want it with my stomach slightly clenched, because a Saturday alone with Randi, spending money on Vee's body, in the afterglow — that is deep water, and the fire-lane kisses have been escalating. What I dread, as a guess from here only: that Cassie's eyebrow is going to cost her something before it costs Randi anything. And the induction is still out there, the dress waiting to be shown to living souls, the mother in the audience. The book is holding a lot of lit fuses very calmly.
