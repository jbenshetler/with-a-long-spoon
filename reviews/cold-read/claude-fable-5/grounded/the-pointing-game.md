## Reader reaction

So there it is. The jacket told me it "began as a game between the two people now closest to her," and I have just watched the game get invented — invented in bed, mid-orgasm, and then played across a whole gorgeous quad until it landed on a laughing red-haired girl coming out of a dining hall. My stomach did something complicated when Pace said "Her," and pointed, and I realized I was looking at Vee before Randi was — before Vee had a name, before she was anything but "the most alive one in sight." The jacket promised I'd be ahead of her from the start. I did not expect being ahead of her to feel like this: half swoon, half a cold drop through the floor.

Let me do the body first, because the chapter starts in the body. The opening sex is superb — explicit, unhurried, and doing character work with every stroke. "He knew the difference between a woman performing release and a woman delivered to it" is exactly the register this book has earned. And the pivot — "Would you like to taste another woman?" asked into her while he's inside her, and her body answering with an orgasm "far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it" — that's the hottest and most psychologically loaded thing the book has done since the bench. The want announces itself before she can dress it. Then the daylight re-ask, which I genuinely respected: he waits for "her gathered back into herself when he asked, free to weigh it and choose." That's the consent grammar this book keeps honoring even while everything else gets morally complicated, and it's why the heat still works on me instead of curdling.

Randi's counter-move is the best piece of characterization in the chapter: "Only in a threesome," she said against his throat, smiling, all silk. "With you." And Pace's reading of it — "The same act. A different owner." — is devastating and, I think, right. After "Standards," after the bathroom mirror in "The Bench" where she decided "he could have the face... not ever," I know exactly what she's doing: she cannot own the wanting, so she hands it to him and becomes its generous instrument. What complicates it — what I want to hold open rather than resolve — is that the chapter keeps showing the face *failing*. The bare "Yes" when he points at Vee, "with nothing around it — the bare arrival of a thing already true before she got to it." The dropped stride. The grin she can't hide. The happy footwork "she didn't decide to do." Her resolve in the mirror was "not ever," and here she is, four weeks in, leaking realness in daylight. I don't know if the deadbolt is rusting or if Pace simply sees through every door she builds. The page doesn't say. I'm leaving it ajar.

How I feel about Pace has moved, and I want to be precise about it. In "The Bench" his patience read as devotion; I trusted him with her almost completely. Here the same qualities — the watching, the reading, the certainty — are aimed *outward*, at a stranger who doesn't know she's in a game. His interior narration is full of generosity ("She deserved that gentleness. He wanted it for her"), and I half believe it, and I also notice how completely he narrates Randi's inner life, how sure he is of what she wants and what's good for her. He might be right every time. The being-right is the thing that unsettles me. And then the collision: Randi's engineered stumble, so clean "the redhead's hand flew to her own mouth... apologizing" — Vee apologizing for a collision that was staged at her. That's where the chill actually landed. It's beautiful to watch — "so clean it looked like nothing" — and it is a con, run on a girl whose only offense was laughing with her whole body, "like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen." Being seen is the thing this book keeps saying is dangerous. She's about to be seen by two experts.

And yet — honesty about the swoon — Randi coming back across the lawn glowing, "Her name is Vee. And we've got a stats class together," the double "*Yes*," first squeaked and delighted, then "breathy this time, the register she only used in his bed and never in daylight" — I melted. That's real joy breaking through a woman who almost never has any that isn't manufactured, and it's joy *about a woman*, out loud, in sunlight, with her hands in her boyfriend's hair. If I didn't have the jacket, this would read as pure liberation. I do have the jacket. Both things are true at once and the chapter refuses to pick, which is exactly what I want from it.

The hunger motif got its cruelest beat yet, almost in passing: the cheeseburger. "No. Easy, automatic, not even a glance back at the tray. 'I can't eat that.'" *Can't*, not won't — after the apple sliced thin, after Pace's tenderness about "a girl who had been portioning herself." And then the last line: "Let's go feed you." It closes the chapter like a bell. Feed her what? On the surface, dinner. Underneath, they have just spent the afternoon shopping for the meal. I actually put my hand over my mouth at how quietly that line does its double work.

Friction, small: Pace's mid-scene meditations occasionally explain a beat I'd already felt — "If pleasure was also a bridge, then let the wanting simply feel good in her" tips toward telling me the mechanism. And the parade of rejected girls runs one candidate long for me (the camel-coat "project" and the blazer chair-of-things started to feel like a catalogue), though I concede each rejection teaches me Randi's taste, which pays off when the taste breaks pattern on Vee — not thin, not curated, not managed. The girl she wants is everything her own discipline forbids. That landed.

Titles. "The Pointing Game" is exact and, for once, the title *did* tip me slightly — I knew from the jacket a game was coming, so the moment he said "I point. You pick," I heard the title click and knew where the afternoon ended. I don't resent it; the book has always let me be ahead. *A Polite Invitation* recolors hard here: the collision-and-apology IS the polite invitation, courtesy weaponized into an opening. And the tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — this chapter is a machine for producing freely given yeses: Randi's clenched-around-him yes, her bare "Yes" on the walk, the squeaked and then breathy yeses at the end. All free. All hers. That's the problem.

What I want next: Vee's POV, badly. I've now seen her exactly as the jacket promised — from outside, pointed at — and I ache to be inside her before these two reach her. Guess from here: the stats class is the vector; Randi will befriend her first, and the "lunches too intimate to name" begin. What I dread is how good it's going to feel.
