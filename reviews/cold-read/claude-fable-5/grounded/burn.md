## Reader reaction

The first thing I have to say about "Burn" is what isn't in it. The last chapter ended with Vee's mouth still tasting of Randi, her hands not steady, wanting to do it again "so badly" — and this chapter opens with "I won't get my brunch this weekend... Randi's got some sorority thing Saturday," and then Randi vanishes from Vee's head entirely. Not one thought of the kiss. Not one. After forty pages of Vee cataloguing Randi's perfume "without admitting she was keeping the file," the silence is deafening, and I genuinely can't tell whose silence it is — Vee's (not looking at it, which would be the most in-character thing she's ever done) or Randi's (a "sorority thing" with no plus-one, one week after she almost lost control of her own bit? that reads like a woman managing distance). The page doesn't resolve it, so I won't. But I sat with that opening line longer than any line in the chapter.

What the chapter actually is: a two-hander, Vee and Pace, and it worked on me almost continuously. The gym section is the purest distillation yet of why I half-love this man against my own better knowledge. "That was the thing that got into her first. Not the weight. That he took her weight seriously. That the smallness of what she was doing bought her back none of his care" — that's the whole Pace thesis in three sentences, and it lands because the page earns it in procedure: the hands seated one finger at a time, the feet walked back, and then the detail that made me put the book down for a second — he loaded his own bar only to what he could rack himself, "so that she was never once responsible for what she couldn't hold. She hadn't even felt the danger, because he had never let there be any." That is either the most romantic sentence in the book or the most chilling, and I honestly believe it's both, because that sentence describes the entire novel. She has never felt the danger. He has never let there be any — visible.

"Everything gets a collar." He says it about a five-pound plate, without looking up, and I felt the line ring backward one chapter to Randi's wrist: the Love bracelet, no clasp, "taking it off requires tools." The book put those two objects a chapter apart and I refuse to believe it's an accident of my own reading. Everything Pace keeps, he collars — for safety, he'd say, and the boys' uncollared bar promptly proves him right by putting a hot wire in Vee's neck. Safety and ownership wearing the same hardware. That's the symbolism doing exactly what this book does: the innocent reading is fully load-bearing, and the other reading stands right behind it.

The confrontation scared me a little and thrilled me more, and I want to be honest about the ratio. "The size of him changed without his moving at all." "You hurt her. Your carelessness hurt her. Apologize to the woman you hurt." The voice going down instead of up. It's protective, it's controlled, it's hot — and the page is careful to give me Vee's actual experience of it, which is that she wanted "for all of it to stop pointing at her," and that the boys apologized to her but looked to him for release. That last detail is quietly excellent: even her accident becomes a room he governs. Then "We're done," decided over her "I'm fine" — and I noticed the override, and I also noticed she was wrong and he was right, and that's how it always goes with him, isn't it. Being right is his deepest form of authority.

Then the balm scene, which is the strangest and darkest erotic set piece the book has run, and it had me completely. The mechanism is diabolical: camphor heat on a timer, so that her two yeses are spent before the fire arrives, and she gets to watch her own future come down her body — "The neck was what the breast would be. The breast was what the tip would be." That's terror and arousal fused so tightly the prose stops distinguishing them ("she gave up telling them apart"), and crucially, the consent architecture holds all the way through and is hotter for holding: he tries to spare her ("one place on her he would carry the heat past"), she refuses the mercy, she pulls his wrist, she says yes before he asks and then he asks anyway and makes her say it again. "Even with the word already given, he would not take it on the strength of her body alone." I believe every step of her choosing. And — the "Barely Stings" rhyme is unmissable now — this is the second chapter running where Vee says yes to a burn whose full size she learns only after the yes can't be called back. The wax, now the fire. She even names it herself: she understood "much too late, the size of the thing she had started." The book is teaching her, chapter by chapter, that the too-late is the point, that the fear arriving after the yes is "nearly the point of it." I felt the heat of that and the cold of it in the same breath, which I think is exactly what I was meant to feel.

The ending wrecked me gently. "Not tonight. We need to wait" — and the chapter hears what I heard: "We. The word went through her almost worse than the burn." He denies her the finish not as play but because it would cost her neck, and he brings her down without taking his hands away — "banking the heat under his palms" — and gives her "nothing but to be held there burning and take it." Also: "Not tonight" is the scar's phrase, his own locked door, now spent on her body. I don't know if Vee caught it. I did. And the last line — "She carried home the places he had not finished touching" — is a perfect closing note for a chapter about deferred fire, and, if I let myself hear it, for the whole Randi-shaped silence too.

Friction: almost none. The balm escalation runs long — the heat's geography gets one or two more waypoints than it strictly needs — but the slowness is the scene, so I'll barely dock it. Nothing telegraphed; the collar rhyme is left entirely for me to make.

The title: "Burn" is honest and triple. The balm, literally; the denied climax, banked; and the thing she carried home still lit. After "Barely Stings," the two titles read like a matched pair — the lie and the truth about the same sensation. Against the book's title and tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" — this chapter is the tagline performed on skin: two freely given yeses, and the fire arriving after, unrecallable. *A Polite Invitation*, indeed: "Do you want me to keep going?" is the politest sentence in it, and the invitation was to fire.

What I want next: the debrief that didn't happen. Vee owes Randi a phone call from two chapters ago ("I want the whole debrief of your own party") and the book has now skipped past the kiss twice. Guess from here: the next Randi chapter will be Randi's management of the aftermath — either doubling the distance or closing it — and Vee's body, which "had not been told" after the kiss and was denied again tonight, is being wound to a pitch where somebody is going to receive all of it at once. I'm braced for who.
