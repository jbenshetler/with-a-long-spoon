FELT READ

This chapter is almost all sweetness — and then one object in one sentence dropped the temperature of my whole body, and I have to talk about that first because it's still ringing. Vee, three steps into his bedroom, takes off her own thong "and threw. It landed green on the low table of odd proportions that stood in the middle of his bedroom. She never looked to see where it went."

I looked to see where it went. I know that table. That is the bench — the spanking-and-edging apparatus he built by hand, the "table that was never only a table" that Randi used to lay her underwear on before she knew what it was for. It's in his bedroom now, standing in the middle of it, and Vee's green satin lands on it exactly the way Randi's things once did, and the page gives it four words of her attention — "of odd proportions" — and moves on. That's the whole book in a sentence: the instrument sits in plain sight, furniture-shaped, and the woman who loves this house doesn't know to look at it. I got a genuine chill, and I want to be precise: nothing in the scene is sinister. He does nothing with the bench. It just waits, and the chapter's title — the new ordinary — suddenly includes it. The ordinary has an apparatus standing in the middle of it.

Now the rest, which worked on me almost embarrassingly well. The opening is one of the truest things the book has done with Vee's interior: she took five nights for school, her own call, and "the plain grace of that had cost her more than a fight would have, because some unreasonable corner of her had wanted him to fight." That's exactly the crooked shape of wanting — resenting a kindness because it didn't perform enough need — and the book lets her have it without tidying it. And the flip side is there too, quieter, if you want it: "He never said so. He only opened the door and waited on the far side of it for her to come back through." As pure romance, that's the most patient man alive. Against everything I know that Vee doesn't, a man who never asks, never argues, never closes a door, and simply waits for you to come back through — that is also a description of how the whole invitation has worked from the beginning. The chapter doesn't press it. I felt it anyway.

The reunion itself is the funniest, most human sex scene the book has given us. The undressing in the hall is glorious comedy — the cold jeans that "had been sensible in the car and were the enemy now," the one-legged hop, the socks peeled off against the wall while he stands there enjoying himself, "and there was no dignity anywhere in any of it and she'd have burned the house down for him." The bra she has to unwind out of "like something off a spool" because he holds it and lets her keep going — that's him in miniature, never pulling, only holding, so that every departure from cover is technically hers. Swoon and design in the same gesture again; I can't get them apart anymore, and I think that's the point.

The sex: her first orgasm arrives "shamefully fast... her body having just published the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private" — that line is perfect, the ledger-keeping shame Vee does turned into a joke her own throat tells on her. The second, slower one, face in his neck, him shaking "just slightly" — tender, real, earned. Not the book's hottest scene, but among its warmest.

Then the pizza, which I expected to skim and instead read like it was plot, because in this book food is plot. "He wasn't performing any of it for her... He'd have made the pizza exactly like this for an empty house" — she takes that as proof of authenticity, and I half do too, and the other half of me notes that his care being temperament rather than courtship is precisely what makes it impossible to read as strategy. Same numbers, opposite verdicts; the stats professor lives rent-free in my head now. Her tasting — "the difference between cotton candy and a bowl of cut strawberries" — is the awakening motif at its best: he has rebuilt her palate, her sleep, her body, her ordinary.

And the flannel. This is where the chapter got me completely. She washed the shirt because "it stopped smelling like you... and one day last week it didn't smell like anything at all" — the checkpoint memory of the washed flannel "smelling of no one" after the Rusty Nail disaster made this land twice as hard — and then the ask, shoved out fast: "I need you to wear it for me. The whole weekend. And give it back to me Sunday so it smells like you again." His face going from puzzlement to almost-hurt (he thought she was returning it) to the smile "that seemed to cost him something," and then pulling it on right there, over his own shirt, two shirts in his warm kitchen because she told him to. I teared up with her. This is the chapter's real declaration — not "I love you," which she still hasn't said aloud, but a ritual for being filled and refilled with him. It's devotion expressed as scent-logistics and it's the most romantic thing in the book so far.

One small line I keep turning over: "He decided not to ask. He was good at not asking things." On this page it's tact. Across the book it's the deepest thing about him — the man who never opens a closed door, who lets everything be handed over. The page means it warmly. I heard both registers.

Trust and suspicion: no new suspicion earned by anyone's behavior here — Pace is nothing but generous on this page. My unease is entirely the bench, and that's not paranoia; the book put it in the bedroom and put her underwear on it. Randi and Cassie are offstage, and after "The Outlier" the quiet of this chapter felt like a held breath — a whole chapter of pure Pace-and-Vee domesticity right after Randi's world showed a crack.

The titles: "The New Ordinary" is exact and, on reflection, double-edged — the extraordinary (a man who cooks from nothing, a drawer of her own, three-times-a-night wanting) has become her ordinary, and the ordinary now includes an unexplained low table she throws her underwear at without looking. It didn't give anything away; it deepened after. Against A Polite Invitation and "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" — this chapter is the tagline's evidence file. Every single thing here is freely given: the five nights, the return, the photo-drawer, the shirt. That's what makes it feel so safe, and that's what the jacket keeps telling me is the problem.

What I want next: I want the bench to stay furniture and I don't believe it will — that's a guess from here, but you don't move an instrument into the bedroom and never play it, and Vee has already learned (the bet, the spanking) that her body answers his hand. I also want to know what "I don't go back," from last chapter, is guarding, and I dread whatever Randi does with the crack that opened under MIRH. And Thanksgiving is coming: Vee to Ohio, Pace and Sheri at one table. I'd read that chapter tonight.
