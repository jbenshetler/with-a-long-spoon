## Reader reaction

FELT READ

This one went down easy and left a taste I keep going back to check. It's the third of these Randi-debrief lunches — Dear, How It's Done, now Fed — and I walked in knowing the shape: the glamorous table, the "sad little story first," the warm extraction. And it still worked on me, because the extraction has changed what it's after. Last time Randi wanted the mechanics and closed the door with that eerie "You don't have to tell me." This time she's after something else entirely: she wants Vee to *own* it. "You knew how that night was going to end before you ever got in the car... So why are you telling me like it's a confession?" — and Vee, who has spent ten chapters being handed permission in careful teaspoons, says the sentence I've been waiting the whole book to hear her say: "I wanted it. And I took it. For once."

I felt that land in my chest before I felt anything sinister about it. That's the book's whole cruel trick and I keep falling for it on purpose: the thing Randi is doing to Vee is also, line by line, the best thing anyone has ever done for her. "You're not caught, sweetheart. You're lucky" — I actually flinched at that one, because it's tender and true in the register Randi says it in, and it is also the single most loaded sentence anyone has spoken in this book. Vee is caught. She is precisely, literally caught, in a net this exact woman knotted, and Randi says the word *caught* out loud, holding her hand, and turns it into a gift. I don't know if Randi hears her own irony. The page doesn't tell me, and I'm leaving that ajar — but the reader-shiver was real.

The dark hum under the brunch, for me, was simpler and stranger than any of that: Randi is asking her own lover's new girlfriend how he was in bed. "How was he?" — and Vee tells her he was patient, gentle, hungry, that he made her feel *singular*. Last chapter Randi's stillness cracked through — the tight smile, the thumb working the glass. This time her face stays "open and warm and glad for her friend," start to finish. Either she's managing it better or she's genuinely glad, and I honestly can't pick, and I notice the book has trained me to be frightened of Randi precisely when she's smooth. Same with her "sad little story" — the beautiful old boyfriend in a hurry. After the "catastrophe date" performance at the Smoothie Bird I have no idea whether this is a real encounter, a fabricated single-girl alibi, or something in between; what I *did* notice is the pattern: Randi's stories always cast her as the hungry one and Vee as the fed one. "Give me something to live on." "I'm running on fumes here." She scripts her own starvation to draw Vee's abundance out — and given the series title, that "something to live on" line has teeth I don't think Vee can feel.

Swoon: yes, quietly. The sheet-at-midnight retelling — "wrapped up in him, watching him cook" — got me all over again secondhand, and Randi's "That's not a painting... Keep that one" (she got there *first*, again, right when Vee's real secret was surfacing — I clocked it, the same soft interception as last time) is such a good line that I forgave its function. And the curb: the hand at the hinge of the jaw, the second press, and this time "Vee had half-expected it... and met it, and when Randi let her go she didn't look down the street for who might have seen them." That's the biggest single movement in the chapter and it's rendered in one clause. Vee met it. The scanning-the-windows panic of last time is simply gone. The Vee–Randi thing is escalating entirely inside the "French thing" fiction, and Vee is now an active participant in the fiction. My stomach did something complicated there — half melt, half drop.

Small true things I loved: the menu read "the way you read a road in fog" again, the mimosa arithmetic "that ended in Thursday" — Vee's poverty as a felt weekly rhythm — and then the second mimosa "she did not price at all." That's characterization by ledger and it's exquisite. The frosted-plum interrogation was the chapter's sharpest tenderness: Randi reading the toes ("You paint the toes for a man who's going to have your feet bare in his hands"), then the color, then "That face isn't about a color, is it" — peeling Vee in three questions flat. Vee's unsaid answer — the polish chosen to wake the burgundy silk of a dress that doesn't exist yet — is the most romantic detail in the chapter and Randi never even gets it; only I do.

Friction: honestly little. The chapter is short and knows it. If I'm strict, the road-in-fog line is a verbatim reuse from two chapters ago — I read it as deliberate motif and it mostly landed that way, but it flickered as stencil for a second. And a third consecutive Randi-lunch does carry a shape-familiarity; the escalation earns it, but I wouldn't want a fourth before something breaks pattern.

Titles: "Fed" is perfect and double-edged. It answers "Famished" — Vee finally fed, in every sense, and saying so with her whole face. But the chapter quietly asks who else is feeding: Randi arrives hungry by her own account, "running on fumes," begging "something to live on," and leaves fed on Vee's telling. The bistro even has a sun porch of glass — Vee gets fitted for her happiness in one sun porch and debriefed on it in another. *A Polite Invitation* and *With a Long Spoon*: the supping-with-the-devil proverb gets more literal every time these two share a table, and the jacket's "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." is now describing Vee's own proud sentence — "I wanted it. And I took it." That yes was freely given. That is the problem.

What I want next: I want the dress finished and the induction, and I dread the mechanism that has to be coming — the plan has been dormant as *plan* for chapters now while everything real grows over it, and the longer it sleeps the worse the waking will be. Guesses from here: the curb kisses keep escalating until one of them isn't deniable; and at some point Randi hears one detail of Pace-with-Vee that she can't fold away, and I think it will be a detail like *singular*.
