# Cold read (grounded) — Barely Stings

## Reader reaction

This chapter is small — one errand, one room, one hour — and it might be the most concentrated dose of the whole book I've had yet. Nothing happens except a waxing appointment, and I came out the other side of it feeling like I'd read the novel's thesis written on Vee's body in white shocks.

Start with what swept me. The salon's honey light, the bowl of apples "nobody was meant to eat," and then that gorgeous little taxonomy on the couch: the girl who came to be looked at, the girl who came to be touched, and "Vee sat down between them and understood which one she was." That's the book doing what it does best — Vee reading the world and quietly filing herself, and the filing itself being half erotic and half a wound. And the swallowed joke — "you're going to want me drunk for this?" — the reflex to "set her a half-inch above the moment," declined. She takes the wine instead. She stays in the moment. That's the Vee of the coat night, the Vee who stopped at the mirror on purpose; she keeps choosing the harder, more present thing, and I keep falling for her when she does.

The flashback to the kitchen is where the chapter's real machinery sits, and it made my stomach do the thing it does when this book is being honest about itself. "I'd like you waxed. By the weekend. Bare." — and then "Will you?" — and her yes arriving "out ahead of her, her mouth answering before her mind had caught up to it." The page then says the merciless thing straight out: "the fear arriving too late to be any use — that was nearly the point of it." And later, on the table, the whole jacket in one sentence: "She'd already said yes to the whole of it, in a bright kitchen three nights back, without once asking what the whole of it was." Every yes was freely given. That was the problem. I didn't have to reach for that reading; the chapter handed it to me and dared me to enjoy the scene anyway. And I did enjoy it — that's the trap working exactly as built, on me as much as on her.

Randi. Oh, Randi. "Barely stings" — a hand over Vee's, "So worth it, baby," and not one word about rolling over. The chapter is scrupulous about not accusing her: it could be a rich girl's breezy sell, the way you undersell a flu shot to a friend. But I've watched Randi steer Vee at every fork for fifty chapters, and I noticed that her briefing covered the wine, the name to ask for, the feeling of walking out expensive — everything about the surface — and omitted exactly the part where Vee would be face-down, blind, "presented," with no wanting in it and nothing to keep back. Whether that omission was careless or calibrated, the page doesn't say, and I'm holding it open. But the title made me hold the sentence up to the light, and it doesn't pass clean. "Randi was always one road further along. It was restful, being told." That line ached and chilled me in the same breath.

The Sondra section is superb, and here's where I have to be honest about the erotic charge: it worked on me, and it worked in the strangest register the book has tried yet. Not the pain — the grammar. "Her body knew that grammar. Knew a hand pressing it down and telling it to hold still... and here was the shape of it with everything true scooped out of the middle. The wrong hand." That is an extraordinary piece of writing about arousal — the body recognizing a form (Pace's "Keep still" at the fitting, the pin, the bench-learned stillness) stripped of its meaning, and the absence itself becoming the ache. "It held her the exact way she wanted to be held and it did not want her at all." I felt that in my chest and lower. And then the ending — alone, her own hand on the "bare astonishing smoothness," "the sound she made was not one of pain," and immediately she's not thinking of her own hand but of his, "learning by touch what she had gone and done for him" — that's the cleanest swoon in the chapter, and it's earned.

The mother's voice came back on schedule and I noticed something: "the kind of girl who." She shut her eyes on it. "It stayed where it was. It always did." — no longer a flood, just a resident. The checkpoint told me it was fading in force by the coat chapter; here it's furniture. That feels like real, hard-won movement in her, even in a chapter where she's letting herself be handled.

Pace, from a distance, is the most ambiguous he's been. The request itself is courtly-clean — he asked, he waited, she answered. But "He never told her why. He asked for the thing itself, clean, and left the place where the reason should be for her to fill" — that's the first time the book has described his method in a way that sounds like a technique rather than a temperament, and coming two chapters after I watched him warm every one of her friends with the same effortless precision, I felt a draft. Not distrust. A draft. The page earned exactly that much and no more.

Friction: almost none. If I squint, the couch-girls taxonomy is one notch too neat — but it's so pleasurable I won't press the charge. The chapter is short and knows it; nothing drags.

The title: "Barely Stings" is Randi's line, and after the chapter it reads as the book's whole method in two words — the true thing undersold, the invitation with the hard part left off the card. It didn't spoil anything going in (I assumed it meant the wax); coming out, it points straight at Randi, and at every warm meal in this book. *A Polite Invitation* and the long-spoon proverb have never sat closer to the surface: she was politely invited to a table, said yes, and only face-down did she learn what the whole service was.

What I want next: the weekend. His hand finding what she did. And underneath that, a low hum of dread I can't shake — guess from here — that this chapter is a rehearsal in miniature for a bigger roll-over: some moment where Vee discovers that the "whole of it" she said yes to, months ago, on a lawn, included parts nobody described. The book just showed me how she responds when she finds out mid-table. She doesn't say no. That scares me more than anything in the chapter.
