# Cold read (grounded) — Above Him

*scene: scenes/above-him.md · model: claude-fable-5 · memory: ck-ch030 + raw ch031..ch034 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

This one made my chest hurt, in the good way — and I want to be honest about that before I let any of what I know back into the room. "Above Him" is the quietest chapter in a long while, and it worked on me almost totally. After the induction's public heat, the bet, and the awful glazed sugar of "One Bite," the book hands me a chapter where nothing happens except a woman realizing she's in love, and it might be the most dangerous chapter yet — not because anything on the page is sinister, but because I have never wanted her to be safe more than I did watching her whisper "Peter" to a sleeping man.

Part i is the swoon, full strength. The door swinging open before she reached it, "her feet swinging out, the porch light wheeling once behind his head" — that lift is pure romance-novel body-joy and I felt it. And then the inventory she takes lying in the cooling sheet: "the door, the spin, the slowness, the salmon, the warm tomatoes, the coming back to her." That's the best kind of love-accounting — concrete, earned, all of it things I've watched him do across twenty chapters. "A man didn't do all of that for a girl he was only passing a season with. He had to feel it." Reader, I believe her. That's the problem, and it's my problem, not the page's — the page is clean. Her conclusion — "she could live clear inside the everything-he-did and not go hungry for the saying" — is beautiful and it also made me flinch, because the book has trained me on hunger. She's telling herself she doesn't need to be fed the words. This from the girl whose whole arc is learning to admit she's famished.

And that's where my one real unease lives, and I think the text earned it rather than my paranoia manufacturing it: the chapter is practically an essay on his wordlessness. "He felt it in everything he did and said it in nothing." "There was no note. There was never a note." "He hadn't said. He never said." Three separate beats, all landing on the same key. On the surface it's the most romantic thing in the book — a man who answers with a drawer instead of a sentence. But a man who never says is also a man whose silences I can't audit, and I know one enormous thing he isn't saying. The chapter let me hold both without forcing either, which is exactly what I want from it.

The weight-loss paragraph stopped me. "Her body quietly burning down whole weekends of him, and she'd never asked it to" — that's gorgeous and slightly eerie at once. She eats "like a field hand at his table now" and is getting smaller for it; the appetite motif literally consuming her. I don't think the page means it darkly — it reads as thriving — but the image of a girl being burned down by her own wanting sat with me longer than the salmon did.

Part ii and iii are a tiny two-act play about the counter, and the drawer reveal genuinely got me. The fake-out — form gone, counter bare, "the bottom went out of her morning," the dread of learning "where a guest's things went once the guest had been too much" — is Vee's whole self-worth machinery in one sentence. And then the top drawer: her scrunchies, her pins in a little dish, "room for more of her, laid out like something prepared and waiting." He compressed his entire ordered life into the bottom drawer and gave her "the one your hand goes to first." I melted. Completely. It's the moleskin, the dress, the thermostat again — love performed as carpentry and provisioning — and it's the closest thing to "I love you" he's ever produced. Though I'll note, because the checkpoint in me won't shut up: "laid out like something prepared and waiting" is also the exact grammar of the bench. In this house, things made ready for you are the love language and the trap in one grain of wood. The page didn't press that; I felt it anyway, one cold fingertip on a warm scene.

No Randi in this chapter at all — not even mentioned — and after "One Bite" that absence is its own kind of quiet. Vee doesn't think of her once. Given that the last chapter ended with pastry glaze on Vee's lip and a want she pinned to her own knee, the total silence reads like the door she shut in that bathroom stall holding, for now.

Erotic charge: low and deliberately so. The reunion sex is all retrospect — "a week of wanting coming out of them at once" — summarized, warm, not staged. The heat here is domestic: the robe belted twice, his arms crossing under hers at the sink, his mouth on her neck "no part of it asking for a thing." It simmers rather than burns, and after two high-heat chapters the cool-down felt right, not slack.

Friction: almost none. If I reach — the chapter is small, and part i leans on recap of a night we didn't see, so the salmon dinner has to carry sensory weight the sex doesn't get. It carried it fine. I didn't feel a thumb on the scale anywhere; if anything the restraint (no ominous note struck, no Randi shadow inserted) is the author trusting me to bring my own dread, which I did, free of charge.

The title: "Above Him" is lovely and double-jointed once you've read it. She's above him in the dark, mouth at his ear, saying the name she keeps — and her drawer is above his, the easy one, the one your hand goes to first. Both meanings are about her being given the high place in his ordered life. And there's a third reading I can't unread: the whole book is about what's above him and her both — the arrangement hanging over this bed that she can't see. The title didn't spoil anything; it deepened on the last page, which is what these titles keep doing. *A Polite Invitation* and "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" hang over this chapter with special cruelty, because this is the chapter of her freest, gladdest yes — the one she gives lying down in the dark, unwitnessed, to herself: she loves him. Nobody extracted that. That's the problem.

What I want next: I dread the symmetry the book is building. She loves him and hasn't said it; he "says it in nothing." Guesses from here: either she says it out loud soon and I find out what his face does with a word he can't answer honestly — or Randi gets the confession before he does, over a tablecloth, and I watch her receive it. Either one frightens me. The drawer says he's keeping her. The jacket says I don't yet know for what.
