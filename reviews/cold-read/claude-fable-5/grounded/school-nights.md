# Cold read (grounded) — School Nights

*scene: scenes/school-nights.md · model: claude-fable-5 · memory: ck-ch030 + raw ch031..ch035 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

After the run of big rooms — the induction stage, the brunch porch that nearly undid her, the drawer he emptied for her — this little chapter is a glass of water, and I drank it gratefully. It's just Vee and Cassie in the cold dorm room, past eleven, the window cracked its two inches, and it might be my favorite kind of scene this book does: nothing happens except that somebody who loves Vee looks at her carefully.

Cassie is the person I trust most in this book and this chapter is why. "You're doing the streetlight thing again. You're a public utility" — not looking up — and then the dry look "held a beat and then gave over, glad." That "glad" got me. She's not policing Vee; she's happy for her first, and only then does she do the arithmetic Vee hasn't: "You don't come home school nights." Not a question. And Vee "went back over the week she hadn't counted and found her own bed had sat empty more nights than it hadn't." That line is the chapter. Vee has stopped measuring herself — which, in one register, is the whole healing this book has given her (the girl who used to keep track of every bite, every dollar, every inch of thigh), and in another register is exactly the condition the jacket promised: she is deep inside something whose shape she isn't counting. The same fact, both verdicts, and the page doesn't pick. Simpson's paradox in a bean bag chair.

The thing that moved me most is the echo I don't think Vee hears herself make. Trying to explain Pace, she reaches and comes up with: the ones before "had been fine. Nice enough, a good enough time." That's the jacket's own language — "The men have been fine... Fine has never once been enough" — arriving in her mouth for the first time, and then she runs clean out of words, "which Vee never did," and laughs at herself reaching. That wordlessness read as truer than any speech she could have built.

And then the quiet gap that I loved: two chapters ago, in the dark, she let "she loved him" be true in her own chest and breathed "Peter" at his sleeping shape. Here, asked flat by her best friend — "Do you love him" — she goes small: "Maybe. I think so... It's all so fast." I don't read that as a lie. It's the difference between what she can afford at night against his side and what she can say in the cold lit room where it becomes a fact other people can hold. Cassie "let the maybe stand," didn't say a thing against it, didn't say I know — and went back to her book. That restraint is love, and it's also Cassie doing what she always does: filing, not prying. I still can't name what's in her watching — the checkpoint memory of "Goodbye, Vee" and "they will never know the love of a woman" hangs faintly over her flat face here — but this chapter gives me nothing to fix it with, and I'm leaving it ajar. On this page she's simply the one person taking Vee's measure with no angle.

Heat-wise it's barely a flicker, and rightly so — though the book can't quite help itself, and I grinned: the tomato burn on the side of her breast, her hand going to the spot "without her knowing it moved," and Cassie's "There's only one way *that* burn happens. Not enough on." Vee cooking half-dressed in his warm house is now so ordinary she doesn't even register that she's confessed it. "The house stayed warm now, warm enough that she never once thought about how much she had on" — the warm-house motif has fully become the shape of the relationship: a place where cover is unnecessary. Against it, Cassie's cracked window and the cold at the door.

No friction to speak of. It's short and it knows it; the one small thing I'd flag is that it's the third or fourth debrief-to-a-confidante scene in a short span (Cassie, Randi, Cassie), and only the wildly different temperature of the two listeners keeps the beat fresh — here it does keep it fresh, because Cassie asks the one question Randi never would: not what did he do to you, but do you love him.

The title: "School Nights" is Cassie's line, and it lands after rather than before — I read the chapter title as innocuous and only felt it when Cassie said it. It's a mother's-rules phrase, a childhood curfew word, and that's the sting: Vee has quietly stopped living by the old house rules — her mother's, the dorm's, her own careful ledger — and nobody has decided it, it's just happened, empty bed by empty bed. Under A Polite Invitation and "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem," a chapter about how she never actually said yes to moving her life to his house — it simply stopped being counted — feels dead on theme without the book ever raising its voice.

What I want next: I'm still carrying the brunch — "Sticky fingers?" and the sugar on Randi's goodbye kiss have not left my mouth any more than Vee's — and Cassie now counting nights makes me both hope and dread that she'll be the first to notice a pattern with a third person in it. Guess from here: Cassie's questions get more pointed before Randi's design does, and Vee's "Maybe" comes out as the full word to the wrong listener first.
