# Cold read (grounded) — The New Ordinary

## Reader reaction

The thong landed on the bench. That's the sentence I can't shake, buried in the middle of the happiest chapter this book has given me in a while: "It landed green on the long, unusually proportioned table that stood in the middle of his bedroom. She never looked to see where it went." I looked. I've been looking since chapter one, when he lifted the top off that table and Randi begged on it. Vee threw her underwear onto the altar of the whole arrangement without knowing it's anything but odd furniture, and the book didn't underline it, didn't even slow down — and that restraint made it land harder. One clause of ice in a chapter of warmth, and it recolored everything around it without dimming any of it. That's this book's whole trick and it still works on me.

Because the warmth is real, and I was swept. The doorway kiss with "the night still on her," the undressing that keeps going wrong — jeans cold from the drive, one leg down to the knee, his hands coming up to hold her exactly when she's most off-balance — I laughed out loud at "You're not helping." / "I'm helping." The bra he holds without pulling, so she has to unwind "like something off a spool" — that's the courtliness-as-seduction move I've watched him run since "May I," and it's still delicious. And the line that got me most in the sex itself: her body "having just published the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private." Five nights, counted in her throat. The book keeps finding new ways to say the same true thing about Vee — her appetite outruns her story about herself, and being caught at it is the hottest thing that happens to her.

The shirt scene wrecked me, plainly. She's been sleeping in that flannel since the rain on the hike; I remember it going stale, then "to nothing," and her misery in it after the Rusty Nail fight. So when she washed it — "it didn't smell like anything at all" — and then asked him to wear it all weekend so it comes back full of him, I got the same foolish hot prick of tears she did. And his answer: pulling it on right then, over the shirt he's already wearing, standing in his own kitchen in two shirts because she told him to. "Not one part of it embarrassed him on her behalf" — that's the exact grace this man deals in, and I believed every second of it. The flicker of hurt when he thought she was returning it was the most human thing I've seen cross his face; he thought, for one beat, he was losing something, and "he decided not to ask." Even his vulnerability is composed.

Which is my one honest unease, and the page half-earned it: she wanted him to fight for the nights. "Some unreasonable corner of her had wanted him to fight," and he never did — "he only opened the door and waited on the far side of it." She reads it as grace that "cost her more than a fight would have." I read it double. It is grace. It is also exactly what a man playing a long, patient game would do — never grip, always wait — and I can no longer tell where his tenderness ends and his method begins, because in him they may be the same organ. "The care was only the shape his attention took when it came down on a thing" — on her, on a ball of dough, and she "could not have said which of them got the larger share, and found she didn't mind." She doesn't mind. I minded, a little, on her behalf, and I'm not sure the book wants me to. I'm noting it and moving on.

The pizza is the food-motif doing its best work yet: she tastes every flavor separately, "the difference between cotton candy and a bowl of cut strawberries" — that's the whole arc of her awakening in one image, the girl who used to eat "with the sound off" now walking down a flavor list one at a time. And he watches her face "as if it had just told him something he'd wanted to know." He's always reading her. It's romantic and it's surveillance and it's both.

Randi doesn't appear and I felt her absence like a held breath — after MIRA, after "just us" said too soft, a chapter of pure Pace-and-Vee domesticity reads like the calm being banked before something spends it. That's a guess from here, not the page.

The title: "The New Ordinary" is exactly right and quietly devastating — this *is* their ordinary now, the drawer, the robe, the bench in the bedroom as furniture, the extraordinary gone routine. But "ordinary" over a house with that table in it is doing double work. The cover board — *WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION* — keeps whispering that every warm thing here was an invitation with a design behind it; my packet shows no tagline beyond that line, though I remember the jacket's "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem," and this chapter is fifty pages of freely given yes.

What I want next: I want Sunday, the shirt handed back full of him. What I dread: Thanksgiving with Sheri, who knows about both women, at a table with Vee, who knows about one.
