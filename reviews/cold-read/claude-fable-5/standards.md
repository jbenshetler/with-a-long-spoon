## Reader reaction

The morning after. Of course there had to be a morning after — "The Bench" ended with a key turning in a lock inside her ribs, and this chapter is what the locked room looks like from the corridor. And it wrecked me a little, quietly, without one raised voice or one bared inch of skin.

First: the common room. This is a masterful little gauntlet. Brooke never stands up — "the not-standing was the move" — and Randi, who sits on Standards herself, reads every layer of it from the inside, which is what makes it hurt: she knows exactly how she's being handled and cannot do anything but be handled. The interrogation is all warmth. "How's the paper coming?" lands like a trap because we watched her tell Pace the paper isn't due till Thursday; now she has to improvise a study group and a café on Wilson while three girls watch without watching. And Brooke's "Engineering, isn't it" — the guess that lands him "a notch down without seeming to reach for it" — followed by the recalibration at "Math. PhD." That's a whole social economy rendered in two lines. The "you're not in one of his sections—" beat genuinely tightened my chest; the room held it and so did I. And then "the release was the cleanest hit" — Brooke ends the conversation because she can, and Randi's three assembled defensive lines die in her mouth. Nobody was cruel. That's the horror of it.

What moved for me on Randi: everything, and further inward. Last chapter she decided, at the mirror, that "the one he had found was going back behind [the face]." This chapter shows me what that costs on an ordinary Saturday. The smile that "arranged... a fraction late." Her laugh matching Kinsey's pitch "very fast. She had not had time to choose the pitch" — that line is devastating, because it tells me the face isn't even fully hers; it's an apparatus that runs ahead of her. And "He's cute. Yeah." — Kinsey's "short-cute" flattening of the man who took her apart last night, and Randi assenting to it, because the truth has no shape that could survive this room.

Then the bedroom, and this is where the chapter goes from sharp to genuinely sad. The pacing of her not-sitting — vanity, bed, vanity; the closet opened for a purpose she "could not, for a moment, locate"; the tap run for ten counts with her hands never under it. These are the movements of a person whose interior has gone offline. The duvet against the memory of his cashmere blanket: "The duvet was the duvet. It was light and synthetic and adequate. It was not — it was a duvet." That broken-off sentence is the whole chapter. She can't finish the comparison because finishing it would mean admitting what the blanket was.

And the line that stopped me cold: "*He's hard to say no to,*" heard again "in a different key. It was not a key she could sing in." The page holds this open — is the different key desire, danger, grief, all of it — and I'm not going to close it either. She stops the thought "where the words stopped," and the final image of the not-crying that "did not feel like holding a door shut. It felt like there being no door" — after a book that has been all about doors, locks, deadbolts (she locks his front door first thing; the bathroom lock last night; the key in her ribs) — that's the bleakest sentence yet, and it's earned.

Trust and suspicion: I don't distrust anyone new. Brooke is doing exactly what a chapter president does; the menace is structural, not personal — though I'll be watching her; she now has Pace's name "filed." My larger unease is the jacket's shadow: I know Randi eventually runs a game on a girl named Vee with this man. Watching Randi decide, two chapters running, that being truly seen is unbearable — I can start to feel how a woman who can't be inside her own wanting might arrange to watch someone else be inside it. That's a guess from here, and the page hasn't said it; but the shape is beginning to show.

Erotic charge: low, deliberately — this is the hangover chapter, and the heat is all residual: last night's clothes, the wrinkled cardigan, the silk showing at the second step, the body that was on the bench now lying rigid under an adequate duvet. The charge is in the absence, and the absence works.

Friction: almost none. If anything, the chapter is so controlled that I noticed the control once — "The whole exchange had taken less than four minutes. Most of it had been smiling" is a beautiful line but sits right at the edge of the author summing the scene for me. I forgive it; it reads as Randi's own tallying.

The title: "Standards" is doing three jobs and I love all of them. She literally sits on the Standards board — the perquisite that got her the single room she's now lying alone in. The common-room scene is a Standards hearing conducted on her. And underneath: her standards, the ones "he's hard to say no to" quietly violated or exceeded — the chapter never says which, and neither will I. As for *A Polite Invitation* — this whole chapter is polite invitations weaponized: Brooke's "Wait—", the "can I ask" face, "Sorry to grill you. Go." The series title *With a Long Spoon* keeps whispering sup-with-the-devil at me, and I'm starting to wonder whether Randi thinks she's the one holding the spoon. The tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — sits differently now: Randi's yeses to Pace were freely given, and she's lying under a duvet unable to cry about what they opened.

What I want next: I want to know what she does — the checkpoint-less dread in me says she'll do something to Pace, or with him, that converts this unbearable being-seen into a game she can run rather than suffer. Guess from here: Vee enters soon, and this chapter is the why. I turned the last page wanting the next one immediately.
