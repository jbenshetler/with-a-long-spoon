## Reader reaction

The morning after, and the book does the thing I hoped it would do: it follows her home. After "The Bench" ended with that quiet deadbolt of a decision — *he could have the face... she was not coming out again* — I braced for a chapter of Pace, and instead I got the machine she lives inside, and now I understand the face in a way the mirror scene alone couldn't give me. This chapter is cold on purpose and it worked on me completely.

Brooke frightened me, and the text earned every degree of it. Not one thing she says is unfriendly, which is exactly the point — "She had a voice the way other people had a perfume." The interrogation is conducted entirely in warmth: the paper checked, the whereabouts checked, the name filed ("*Pace,*" like she was filing it), the guess that lands him "a notch down without seeming to reach for it" — Engineering, corrected to math PhD, absorbed "without conceding the notch." And the ending — "Sorry to grill you. Go." — named by the narration as "the cleanest hit," which I might have flagged as the author's thumb except that Randi *sits on Standards herself* and would score the exchange exactly this way; the free indirect voice covers it. What chilled me most is that Randi loses without a single visible loss. Four minutes. "Most of it had been smiling."

One place my ear pricked and I'm holding it open: the section question. Every pause in that scene is "a fraction of a second"; that one is "wider here," and "the room held it" — and then Randi over-answers: "He's not anywhere near mine." The page doesn't say she lied. I don't get to say it either. But I noticed the width of that pause, and I noticed that I noticed, and if it's nothing, it's a strange nothing.

And Randi — my God, the room scene. The walking to the vanity and not sitting, to the bed and not sitting, the hand lifted toward the closet for a purpose she can't locate. The duvet against the memory of his cashmere blanket: "It was not — it was a duvet." That aborted sentence is the whole chapter. And then the line that hurt worst, "*He's hard to say no to*" heard back "in a different key... not a key she could sing in" — the joke she deployed for the girls arriving in her own room as something she cannot afford to think past. This is the interiority I read for: the not-crying that "did not feel like holding a door shut. It felt like there being no door." After last night's locked bathroom door, after the hum "the size of a key turning in a lock," the doors and locks are accumulating into something, and this chapter's contribution — *no door* — is the bleakest yet.

My feeling about Pace didn't move, because he isn't here, and his absence is doing work: I found myself missing his warmth on her behalf, in the cold corner room under the adequate duvet, while knowing she has already decided he'll never get past the face again. That double ache — wanting her back in his house, knowing she was more surveilled there than anywhere and *less* — no, differently — I can't resolve it, and I don't think the book wants me to.

Heat, honestly: almost none, and rightly. What charge there is lives in the residue — last night's clothes worn up the stairs, the silk showing at the second step, un-adjusted. That un-adjusting is the one flicker of the woman from the bench.

The title: "Standards" is doing at least three jobs and I felt all of them land. Her board seat — the enforcer summoned before a subtler enforcer. The class-sorting Brooke performs on Pace. And the mother's knife-edged standards, the appetite trimmed to almost nothing, the closet running "bone to camel to oatmeal." She is a woman made entirely of standards, and the chapter shows the price of meeting them: a made bed she didn't sleep in. The cover board — *WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION* — no tagline or jacket copy in what I was given. The proverb about supping with the devil needing a long spoon keeps circling: after this chapter I genuinely can't tell who the devil is supposed to be — Pace, Brooke, the house, or the face itself — and "a polite invitation" now sounds exactly like Brooke's "Wait—". Politeness as trap is this chapter's whole grammar.

What I want next: I want to know what she does with the notifications she turned facedown. I dread — guess from here — that Brooke isn't finished, that the filing of his name was the start of something, and that Randi's "not ever" is going to hold with Pace right up until it breaks somewhere ugly. I turned the page wanting the next chapter immediately.
