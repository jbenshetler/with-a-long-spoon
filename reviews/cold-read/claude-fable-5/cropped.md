# Cold read (grounded) — Cropped

## Reader reaction

This is the quiet chapter after the loud one, and it got me in a way the loud one didn't quite. Nothing happens — two girls at a cafeteria table on the night everyone comes back — and yet the whole book is sitting at that table with them.

The thing that landed first, before I could think about it, was the word. "I cropped it before I sent it. The version they have stops at the waist." My stomach did a small drop, because I've seen Vee crop a photo before — the nude, trimmed down for Randi before the sting of "you only gave him half?" pulled the whole of it out of her. Now the induction picture, cropped at the waist for her mother, so the leg she gave the room on purpose never reaches Ohio. She's become someone who cuts a different version of herself for every viewer: Dad gets hiking and the boom box on concrete; Mom gets a photo that stops where the slit begins; Cassie gets "the parts that fit"; Randi got the crop and then the whole. And the ache underneath, the one the chapter never says and doesn't have to, is that Vee herself is living inside the most ruthless crop of all — the version of her own romance with the pointing game and the debriefed lunches trimmed out of frame. She's learning the family craft. She just doesn't know it's being practiced on her.

How I feel about her: more tender than ever, and a little proud, honestly. "I'm not ashamed of it. The opposite, if anything" — from the girl whose mother touches her own collarbone as a form of speech, that's a real mile marked. The mother is rendered in one gesture ("she didn't say anything, just touched her own collarbone while she looked. You know how she does") and Cassie's flat "I know how she does" told me these two have twenty years of shorthand. That exchange warmed me more than anything else on the page. Cassie remains the person I trust without reservation — the way she asks "So what is he. Not to your dad. To you," careful and exact, and then doesn't push. She's the only character who asks Vee real questions and then leaves the door open instead of walking through it. Compare that to Randi's lunches, where every door gets walked through with pastries. The contrast isn't stated; I just felt it.

The sharpest observation in the chapter is Vee's read of her father: "my dad would take it better if I told him Pace takes that dress off me than that he made it. Sex he understands. The sewing he never would." And then "weird is worse than wrong. Wrong is something he understands and rejects. Weird is something that doesn't fit anywhere in how he sees the world." That's smart writing wearing Vee's voice, and it mostly fits her — she's the econ student who prices things, like her dad "pricing" the dress — though I'll admit the monologue runs a touch long and polished for cafeteria talk; for a few paragraphs Cassie disappears into a listening post while Vee essays. Small thumb on the scale, not a heavy one.

And then the ending, which is the chapter's real event: Cassie's question, and Vee has "nothing that fit in Cassie's frame, not the right word for what he was, not the right story for how it had gone, not the version of herself that stood up cleanly in the telling." Is it that you can't say, or that you don't want to? "Both. Probably both." That's the most honest sentence Vee has produced in forty-two chapters, and it unsettled me precisely because it's honest — she can feel the shape of something in her story that doesn't survive plain telling, even to the person who loves her most simply. She doesn't know what I know. But her instinct is brushing against it. "She was grateful. She was also, a little, not" — that closing beat, wanting to be pushed and being spared, is exactly the flavor of this book's loneliness.

No suspicion earned of anyone new. Randi is completely absent from this chapter — not present, not even named — and I noticed the absence the way you notice a missing tooth. Erotic charge is nearly nil and that's right for the chapter; the one flicker is "Pace takes that dress off me," said to her best friend at dinner, casual as a fact, which measures how far she's come from the girl who couldn't wear a bikini.

The title did its work after the fact and recolored the chapter on second read of my own memory — cropping as this family's love language, inspection and omission. The volume title, *A Polite Invitation*, and the series title, *With a Long Spoon*, keep sitting exactly where they've sat: everything about Vee's happiness is invitation-shaped, and she keeps RSVPing yes to things she was never shown the whole of. No cover tagline in what I was given beyond the cover-board line.

What I want next: I want Cassie to ask one more question, someday, and I dread the day she does, because Cassie's radar has never once been wrong. Guess from here: the "both" Vee couldn't say is going to matter — she's started noticing that her story doesn't tell cleanly, and that's the first thread of the whole garment.
