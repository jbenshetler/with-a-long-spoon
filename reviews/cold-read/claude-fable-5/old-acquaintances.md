# Cold read — Old Acquaintances

*scene: scenes/old-acquaintances.md · model: claude-fable-5 · read after: fairytale*

## Reader reaction

**The felt read**

Oh, this hurt — in the good way. After the silver chill of Gstaad, the book turns the New Year's Eve coin over and shows the other face: the same night, the same texts, from the twin bed in Ohio. And the trick of it — the trick that made my stomach drop — is that I already know both texts. I read Vee's "thinking about you 💛" last chapter as an act of grace arriving into Randi's emptiness; now I read the composing of it, "read it twice for anything that gave her away," and it turns out that little open hand was drafted in agony, at 11-something on the worst night of her year, with the phone flipped face-down and back over. And Randi's reply — which last chapter I saw priced exactly ("Every word of it true. None of it the thing") — lands on Vee's ceiling and Vee *believes every word of it*. "She was fine. Better than fine... a devastatingly handsome billionaire." The dramatic irony is almost unbearable, and the book never nudges me once. Vee swallows the fairytale whole because she has nothing else to eat.

The room that "did not know her" is the best piece of writing in the chapter. "The room kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed" — the trophies, the girls who hadn't texted in a year, the clarinet in the closet — and then the closing line, "the cold room kept watch over the girl who was gone," which is genuinely lovely and earned. This is what the book has been doing to Vee since the flannel: she can't go back, and forward is a shut door.

The cruelest, truest sequence is the fantasy that turns. She reaches for her one safe memory — the fra diavolo night, the slipping sheet — "to be warm," and her own jealousy performs a substitution on it: "the hair went gold and the leg went tan and the blonde sat where Vee had sat." *She could not keep her own night.* That's devastating, and it's also the book's method made visible in miniature — Vee, of all people, now running an imagined other woman into Pace's kitchen, building her "from spite: blonde, tan, a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's." That self-description stung. And the Sheri seed from Thanksgiving finally pays: "He had cooked for Sheri... about Christmas he'd said nothing at all." I know (or believe) Pace cooked for no one — but Vee doesn't, and her handholds — the bartender, Randi's own "he leaves you the room" — "went straight through her hands. The floor was gone."

One more knife: the cropped photo. She scissored her own leg off the induction picture before sending it to her mother — "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it." That's *Cropped* come home; the division isn't imposed anymore, she performs it on herself. And "He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now" retro-poisons one of the book's tenderest lines. That's this author's signature move and it keeps working on me.

No erotic charge here beyond the remembered sheet-slipping warmth, deliberately confiscated. Friction: almost none. Maybe the imagined-blonde device runs one beat long, but I bought every step. Trust/suspicion: nothing new to suspect — only the ongoing, awful asymmetry: Vee still believes Randi's fairytale and still doesn't know Randi saw the thing she "never once let on she'd seen"... wait — that line: Vee *knows she never said the thing*, and knows she folded it away "in the glass room." Which "thing"? I read it as Vee's envy/knowledge of the money — she saw Randi blanch at the Miranda Holdings name and never let on. Kept quiet as a kindness. Both these women are curating for each other.

The title — "Old Acquaintances" — is *Auld Lang Syne*, obviously, New Year's; but on the page it's the ribbons, the girls on the corkboard who stopped texting, the room that loves a girl who's gone. Should old acquaintance be forgot: the room hasn't forgotten Vivienne; Vivienne has outgrown the room. Also, quietly, Pace as a possible old acquaintance now — "Maybe it was over." Perfect title, gave nothing away, deepened after.

The book title keeps tightening. Sup with the devil with a long spoon — and here's Vee with no spoon at all, drinking the fairytale straight from the ladle.

What I want next: term to start. Randi has "a day she could circle"; Pace is "a blank." *Guess from here:* Randi returns first, becomes the confidante about the Pace rupture — the person Vee cries to about Pace being the person who built the whole thing — and that's where the trap's teeth are. And Pace, acts-not-words, will do something rather than say something. I dread the anthro class brunch where Randi hears about the breakup and has to decide what to do with it.

**Symbolism:** The room as the discarded self — "kept watch over the girl who was gone." The self-cropped photo = Vee now dividing her own image between worlds, the surveillance internalized. The fairytale believed = the trap running on Vee's own hunger.

**Characterization:** Vee deepens beautifully — jealous, spiteful, self-aware about the spite, generous anyway (the demand-nothing text). Parents get real texture in a paragraph (Wheel of Fortune, the letters). No one flattens.

**Pace — within chapter:** Slow by design, a single insomniac hour; earns it. The blonde-fantasy stretch is the only slightly long beat.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** Excellent — a diptych with ch.45, same night from the other side; the irony between the two texts generates more momentum than plot would. Holding pattern plot-wise, but charged.

**How I feel**
Vee: heart fully broken for her — the diptych structure (both texts now seen from both sides) makes her generosity and her ignorance excruciating; her spite and jealousy only make her realer. Randi: the fairytale being *believed* makes her ch.45 choice feel more consequential — real love, engineered delivery; wariness and tenderness both up. Mood: cold lavender dark; dread and tenderness in equal measure; the term restarting feels like a trap re-arming.
