# Cold read (grounded) — Old Acquaintances

## Reader reaction

What this chapter does — and it does it quietly, without one scene of anything happening — is hand me the other half of a phone screen I already held. I watched Randi send *"it's honestly a fairytale out here"* from a hotel room where she'd just failed to feel anything next to a faultless man, and now I get to watch Vee receive it in a twin bed under her old synchro trophies and believe every word. "Even a poisoned Gstaad was more life than she had." That's the cruelest sentence in the chapter and Vee wrote it about herself. I know the poison went deeper than the PPP money she thinks she's referring to. She envies a woman who spent midnight putting her face back on in a stranger's bathroom, missing *her*. The dramatic irony isn't clever here — it hurts, both directions at once, because each woman is lying in the dark wanting the other one and neither text says so.

The room got me before any of that. "The room did not know her. It had known Vivienne — knew her still, in high resolution, and loved her." The mother's name for her, the ribbons, the little gold girls with their toes pointed, the legs that outgrew the bed in middle school and never got a bigger one — the whole first third is the physical fact of a girl who left and came back changed and has to sleep inside her own outgrown skin for two weeks. The casserole that "had never once been the thing Vee was hungry for, not then and not now; she just hadn't had the word for it then" — that's the food motif doing its best work of the book, turned backward onto childhood.

And then the spiral, which is where my stomach actually dropped. She reaches for her best memory — the fra diavolo night, the sheet slipping, "the evening having used her well" — to warm herself, and it turns *while she's inside it*: the hair goes gold, the leg goes tan, the blonde sits down in her chair in the wine light. "She could not keep her own night." That's the worst thing loneliness has done to anyone in this book so far, worse than the fight itself — jealousy retroactively colonizing a memory. And the detail that she builds the rival as her own opposite, "a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's" — her mother's floozie voice is right there in the word choice, doing its old work the moment Pace's warmth isn't present to burn it off. I know, from everything the book has shown me, that Pace is almost certainly alone at that stove or feeding Sheri out of the same pity as Thanksgiving. But I also can't fully reassure her from where I sit, and that's new. He *didn't* call. He *didn't* come after her. "He left you the room. He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now" — that line indicts him fairly. His one method, the open door and the waiting, is indistinguishable from indifference across three hundred miles and a silence he won't break, and for the first time I felt genuinely angry at him rather than just wary of the architecture. Say the word, Pace. Or at least say *anything*.

The chapter's saddest ledger entry is the last one: Randi comes with a day she can circle — a class, a face that will call her gorgeous — and Pace comes with "no hour she could name, no room he'd surely be in." Certainty has quietly changed sides. If I'm tracking whose gravity is winning, this chapter moved the needle toward Randi without either woman touching the other, and Vee still can't see that "the only girl she could talk to about real things" is the thing itself.

Friction, small: the blonde-fantasy machinery is rendered at length and once or twice the narration explains what it's doing — "what you believe at midnight. Cold. Alone." — a beat after the images had already done it. And the phone-vigil is the third consecutive chapter of Vee alone with an unsendable message ("Bare," now this); it's deliberate accumulation, I can feel that, but I was aware of the repetition.

The title: "Old Acquaintances" is Auld Lang Syne, of course — should old acquaintance be forgot — and it cuts several ways: the girls on the corkboard who haven't texted in a year, Vivienne herself as the old acquaintance the room keeps dusted, and the fearful question of whether Pace has just become one. It's the right title and it didn't give anything away. The volume title, *A Polite Invitation*, and the series title, *With a Long Spoon*, sit colder than ever against a chapter where the invited girl is home alone wondering if the invitation has lapsed — the packet showed me no tagline beyond the cover board line, and I don't need one; "sup with a long spoon" keeps confirming itself.

What I want next: the term to start. I want the reunion with Randi — I'm actively hungry for it, which tells me the book has me exactly where it wants me — and I dread-want the Pace reckoning. Guess from here: he hasn't moved because he genuinely doesn't know a repair is required, and someone (Randi, probably, who coached the last apology) will end up brokering it — which means Vee's reconciliation with him will once again be secretly stage-managed by his other lover. I hate how plausible that is.
