## Reader reaction

So this is where it starts. I've been holding the jacket's promise — "it began as a game between the two people now closest to her" — and I did not expect to be handed the origin this soon, or to be made complicit in it this pleasurably. The title told me nothing going in; three pages in it names itself, and by the end "The Pointing Game" has become one of those titles I'll never read innocently again.

The felt read, honestly: this chapter worked on me twice, in two different registers, and I want to be precise about both.

First, the bed. That opening is some of the hottest writing in the book so far — him sliding into her "while she was still swollen and still pulsing around nothing," the question "Would you like to taste another woman?" landing in her body before it reaches her mind, and her coming on the question itself, "far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it." That is exactly the kind of erotica I read for: the desire is the event, not the mechanics. And Randi's recovery — "Only in a threesome. With you." — is the best character beat she's had since the mirror in The Bench. Pace's reading of it is devastating and, I think, correct: "The same act. A different owner." She cannot own the wanting of a woman, so she gifts it to him. After the bathroom-mirror chapter, where she decided the one he found "was going back behind it," this is the same machinery in daylight: the face takes ownership of what the body already confessed. I believe her flinch completely; I believe the costume completely. And the page leaves open what I'll leave open — whether her "yes" at the end is desire for Vee, relief at a survivable container for desire, or both braided. It reads like both.

Second, the quad. The pointing game itself is somehow hotter than the sex — the taxonomy of refusals ("Too expected." "You'd get both or neither." "She'd be a project.") is Randi thinking out loud about her own taste for the first time in her life, and Pace watching her learn it is genuinely tender: "He was not choosing a woman. He was watching her learn that she could." I melted at that line, and I want to be honest that I melted, because—

Then Vee comes out of the dining hall, and I felt two things simultaneously and neither canceled the other. One: the description is rapturous — the copper hair the wind keeps lifting, the head thrown all the way back, "laughing like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen" — and Randi's bare "Yes," the word arriving "with nothing around it," is the most undefended thing she's done outside the bench. Her happy footwork on the brick, the squeaked "*Yes*" — I was grinning with her. Two: I know from the jacket who Vee is. I know the year being planned is hers. So the engineered collision — three seconds of execution so clean "the redhead had walked into *her*" — landed on me as beautiful and cold at once, a con executed on a girl who apologized for being run into. The book told me on its cover it would let me "fall for it exactly as she does," and here I am, ahead of Vee from her literal first step onto the page, charmed by her predators. That's not unease the text failed to earn; that's the unease the text is *made of*, and I felt it as a low hum under the delight rather than a betrayal of it.

What moved for me character-wise: Pace complicates in a way I want to keep watching. His scrupulousness is real — waiting for the heat to leave the room before asking the daylight version of the question, wanting her "gathered back into herself... free to weigh it and choose; that mattered more to him than the answer did." That's the consent ethic I trust him for. But this chapter also shows him doing the thing he does: engineering conditions under which another person's want surfaces on his schedule. "Go meet her" — and her look back, "is this one mine to take," and his nod. He gives; he also directs the giving. And "Let's go feed you" as the closing line, after she refused the cheeseburger with "I can't eat that" — after the apple slices in The Bench — the feeding motif is turning into his whole theology, and I notice I can no longer tell whether it's love or husbandry. The page doesn't tell me either. Good.

Randi I feel *for* more than ever, which surprised me after Standards left her frozen under a duvet in her shoes. Here she's lit up, playing, surprising him and herself — and every glow is shadowed by what I know she decided in that bathroom. The grin "breaking the managed surface" is real joy. It's also the first time I've seen her joy, and it took a woman across a lawn to produce it.

Friction: very little. One line tipped toward telling me what to conclude — "not a woman who hungered for another woman, which was unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy" — it's Pace's analysis and it's sharp, but it names the mechanism so completely that it slightly flattened a thing I'd already felt. Minor. And "he wanted the game to give that wanting air in daylight without making her pin it beneath a solemn name" runs close to self-congratulation, though I accept it as his sincere self-account, which is exactly what makes him worth watching warily.

The book's title recolored hard this chapter: *A Polite Invitation* — the collision *was* the invitation, delivered so politely the invitee apologized for receiving it. "Every open door is a temptation and every step is hers" — Vee's steps haven't started yet, and I already know none of the doors are accidents. *With a Long Spoon* — sup with the devil; I'm starting to wonder who at this table needs the spoon.

What I want next: Vee's interior, badly. I've only seen her through the hunters' eyes — that laugh, that ease — and the jacket says she's "quietly starving," which nothing visible today showed. Guess from here: the "stats class together" becomes Randi's lunches, and I'll watch Randi fall for the girl she claimed only as Pace's gift, since the jacket says the game gets "as true for the two who started it." Dread, mild and earned: that Randi's bathroom vow — never coming out again — means Vee gets the face too, and won't know it.
