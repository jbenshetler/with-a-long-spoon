The chapter opens with her slowing at the table with its top back on — the thing she wouldn't look at Saturday morning — and saying *Let's do something different. Today.* And I sat up, because I know what he doesn't: she walked out of that bathroom having decided, "quiet as a deadbolt going over," that he could have the face and the one he found was never coming out again. So I read this whole chapter double. Pace reads her body and trusts it — "He took the body's answer for the true one. He usually did" — and the body keeps telling the truth: she comes *hard*, around nothing his body is doing, from the bare question "Would you like to taste another woman?" That is one of the hottest single beats in the book so far, hotter than a lot of full scenes I've read elsewhere: a desire so new it detonates before she can make it "mean anything safer." But her *mouth* is running the bathroom-mirror program. "Only in a threesome. With you." And Pace narrates her maneuver with open admiration — "The same act. A different owner." — and calls it beautiful, and says okay, easy. He thinks he's giving the wanting air. I think she just handed him the deadbolt with a bow on it, and he filed it under gifts.

Or — and I have to hold this genuinely open, because the page does — the game works. Because the second half of this chapter is *delightful*, and her delight is not the face. The bare "Yes" when Vee comes out of the dining hall, arriving "with nothing around it"; the half-second of happy footwork on the brick she "didn't decide to do"; the grin she's *failing* to hide. Those are the same involuntary register as the bench-orgasm — things her apparatus didn't produce. The engineered collision is a set piece I'll remember: a decade of dance folded into three seconds so clean the redhead apologizes for being walked into. I was rooting for her across that whole lawn, and I felt Pace's warmth at the light pole as my own. That's the swoon, and I'm naming it: the pointing game swept me up. Her learning, mid-walk, that "the choosing was hers" — "I point. You pick" — that landed as generosity, his particular kind, the same kind as the thin apple slices.

And then the last line pulled the floor a quarter-inch sideways. "Do you think she'll like the bench?" Low, without lifting her head. It's a hot line and I felt it as hot — and also, one beat later, I heard what it does: it takes the thing that cracked *her* open and points it at someone else. Vee, ninety seconds after learning her name, is already being fitted for the position Randi begged in. Maybe that's just appetite talking, the wanting given air exactly as Pace hoped. Maybe it's the face finding a way to be *adjacent* to being seen without ever being seen again — send the other woman down onto the leather, keep the deciding. The page doesn't say, and I'm not saying either. But the hum in my ribs matches hers.

Where my feelings moved: Pace, for the first time, I'm watching with a small honest wariness the text itself earned. Not because he's unkind — he's scrupulous, he waits for her to be "gathered back into herself" before asking for real, he wants her free to choose, and that moved me. But *Standards* showed me his misread in real time — "a remarkably good evening," while she was rebuilding the wall — and this chapter's narration is wall-to-wall confident interpretation of her. "He usually did" is doing a lot of quiet work. A man who is usually right is a man the book has already shown being wrong once. Randi I feel more tenderly than ever, and more nervously: her "No. I can't eat that," easy, automatic, at the cheeseburger — the portioning again, the apple's dark twin — hurt in one line. And Vee: the page made me half in love in a paragraph. Laughing with her throat bare to the sky, "like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen." Given what being seen did to Randi, that description is either the most beautiful thing on the quad or a target painted in sunlight. I ached for her a little already, and she hasn't said a word I could hear.

Friction: barely any. Once or twice Pace's narration explains Randi's move so completely — "not a woman who hungered for another woman, which was unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy" — that I felt the tidiness of it, though since it's *his* tidiness, it may be the point of him.
