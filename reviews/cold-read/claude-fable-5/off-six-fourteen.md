# Cold read (grounded) — Off Six-Fourteen

*scene: scenes/off-six-fourteen.md · model: claude-fable-5 · memory: raw ch001..ch008 (pre-first-checkpoint) · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

This chapter is small and it wrecked me a little, in the quiet way — not with heat, there's no heat in it, but with how completely I believed Vee's happiness and how much I know that she doesn't.

The felt read first. Vee comes home talking before her coat is off, and Cassie gets pulled off the desk chair "the way good news pulls you to your feet," and I was right there on the lower bunk with her, sock feet up, grinning. The retelling of the kiss is lovely precisely because it's a retelling — we already saw it happen at the end of "The Long Way," and hearing Vee find the smaller, quieter voice for it ("the words coming smaller and one at a time, so that Cassie leaned the last inch in to have them") made it land a second time, differently. And the spin — "a clumsy whole-body turn that nearly took out the closet door and was over before it was really a dance at all — the room too small to hold what she had to do with it" — that's pure swoon. I love this girl. She said the starchy thing, and then she chose past it, and she knows she chose, and she's proud of it, and she should be.

Except. The line that stopped my breath: "Nobody did anything to me. I wasn't talked into it. I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose." That's true. Every word of it is true at the level of the tree and the kiss. And the jacket copy has been sitting in my head since page one — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — and here is Vee saying it herself, glowing, as her proof of safety. I know what she doesn't: that Randi picked her off a lawn in a game Pace pointed her into. Her yes was freely given AND the path she gave it on was laid for her. The book keeps letting both be true at once and it is doing something to me, this doubleness — I can't stop rooting for her and I can't stop wincing, and neither feeling cancels the other. That's not unease I'm manufacturing; the chapter built it deliberately, and it's the best thing in it.

Then the title did its work. "Off Six-Fourteen" — I read the chapter title before the chapter and it meant nothing. Then Vee says it, counting it off "like a dossier, like plenty": "He lives out west of town, off six-fourteen, past where it goes to gravel." And I went cold, gently, because I have BEEN off six-fourteen. I've been in that house for a whole long night. I know the too-warm rooms Pace keeps warm so he can take his time. I know the kitchen table he built, the bedroom around the corner, and I know exactly what sits in the middle of that bedroom looking like a low table of odd proportions — "full of what it was, doing what it would continue to do, waiting for the next time." Vee thinks she's naming a road. She's naming the bench's address. Cassie's dry "That's the whole address. Off six-fourteen." is a joke about vagueness; for me it's the opposite of vague. It's the most specific place in the book. That's a title that recolored the whole chapter on contact, and it's the reader-ahead-of-her machine the jacket promised, running at full efficiency.

Cassie. My feeling about Cassie keeps deepening and I want to be careful with it. The state-troopers bit is funny and it's also real vigilance in a costume — she is the only person in Vee's orbit who asks where, and who, and is he rich, and did he get you drunk. "How much did he give you to drink?" is a best friend doing due diligence disguised as a bit, and Vee's answer is the answer Cassie needed to hear before she'd let herself grin. And I can't forget "Goodbye, Vee" from the lecture hall — the whole word, "the word for a longer parting than a Wednesday" — or "They will never know the love of a woman" at the pool, or the fact that Cassie's flat patient look is reserved for Randi specifically. The page hasn't told me what Cassie sees or what Cassie feels, and I'm not going to fix it. But she reads as the one person in this book whose radar isn't for sale, and her radar keeps clicking near Randi and staying quiet near Pace — "That's not a boy. That's a man" — and I notice that Pace has so far earned her provisional pass and Randi hasn't. Whether Cassie's right about either is exactly what I'm reading to find out.

Randi is entirely absent from this chapter — not present, not even mentioned by name — and her absence is loud to me in a way it can't be to Vee. Vee's best hour, her best evening, her first self-chosen kiss: all of it is downstream of the collision on the lawn, and the person who arranged the collision doesn't appear in the victory lap. I felt that as a reader, not on the page — the page is all warmth — but the checkpoint of my memory won't let the warmth sit unaccompanied.

Erotic charge: essentially none, and correctly none. This is afterglow rendered as friendship, and it works as that. The closest thing to heat is the memory of the kiss carried in Vee's lowered voice.

Friction: almost nothing. If I reach — "because the gladness in Cassie always had a flat dry floor under it" is the narrator explaining Cassie a half-beat after the dialogue had already shown her, a small doubled stitch. That's the whole complaint. The chapter is short and knows it; it does one thing, the thing lands, it gets out.

The book title and series: "A Polite Invitation" keeps sharpening. The hiking invitation is the politest one yet — daylight, a trail, "real shoes," water, nothing to say no to — and it's the invitation that takes her out past where the road goes to gravel, to the house. "With a Long Spoon" I still hold loosely — the proverb about supping with the devil hovers for me, and if that's the register, then Vee borrowing "good ones" is her outfitting herself for the walk toward whatever the long spoon is for. The tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — this chapter is practically a dramatization of it, spoken in Vee's own voice as triumph.

What I want next: the hike, obviously, and I dread it a little in the sweetest way — dread isn't even right, it's suspense with my stomach in it. Guesses from here: she sees the house; maybe she sees the furniture and, unlike Randi, actually hears "I built it." I also half-expect the book to keep Randi and the house apart in Vee's knowledge for as long as it can — Randi knows that gravel road too, and neither woman knows the other has stood in that bedroom. The moment those two facts touch is somewhere ahead of me and I can feel its gravity from here. And the closing image — the window letting "its cold thin line in over the both of them, and Vee lay back in the bean bag warm and did not feel it" — is as close as this warm chapter comes to saying what I'm feeling: the cold is in the room; she just can't feel it yet.
