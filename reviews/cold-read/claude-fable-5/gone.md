# Cold read (grounded) — Gone

## Reader reaction

Back behind the curtain. After three chapters riding Vee's gold rush — the dress, the shoes, the sidewalk laughter — the book drops me into Pace's warm bedroom with Randi astride him, and the whiplash is the point: everything Vee just lived, I now hear retold as pillow talk between the two people running her life. I read the whole thing with my stomach slightly tilted, half melted and half appalled, which by now I recognize as this book's home key.

First, the thing that got me most: "I didn't think I'd like her this much... She got past me before I could help it." That's the closest Randi has come to saying a true thing out loud since the bathroom mirror in chapter one, and she says it into his chest, in the one place where she "sets it down at his door." I believed it completely. The jacket warned me it would get real for the two who started it, and here it is getting real in Randi's mouth, in real time. And then — because she's Randi — she immediately converts it into performance again: the shoe-store story.

And the shoe-store story is *wrong*. I was there two chapters ago. Vee found the canoe shoe and set it down in front of Randi with a deadpan line; it was Randi who put on the rhinestone platforms and did the walk; the older couple got from Vee "a little flush, there and gone." In Randi's telling, Vee got the canoe on, did "the full bigfoot," got caught mid-stomp, and "went so red I thought she'd go up. Hand over her mouth." Then: "It was so cute. She was so embarrassed." Softer. "That was kind of hot." I don't think this is a continuity slip — this book has been obsessed with retellings from the start. Vee ladles the middle out of her versions; now I see Randi ladles things *in*. She's rebuilt the afternoon so that Vee is the clown and the blusher, transferred her own antics onto her, and then eroticized the embarrassment she invented. That "kind of hot" is the most honest and the most unnerving line in the chapter at once: Randi's want for Vee, no longer dressed up as "a threesome, with you" — just a girl going red, and Randi finding it hot, and saying so where only Pace can hear.

Then the temperature drops without either of them raising a voice. "She's not ready, though. She'll get there. You'll want to take your time." Handler's talk. Project-management. And Pace's answer — "I want her to get there on her own" — is the sentence I've been circling all book: it is exactly his whole courtly ethic, doors left open, the pencil handed back, and it is also exactly what a very patient trap sounds like. The page doesn't let me pick. Randi's "God, you two" — with something crossing her face and going — doesn't pick either, and I felt that flicker like a splinter. What crossed? Impatience? Jealousy? Recognition that his patience is a kind of love she didn't sign up to witness? Held open, and I'll hold it open.

His diagnosis of Vee — "She's ashamed. Somebody taught her to be ashamed of her body," said "low, certain, without heat" — landed hard because it's *true*, I've lived twenty chapters inside that shame, and hearing it stated flatly in a bed Vee doesn't know exists made me feel exactly how far ahead of her I am, and how little I enjoy it. Randi's gloss — "And you're showing her she doesn't have to be... That's why she's so gone over you" — is also true, which is the trap the jacket promised: nothing anyone says here is a lie about Vee. They see her more accurately than her own mother ever did. That's what's unbearable.

The chapter's knife-turn is the lamp. Two chapters ago Randi's attention was the lamp — "the full warmth of her attention on, like a lamp finding you." Now Pace, eyes on nothing, seeing it: "She comes in and the whole thing goes brighter. Like somebody found the lamp... she's the most alive person standing there, and it never once occurs to her." And Randi goes still — "Not the loose stillness of after. A different one, the kind that takes holding." That's the third time I've watched this stillness (the unsipped cup, the tightness at "he looked at me and I was fine"), and it's the worst one, because he's just handed the lamp — her lamp, her whole social technology — to the girl she picked, and named Vee *alive*, which is the one thing Randi decided in a locked bathroom she would never be again in front of him. "Yeah," she said, when she'd been quiet a beat too long. "She is." I ached for her. I keep aching for the most dangerous person in the book and I've stopped apologizing for it.

The erotic charge: real but strange, deliberately. Randi dragging her breasts down his chest to demonstrate Vee's body — "It's *criminal*, Pace. But you already know" — is genuinely hot and genuinely vertiginous: she's using her own body as a projection screen for the woman they're both courting. And the text is careful about what moves him: "not the picture of Vee she'd handed him, but Randi... wanting something out loud in his bed and not able to stop it." I believed that — his whole erotics is undefendedness, it's what he dug out of her on the bench — but then her "So soon?" arrives right after both of them have been rhapsodizing Vee, and the page lets that hang. Whose name is in the room while she rides him? The chapter refuses to say, and I felt the refusal as intended. The closing sex is brief, warm, well-done — "he let her set the pace of it" — with the blue toenails from chapter one still perfect, and Sade under the door where Steely Dan used to be.

One small line I keep turning over: "He didn't take up the rest of what she'd set down; that was hers to hold." Her want for Vee, spoken and left unclaimed by him. It reads like tact and like strategy at once — the same doubleness as everything Pace does.

The title: "Gone" is doing three jobs and I felt each land in sequence. Vee is "so gone over you." Randi is gone too — "she got past me before I could help it" — the word she'd never use pinned on her by the title. And something crossing Randi's face "was gone," the chapter's whole grammar of concealment. Nothing was given away in advance; it recolored as I read, which is how this book's titles keep behaving. *A Polite Invitation* and the tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — have never sat heavier than over "She'll get there. You'll want to take your time... I want her to get there on her own." They are literally engineering the conditions of a freely given yes. That's the long spoon, and I'm holding it.

What I want next: I want to know what "there" is and I dread finding out that it's exactly what I think — a guess from here: the threesome from the pointing game, approached so patiently that Vee will believe she invented it. I also can't stop thinking that Randi's growing realness on both fronts — for him, for her — is the unstable element. Pace thinks he's cultivating Vee; I'm starting to think the person ripening fastest is Randi, and she's the only one with a vow in the way. And somewhere across town Vee's dress hangs in a garment bag, shown to not one living soul who matters — except the two people who, it turns out, discuss her body, her shame, and her readiness in bed. The induction is coming. I both want it and brace for it.
