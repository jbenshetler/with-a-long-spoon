## Reader reaction

This one's short — a breath of cold air after two chapters of Vee's misery and repair — and it did something to me that no chapter has done yet: it gave me Pace through the eyes of someone who owes nobody in this story anything.

Sheri. Brand new, and vivid inside of a page: the small frame "not much bigger than a child's," "never as much of her as the work she got out of it," the bleached ponytail, faster on the climbs for two years and never letting it go unmentioned. She remarks on women "the way other people remarked on the weather" — "Hell of a backside on her, though. I'll give her that" — and I liked her immediately, the way you like someone who has no performance running. Which is exactly the thing: she's the first person in this entire book with no performance running. Randi is a face. Vee is editing every retelling. Brooke smiles and files. Even Cassie holds things behind her eyebrow. Sheri just says what she sees. In a book this thick with managed surfaces, her bluntness landed on me like fresh water.

And what she sees is the thing I've been unable to verify for twenty-seven chapters: "Listen to you." Pace runs on about Vee — the thumb down the walnut grain, the math book she didn't put back "like it had burned her" — "He heard himself going and didn't stop." That's not the plan talking. That's a man smitten past his own noticing, and it took an outside witness to make me trust it, because his tenderness on the page has always been shadowed by the pointing game. Sheri can't be fooled by the courtship because she isn't in it. This is the strongest evidence yet that whatever this started as, it's real in him now — and I felt actual relief reading it, which tells me how much I've been wanting permission to like him.

Then the chapter took the relief back with four words. "What happened to the pale sorority chick?" — "Randi. That's not finished." Flat. No guilt in it, no hedge, just a fact stated to a friend on a cold road. The whole Vee half of the conversation glowed, and then that line sat there like ice on the shoulder of it. And Sheri, running "the arithmetic anybody could run": "Two of them at once. If that's not already complicated, it will be." She's the first person in the book to say the obvious thing out loud, in daylight, without an agenda — and hearing it said plainly, from outside, made the situation feel more dangerous than any of the coded lunches have. Not a prophecy, just arithmetic. That's what chilled me: it doesn't take a scheme to see the crash coming. It takes one honest friend on a bicycle.

The other thing I noticed and want to hold carefully: "After you left Thursday, I kept an eye on her." So Sheri was at the Rusty Nail. Did he ask her to watch Vee, or did she volunteer? The page doesn't say, and I'm leaving it open — but either way it rhymes with the whole gets-girls-home-safe ethic Vee threw in his face. Even walking out on a fight, someone of his was watching that she was all right. "All she did was sit by herself, glaring at the floor, nursing the one drink. Danced with nobody. Left alone." Independent confirmation of exactly the night Vee told Randi about — and now Pace has it too. He knows she didn't shrug him off and dance. That's information he's carrying into whatever door Vee knocks on tomorrow night, and I love that the book handed it to him quietly like this.

Small pleasures: the naming beat — "That short for something, or just the letter?" / "Vivienne" — landing right after Rock taught me that Vivienne is the trouble-name and Vee is the love-name; he gives Sheri the full name and then floods past it into the love. And "She couldn't take your heat" — a deflection wearing a joke, and a curious one; whatever Sheri is to him (two years across a handlebar, and the page doesn't say more), he rates her.

Erotic charge: none, and correctly none. This is a palate chapter.

Friction: almost nothing. If I reach — "she'd said her piece and was already looking down the road" is the chapter telling me Sheri doesn't moralize a half-beat after showing me she doesn't. But it's a whisper.

The title: "hills-and-valleys," lowercase, hyphenated like a trail name. On the surface it's the ride — they talk on the flats and descents, the climbs take the breath for themselves, which is its own little law: the hard parts get done in silence. And that's Pace exactly — the man who went flat and quiet and left the bar without a word, doing his climb alone. It also can't help being the shape of the last three chapters: the dance floor's high, the bar's valley, this cold clear morning. It sits nicely and doesn't spoil anything. The book title keeps tightening: "A Polite Invitation" — and here's a chapter where the invitation's architect says of his other lover, politely, "That's not finished." The jacket's tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — hums under Sheri's arithmetic: nobody in this triangle has done anything but say yes, and it will be complicated anyway.

What I want next: Vee at his door in the black dress and the borrowed shoes, obviously — the repair scene the last three chapters have been banking toward, and I notice the book making me wait for it the way Randi made Vee wait. Guess from here: the reconciliation lands, and lands hot — he's gone on her and now I know it. What I dread, in the good way: those shoes and that scarf are Randi's, on Vee's body, walking into Pace's house — he'll know whose they are, and Vee won't know he knows. The book keeps dressing her in things whose provenance she can't read.
