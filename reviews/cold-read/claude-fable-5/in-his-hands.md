# Cold read (grounded) — In His Hands

## Reader reaction

The photo scene got me two ways at once, and I want to be honest about both. First the swoon, because it's real: him rolling crosswise over her to lay his head on her breast, "his feet off the far edge of the bed," looking up at her face — that's the tenderest image the book has given me of him, a big careful man made small and comfortable on her body. And *He's all mine*, in italics, right before he tells her he thinks of her when he's alone — I melted, and I felt her melt, and the melting was earned. Then he poses her, and this is where the chapter is doing something I felt in my chest before I could name it: he arranges her limb by limb, "at each place his hands set her he put his mouth there after," and she lies there "with no wish to argue with any of it," laid out like a woman on an old calendar. It's gorgeous. It's also him handling her exactly the way he handles everything he's made — the dress, the furniture, the joints with no screws — a kiss to the shoulder like a craftsman checking a fit. I loved it and I noticed it, both.

And then the promise. She says: "Only if you promise you'll never share me with anyone." He says: "Never. I'd never share your photograph." I read that twice. She asked about *her*; he answered about the *photograph*. This book has taught me that Pace's words are set down "clean and apart" — he is exact when it costs him nothing to be vague — and the reader-me, who knows Randi gets debriefed on everything, felt the temperature drop half a degree in a warm room. Because he *does* share her. He has shared her since before she knew he existed. The photo promise may be kept to the letter forever and the spirit of it is already broken. "She sent it before the nerve could leave her, and watched the bar cross... and gave him herself to keep." That sentence is beautiful and it made my stomach hurt. The tagline's ghost — every yes freely given — has never sat more squarely on a single tap of a screen.

The middle stretch is pure pleasure and I refuse to apologize for how much I enjoyed it. "You've used me terribly," she informs him, "with enormous satisfaction" — that's Vee whole: the girl who couldn't eat in front of people now demanding to be fed before she starves at his table. The cooking is long and I didn't want it shorter; the pan that "said so, loud at first, then settling to a steady talk," the lemon "in a fine bright rain," her taking the dish apart on her tongue and knowing "exactly whose doing that was." That last clause is the chapter in miniature — everything good in her life right now is *his doing*, including her ability to taste it, and she says it with gratitude where I hear it with a faint hum of dependency.

Then the two dark seeds at the table. "I don't go back" — the parents door again, but this time she put her hand flat on it and felt it not open. "There was no door left open for her to step through" — this book's doors are never casual, and his voice going "careful, each word set down clean and apart" is the most texture that wound has ever shown. I don't know what's behind it and I like that she didn't push; her letting "the quiet be kind" is her best instinct. And Sheri at Thanksgiving — Vee's flicker of jealousy, owned and honestly extinguished ("She's alone"), is lovely characterization. But I know what Vee doesn't: Sheri is the one person who knows about *both women*. Pie at that table while Vee is in Ohio, and where is Randi that day? The chapter doesn't ask. I did.

One more thing that snagged, softly: before the photo she goes to the bathroom and "worked at herself in his mirror until she'd found the version of herself she wanted him to keep." That's Vee building a face. In this book, faces built at mirrors belong to Randi. He'd said "I like you exactly like this" — and she went anyway. It's small, human, completely believable, and it whispered.

The title: "In His Hands" is everywhere — her limbs placed by them, the phone lifted in them, the garlic crushed under the heel of one, and finally her, the whole of her, given into his keeping. It doesn't recolor the chapter so much as name what the chapter kept showing me: how much of her is now literally his to hold, and how good his hands are, and how little she'd have left if they opened. The volume board — *With a Long Spoon, Book One: A Polite Invitation* — has never felt more like a warning read in comfort. She keeps accepting invitations that are perfectly polite.

Guesses from here, marked as guesses: that photo exists now, and I don't believe the book made it exist for nothing — I dread the frame in which someone else sees it, or the moment Vee learns how much of "her" has already been shared without a photograph at all. And Thanksgiving is arranging bodies: Vee in Ohio, Pace and Sheri at the table, Randi unaccounted for.
