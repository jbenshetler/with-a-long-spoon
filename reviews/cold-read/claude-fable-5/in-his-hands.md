# Cold read (grounded) — In His Hands

## Reader reaction

This chapter is almost pure warmth, and I want to say that first and plainly: it worked on me. After the run of the induction, the bet, the two costumes, the drawer — this is the settled interior of the thing they've made, an evening with three rounds in it and a dinner in the middle, and I read it smiling for long stretches. The opening image alone — her hair "a red curtain drawn against the lamp," kissing him inside it, blind — is the kind of intimacy this book does better than anything else I've read lately: explicit and tender at once, the soreness "she'd be aware of tomorrow and glad of." And then him rolling square across her to lay his head on her breast, kisses that are "not about sex for him" until her body brightens under his mouth anyway and "rather spoiled her theory, and pleased her to no end" — that's Vee's voice at its funniest and most alive, and I love her in it. *He's all mine,* she thinks, and I felt the double floor under that sentence give a little, because I know exactly how not-all-hers he is, and she doesn't, and the book didn't have to underline it. It just let her think it.

The photograph is the chapter's heart and its one true chill, and the chill is earned by a single word. She sets the condition: "Only if you promise you'll never share me with anyone." He answers: "Never. I'd never share your photograph." Read fast, it's a promise. Read the way this book has taught me to read him — a man who is exact, who never says a false word and never quite the whole one — and it's a narrower promise than the one she asked for. *Your photograph* is not *you*. He has been sharing *her* — the project of her, the debriefs, the whole architecture — with Randi from before the collision. I don't think the page is telling me he'll pass the picture on; I genuinely read his want as real and his word as good on its own terms. But the substitution in his sentence is the kind of thing this prose does on purpose, and it put a cold thumbprint in the middle of the warmest scene. Then "she believed him, and believing him was the brave part. She sent it… and gave him herself to keep" — and I ached, because her bravery is real and beautiful and it is being spent inside a frame she can't see. This is the jacket's whole cruel mechanism in one Delivered bar.

The posing sequence itself is gorgeous and did the thing to me the fitting did back on the riser: his hands setting her, "at each place his hands set her he put his mouth there after," shoulder, hip, knee. And I noticed — she came back from the mirror *arranged*, having built the version of herself she wanted him to keep, and he said "No, not like that. You'll have to wait" and made her *his* version instead. She's radiant about it ("her as his eyes had her"), and I'm mostly moved too — but that's also this book's engine in miniature: her self-presentation gently overwritten by his authorship, and her gratitude for the overwriting. I don't call that sinister on this page. I call it the thing I can no longer see innocently.

The kitchen is a joy, full stop. The pan that "said so, loud at first, then settling to a steady talk," the lemon coming off in "a fine bright rain," the wine glugged into the pan and her genuine sadness about it — I could taste this chapter. And the line that got me most wasn't erotic at all: "She had used to just eat. Now she tasted, and there was no going back to the other, and she knew exactly whose doing that was." That's the whole book — he has permanently changed her relationship to her own appetite — and whether that's the greatest gift anyone ever gave her or the deepest hook ever set in her is exactly the question the book refuses to close.

Then the two turns at the table, both small, both real. "I don't go back" — the parents door again, the one that closed at the fireside in ch25, and this time we get the sound of it closing: "each word set down clean and apart from the last." She takes her hand off it, and I respect her for it, and I want that door opened more than ever. And Sheri at Thanksgiving — Vee's flicker of jealousy, "another woman at his table on a day meant for family," extinguished by "She's alone." Here's where being ahead of her stings in a different key: Sheri isn't a rival, but Sheri is the one person who knows the shape of everything — "Two of them at once… complicated" — and she'll be at that table, and Vee's easy gladness ("she was glad he wouldn't spend the day by himself") is one more room she's standing in without knowing who else has a key. Also: Randi is nowhere in his Thanksgiving. Not mentioned, not accounted for. I noticed the absence and I don't know what it means, so I'm leaving it ajar.

Friction: almost none. If I press, the cooking passage runs long for a chapter that's already fed me several kitchens — but it earns it by being the best-written cooking in the book so far, and the "watched her take the first bite… as though she'd just handed him something" callback to his own confessed fantasy from an hour earlier in the same chapter is a lovely closed loop. No thumb on the scale that I felt; the "I'd never share your photograph" line is the opposite of over-explaining — it trusts me completely.

The title: "In His Hands" is doing at least four things and I like all of them — her body literally in his hands as he poses her; the phone in his hands at the foot of the bed; the dinner made by hand like everything else in that house; and the state of her, whole, entrusted — "gave him herself to keep." After the checkpoint's long ledger of hand-made things whose purpose was hidden (the bench, the shirt, the dress), a title that puts *her* in his hands reads warm on the surface and colder underneath, which is this book's signature move. *A Polite Invitation* and "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" — this chapter is maybe the purest specimen yet: the photo was her idea, her offer, her nerve, her send. Freely given, every pixel. That *is* the problem, and I felt it in my stomach as the bar crossed.

What I want next: I dread the photograph's afterlife — not because the page promised one, but because the book has taught me that everything given gets metabolized by the design, and I mark that clearly as a guess from here. I want the parents door opened. I want to know what Thanksgiving looks like — Vee in Ohio with the mother whose voice lives in her, Pace and Sheri at his table, and Randi… where? And I'm braced, still, for Cassie or Brooke to be the first to see a seam.
