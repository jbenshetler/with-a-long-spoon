# Cold read (grounded) — The Usual

## Reader reaction

Oh, this chapter wrecked me. After spending the last four chapters watching Vee break her own heart in the dark and Randi freeze inside a gilded Swiss fairytale, we finally get inside Pace's head — and the man who always goes down to sleep "easy as a man with nothing grinding in him" is drinking himself sick in the dark, bleaching his own vomit off the bathroom floor, and sobbing over a bowl of boiled chicken and broccoli when a truck drives past without turning in. My heart completely broke for him, even while I was cheering Sheri on for taking his head off across that diner booth.

My feelings about Pace shifted more in these pages than in the whole book so far. Up to now, even when I trusted his care, he had the terrifying composure of a man fully in command of his house, his women, and himself. Seeing him broken open makes him acheingly human. And finally, *finally*, the mystery of his silence on the porch is answered: he *did* understand Vee's "crackers and a meal" confession, and the true answer — that he loves her — rose in him whole and ready, and he "chose the smaller truth" because he was a coward. That line — *"at the moment she had asked him to be brave he had been weak"* — gave me such deep relief and sorrow at once. It wasn't coldness, and it wasn't calculation; it was superstitious terror left over from being fifteen and losing Daphne ("There had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone. Daphne was the person he would have told"). He taught himself that saying "I love you" is what makes a loss unsurvivable. And I loved Sheri more than ever here. She is the exact friend he needs: no awe, stealing his fries, cutting straight through his brick-by-brick defenses with *"Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty."* Plus we learn *why* she and Pace understand each other's silence about family — her ex Paige pushed her to "call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas," and Pace answers, *"People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice."*

The swoon and the ache here are inseparable. Watching him blush in a country diner and try to explain why he's so gone on Vee — *"The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles"* and how she understands *"the philosophy of"* his food — while Sheri roasts him ("Lord almighty. Get over yourself") made me melt. And then the morning after: the contrast between the feast he builds for Vee and the "prison food" he boils for himself from neat freezer rows ("The bowl held everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted") is devastating. When he hears the engine on the highway, knows immediately it's the wrong sound, and still can't stop his body from hoping until it passes and he bends over the table and sobs — that landed right in my chest.

And yet, the chapter keeps the central tension razor-sharp. Right in the middle of his whiskey grief, we learn he hasn't seen or heard from Randi in *six weeks* — not since the November morning she found Vee's green panties. He finally admits to himself her crying wasn't "hormones." Yet when he imagines the future he still wants, his daydream is the three of them in his kitchen the morning after: him at the stove, Vee in his sheet at the table, and Randi across from her with her feet up on "the lacquered seat." Even shattered, even knowing he loves Vee, he is *still* holding onto the fantasy of the three of them and wondering whether Vee "would carry it." That disconnect between how purely he loves Vee and how blind he still is to the bomb sitting in his house makes me hold my breath.

The erotic charge here is a quiet simmer of memory and longing — Sheri's bawdy *"Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you"* making his face hot, the remembered weight of Randi's back against his chest, the memory ofRand's voice while he was inside her telling him how Vee came back for the kiss, and Vee walking barefoot and unguarded to the bathroom. My only tiny bit of reader friction was a momentary timeline puzzle when Sheri mentioned *"That brunette you brought dancing before Thanksgiving — the sorority one"* — we haven't seen Pace take Randi to the Rusty Nail on the page, though it tracks that they went before the Vee era or earlier on, and it was delicious learning that Randi looked at Sheri like "one more of your harem applicants."

The title, **"The Usual,"** works on three levels at once: the diner order where the kid swaps the double burger and shake because he doesn't know tiny Sheri is the big eater; Pace's grim return to his old bachelor "usual" of freezer-bag chicken and broccoli; and his failed attempt to fall back on his usual emotional rule of keeping the word unsaid so loss stays survivable. Against **WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK TWO — A WARM RECEPTION**, it's almost ironic: the man who always kept a warm reception waiting behind an unlocked door is now sitting in a cold house where the laminate chills his palm, waiting for a car that doesn't turn in. Next, I desperately want the new term to start — I want him to walk up to Vee and say the words out loud, and I am dreading and dying to see what happens when Randi comes back from Gstaad after six weeks of silence.
