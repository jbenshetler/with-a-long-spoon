# Cold read (grounded) — Old Acquaintances

## Reader reaction

**Felt read.**

This is the loneliest chapter of the book, and it's lonely in a way that echoes — I only realized partway through that Randi spent her midnight in the exact same posture: lying against borrowed warmth she couldn't make her own, cold everywhere the warmth didn't touch, in a room that was doing its best for her and couldn't. Two women, two New Year's Eves, both keeping their real life a secret from the person texting them. Randi sent the fairytale; Vee read it as one. Neither is lying, exactly, and neither is telling the truth. That parallel gutted me, and neither of them can see it.

The opening image is the strongest thing here: "The room did not know her. It had known Vivienne — knew her still, in high resolution, and loved her." The ribbons, the little gold swimmers, the clarinet — this is the book saying out loud what the cardigan and the "floozie" voice have been circling for forty chapters: the girl her parents built is still the girl this house is keeping, and Vee has outgrown the bed, literally, knees bent since middle school. And that last line — "the cold room kept watch over the girl who was gone" — is Randi's "face" turned around. Randi left a woman on a bench and rebuilt a mask; Vee left a Vivienne in Ohio and came home to find her still dusted and loved and waiting. The room is wearing Vee the way Randi's face wears Randi. I don't think that's me reaching; the chapter put "the face" language and the mask motif too close to this for it to be accidental on my end.

What moved me most — and hurt most — is the photograph. "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it." She cropped the leg — the leg was the whole point, the leg was the moment her body "came up to meet the moment," the one time she was "out where a whole room could see" — and she scissored it off for her mother with her own hand. She does to herself, for her parents, exactly what the burgundy dress was built to undo. And then immediately after, she scrolls Randi's feed and envies it: "it did not matter that she knew what had bought the dream. She knew. She'd been in the glass room when the name came up and Randi blanched." I love that Vee's honesty extends even to her own envy — she names the Gstaad as "poisoned" and wants it anyway, from a twin bed with Wheel of Fortune in her ears. That's the realest thing in the chapter. Envy doesn't check its sources.

The Pace ache is nearly unbearable, and the book is doing something sly that I let work on me before I caught it: Vee "finds a who." She builds the blonde — "a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's," which is her mother's vocabulary arriving as self-hate, the floozie voice in new clothes — and I felt the floor tilt, because the unresolved blonde from the Rusty Nail is still open on the board. Is she building her from that night? The text says "some pretty girl from the dance hall," and the hair "went gold" — I genuinely can't tell if it's the same woman or the archetype of her, and I think Vee can't either. Then the worse thing: she reaches for her best memory, the fra diavolo night, the falling sheet — and the blonde sits down inside it. "She could not keep her own night." That line landed like a hand on my chest. That's not suspicion about Pace; that's the damage of namelessness, metastasizing. She has no standing to ask, so her imagination is free to cannibalize everything she has.

And underneath, I hold what Vee doesn't: he cooked for Sheri at Thanksgiving because "she's alone" — and Sheri is the woman who knows about both of them, who watched over Vee all night after the dance. Vee's jealousy keeps picking the wrong targets. Every time she reaches for the floor — the bartender's testimony, Randi's "he leaves you the room" — it goes through her hands, and she's right that it does: those assurances only ever held while she knew she was the one. The one hard fact she has (Sheri, the shared table) is actually the most innocent fact in the book, and it's the one eating her. The dramatic irony here is almost cruel, and I mean that as praise.

The 11:59 reach — arms around her, his arms — that "would not hold" because of whose he had them around instead: that's the whole chapter in one image, wanting and fear occupying the same embrace. And "A day she could circle for the one and nothing at all for the other" — she has a scheduled hour with Randi and nothing with Pace, and she's honest enough to end on holding both and setting neither down. Including the man she may have just lost with "what about Pace," and letting nothing come after it.

Friction, honestly, is minimal. The billionaire line — "a devastatingly handsome billionaire" — winked a little hard at the previous chapter's John (who read as money, not billions), but it's Vee inflating Randi's world from outside, so it plays. Otherwise I was fully inside this cold room the whole time. It's a quiet chapter, but nothing dragged; the solitude *is* the content.

The title: "Old Acquaintances" — Auld Lang Syne, obviously, the song of the night it's set on, and it did tell me going in this was New Year's Eve, which the previous chapter had already established, so nothing was given away. But it means more than the song. The room full of old acquaintances — the ribbons, the trophies, the girls "who hadn't texted in a year" — and the old acquaintance Vee can't stop being for her parents. Should auld acquaintance be forgot — the whole chapter is Vee unable to forget the girl she was, and unable to fit back inside her. And, faintly, the Randi question: their friendship is an "old acquaintance" that's actually new, built on a collision. The volume title, *A Polite Invitiation*, still means nothing concrete to me — if anything, Pace's courtly "May I" style is the closest it's come to touching ground.

What I want next: the term starting, that first Randi morning — I'm braced for the goodbye kiss with its held beat now that both women are carrying unnamed things. And I want Pace's silence broken from *his* side, because the reader knows something Vee doesn't: his "I love how good we are together" was an assembling, a deflection, and I don't know if he spent the break folding her out or working up to the word. Guess from here: the reunion will be at the anthro class or a brunch, not his house — the book has taken the door away and I don't think it hands it straight back.
