# Reader reaction — "Old Acquaintances"

This chapter landed on me harder than almost anything in the book so far, and it did it with nothing happening — a girl alone in a cold bedroom while the year runs out. Vee lying awake in Ohio is the loneliest page this book has given me.

The room did it first. "The room kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed" — that's the whole chapter in one line. The corkboard ribbons, the frozen little gold swimmers, the clarinet she hasn't touched since senior year: a museum of Vivienne, and Vee doesn't live there anymore. "She'd been breathing bigger air for months, and come home to a place that no longer fit her." That's exactly the shape of coming home from college, and it ached. The bent knees — the bed her legs outgrew in middle school, and she's grown again since — is my favorite physical detail the book has done; she's too big for this life twice over.

And the way the chapters rhyme: I read "Fairytale" last, where Randi lies awake in a luxury hotel with a beautiful stranger, fixing her face, deciding *not* to tell Vee — and now the mirror, Vee lying awake in a twin bed under lavender plugins, sending that open-hand text, and Randi's reply arriving from exactly the scene I just watched her fake her way through. "Every word of it true. None of it the thing" — and Vee receives it and *fills it in herself*: "a devastatingly handsome billionaire, a weekend so wild Vee wouldn't recognize half of what they did." The dramatic irony is brutal. Vee even supplies Randi a lover she'd just taken from a man she experienced as a sandwich eaten fast. Neither of them can say the thing, and they're texting warm nothings across six time zones at each other. Vee's line — "Randi, the only girl she could talk to about real things. The real things they talked about were mostly Pace" — while I know Randi's real thing is Vee herself. The gap between what that text means to each of them is the saddest thing in the chapter.

The Pace material: her half-typed *are you* — and deleting it letter by letter, "glad he wasn't a man who lived on his phone." "He left you the room. He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now." That's the fight recast: what she loved about him (the restraint, the room) is exactly what starves her now. The split photograph cut me — the induction photo she sent him whole, and the one she scissored at the waist for her mother. "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it." She's learned his habit of withholding without noticing.

Then the spiral at midnight. The jealousy fantasy — building the blonde "from spite," a "stripper's body" where the blonde's is a "fashion model's" (that's Vee's shame vocabulary creeping into her self-image, and it stung) — and worse, the corruption of the fra diavolo memory, the best night she has, until the girl at the table turns blonde. "She could not keep her own night" is a terrible sentence in the good way. And the detail she claws at the wound with: Sheri at Thanksgiving — *she's alone* — and "about Christmas he'd said nothing at all. A whole week she couldn't see into." It's not jealousy of a fact; it's jealousy of his opacity, that she has no name for herself and therefore no claim, no standing even to ask.

Two things felt slightly less than fully earned. The spiral to jealousy comes fast — the pivot from the fra diavolo comfort memory to the blonde in it is genuinely chilling, but I'd have liked one more beat of the bartender's and Randi's reassurances failing her ("the floor was gone" — that worked, actually, maybe I take it back). And "a stripper's" self-description is harsh enough that I want to be sure the book knows it's her mother's voice — I think it does.

The title, "Old Acquaintances" — the song, obviously, the midnight song, old acquaintance not forgot. Everyone in this chapter is holding onto someone at the stroke of midnight and lying about it: Randi to her safe life, Vee to both of them, Pace to his silence. The book title, *A Warm Reception*, keeps turning ironic — Vee back in the warm house that no longer fits, wanting the one warmth she can't ask for.

I dread and want the next chapter: term starts, and Randi's "face that would light up and call her gorgeous" is the one sure thing she has. Pace has "no room he'd surely be in." I dread the first day of stats class.
