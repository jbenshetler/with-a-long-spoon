# Cold read (grounded) — Off Six-Fourteen

## Reader reaction

The first thing that hit me was pure gladness — this is the happiest chapter in the book so far, and it's happiness earned, and I felt it in my chest the way Vee's spin must have felt in hers. After "The Bench," which left me chilled, and "Standards," which left me colder, and all the watching-Randi-perform chapters, this room — the cracked window, the sock feet, the highlighter — felt like the warmest place in the whole book. Two friends, one of them lit up from the inside, the other one flatly, completely glad for her.

And the center of it, the thing I keep turning over: "Nobody did anything to me. I wasn't talked into it. I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose." That line landed on me harder than anything in the bench chapter, because it's the exact thing Randi *couldn't* say. Randi on the bench had her yes pulled out of her, real and hers, yes — but the aftermath was the bathroom mirror and the smile reassembling and *not ever*. Vee under her tree got the same man's same restraint — he walked on, he left the last inch — and she closed the distance herself, and then came home and told her best friend about it in a room with cold air coming in and nothing performed. That's the contrast the whole book is built on, and this chapter makes it without once pointing at it. Vee gets the scene Randi couldn't survive: being seen and being *glad* about it.

Cassie, meanwhile, keeps being my favorite person here. "How much did he give you to drink?" — that's the question almost nobody in this book would ask, the flat dry floor under the gladness, and it's exactly right. She's the only character whose care shows up as suspicion. And the exchange that followed — "Nothing. We had *tea*" — was Vee passing a test she didn't know she was taking, and Cassie believing her, and something in me relaxed that I didn't know was tight. But I notice Cassie also can't quite say *goodbye* to this the way she did at the lecture hall ("Goodbye, Vee," flat and finished). Here she says "Borrow good ones" and goes back to her page. She's staying. I'm holding that earlier goodbye open, not deciding what it was.

The unease, and I want to be honest that it's small but real: "off six-fourteen, past where it goes to gravel." Cassie makes the joke for me — "That's the whole address" — and I laughed, and then the laugh didn't entirely settle. Vee is being carried somewhere she can't see the shape of, again, and she's *glad* about it, and my memory of what this man's house contains — the bench with the table-top, the toy box, the lamplight — sits under this chapter like the water under the pool. She doesn't know what "his place" means yet. He asks her hiking, not to the house, and I noticed that he did the same careful sequencing with Randi. I don't distrust him the way I'd distrust a villain; I distrust the pattern, mildly, the way you'd notice a man always dealing from the same deck. The not-kiss, the restraint, the "May I?" — all of it is genuinely good, and all of it is also *how he works*. Both things are true and I'm holding both.

The swoon is real, though, and I won't let my wariness eat it. The spin. "The room too small to hold what she had to do with it." Vee clumsy and breathless in a bean bag — this girl who is all held breath and cardigans and mothers' voices in her head, doing a full-body turn. That unraveled me. And Cassie taking the pillow on the shoulder without unfolding — the whole friendship is in that one gesture.

Heat: almost none in the act sense, and yet the chapter is warm all the way through — it's afterglow relayed, and it works.

The title, "Off Six-Fourteen": it's the road, the half-address, the unknown. Cassie's joke makes the title the joke — he lives somewhere a friend couldn't find her. It recolors the warmth just slightly, the way titles in this book do. The volume title, *A Polite Invitation*, and the series line *With a Long Spoon* — the invitation half has been delivered (the hike, "real shoes"), and I'm braced for whatever the house holds. The spoon line still says to me that supper with the devil is coming, eventually, and this chapter is the part before the supper.

What I want next: the hike. And I want Pace and Vee on the trail together — I want to see whether the man who was so careful in daylight holds that care outdoors, or whether the trail is where the choosing starts. Guess from here: the hike ends at the house, or within sight of it. And I dread, mildly, that it goes well — because going well is how this book catches you.
