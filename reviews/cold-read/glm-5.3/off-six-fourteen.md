## Reader reaction

The chapter title — "Off Six-Fourteen" — is the address. That's what lands at the end, and it lands oddly. The whole chapter is a happy, small-hours dorm room scene — the best friend debrief after a first date — and the title is just the fragment Vee offers Cassie when pressed about where Pace lives. "Off six-fourteen, past where it goes to gravel." Cassie is the one who flags it: "That's the whole address. Off six-fourteen." And the scene laughs it off. But because of what I carry from the earlier chapters — that this is a man with a house, a bench, a habit of building things in advance for women — the title's casualness has a small cold spot in it. I don't distrust Pace, exactly. But I noticed that Vee's safety dossier ("See. I know where he lives and everything") is a road number and a promise he'll bring water, and the book noticed it too, through Cassie. That might be nothing. It might be the book being honest about how little Vee actually knows. I'm holding it open, not deciding.

The felt read first: this chapter is pure warmth, and I let it be. After "The Bench," which left me shaken and a little heartsick for Randi, and the long careful build of Vee's side of the story, I *needed* this. Vee comes home glowing and talks with her whole body; the spin that nearly takes out the closet door; the bean-bag heap; "Look at me." That spin did more for me than a page of declared feelings would have. She is so clearly the girl who kept herself covered to the collarbone at Chi Latte, who heard her mother's voice over the froyo cup — and here she is, alone with the one person she doesn't perform for, doing a clumsy dance because she kissed someone *on purpose*. The chapter's emotional center is exactly where it should be: "Nobody did anything to me. I wasn't talked into it. I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose." Given everything this book has shown me about Pace's patience being a kind of power — with Randi especially — Vee saying that, and Cassie *believing* it, is the cleanest note in the book so far. And I believe it too. Whatever Pace is doing, in this chapter's history he asked "May I choose?", walked away from a kiss without pressing, and Vee pulled him back. The consent texture with Vee has been the book's best thing.

Cassie continues to be the person I trust most. "How much did he give you to drink?" is exactly the question, and the fact that it comes *after* the gladness — she grins first, suspects second — is what makes her real. She isn't a guard dog; she's a friend whose love has a flat dry floor under it. "Borrow good ones" as a closing line is perfect: it's shoe advice and it's *be careful* in the same four words, and Vee takes it as shoe advice, and that gap is the whole chapter's quiet undertow. Cassie's "Goodbye" from the lecture chapter still sits with me — she has some instinct about Randi's orbit, or about where all this points — and here she's got the state troopers joke, the "is he rich" deflection. I have no suspicion of Cassie. My unease, when it comes, is about the structure I can half-see: Randi chose Vee on a walk where Pace pointed, and then a "cute guy in line" happened to cross a coffee shop to help Vee with the exact problem she was stuck on. As a reader I don't know that these are connected — Vee certainly doesn't — but the coincidence has started to itch. The book has taught me that Pace plans. I'm marking that as a suspicion the text has earned, not a conclusion.

Heat: essentially none in the body, and the chapter doesn't want any. The charge is retrospective — the kiss retold in a smaller voice — and it works as warmth rather than heat, which is right. What pulls me onward is dramatic irony, not desire: two women, one house off six-fourteen. Randi deciding, in the bathroom mirror, that "he could have the face" and the found one was never coming out again — and now Vee, the girl Randi *pointed at with her whole body* on the quad, is being walked toward the same man by what looks like chance. If the book is going where I half-guess it's going, "A Polite Invitation" as a volume title reads darker and truer every chapter — the politeness is real, and so is whatever's being invited to. But the subtitle's warmth hasn't been a lie yet; Pace has genuinely been good to Vee. Both things can be true. That's the book's whole trick.

Friction: almost none. The scene is short and earns its length. The only moment that brushed me wrong was Cassie's "is he rich" routine going on just slightly long — a beat of comic shtick in an otherwise precise scene — but it recovers into "Borrow good ones." Vee's not knowing whether he's rich, and it not troubling her, is a nice inversion of Randi, who read his furniture like a price tag she couldn't parse.

I want the hike. I dread the hike a little. And I'm braced for whenever Randi and Vee stop being parallel and start being the same room.
