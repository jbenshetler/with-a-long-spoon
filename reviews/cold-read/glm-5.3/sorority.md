# Cold read (grounded) — Sorority

*scene: scenes/sorority.md · model: glm-5.3 · memory: ck-ch020 + raw ch021..ch024 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

Oh, this chapter. This chapter is the sunshine before — well, I don't know before *what*, but the book keeps handing Vee these golden days and I keep enjoying them while a small cold voice in me takes notes.

**The felt read**

Stats class first, and the structure of it got me immediately: Vee walks in "fit to burst," and for once she's the one with the news, the center, and *both* of her friends turned toward her — the professor doing Simpson's paradox up front while the two women beside her have eyes only for her. And that's the joke and the ache of the whole scene, because the professor's lecture is the book whispering its own secret to a room that isn't listening. Same numbers, looked at one way and then another, and the answer reverses. Nobody touched a single data point. That is *exactly* what's being done to Vee — every individual event of her life these weeks reads as devotion and friendship, and I, the reader, know that aggregated another way the picture flips. I honestly got a little chill when he said "Same numbers. Nobody touched one of them." The dramatic irony here is almost unbearable, and it's beautiful because it's deniable — a reader who wasn't wary would just think it's a funny lecture scene.

Cassie watching Vee instead of the board, though — that's the beat that got me in the heart. "The friend was the only part of any of it Cassie had ever been able to see." Cassie doesn't care about the dress; she cares what the dress *did to Vee*, and "Was it a good night?" with the unplanned "Oh, yes" following it, and something easing in Cassie's face — that's love, plain and unadorned, and it made me trust her even more. Cassie remains my ground truth. And Randi in that same lecture: "for once there was nothing at all on her face." Nothing. That blankness, right after last chapter — after she rode Pace with her eyes shut and said no *tell me* and went to his kitchen to pick a fight about how long he's taking with Vee — reads to me like a woman holding something heavy very still. Then "You lucky slut," warm and delighted, and the mask is back. The page keeps giving me Randi in two layers, and I've stopped being able to read the seam.

The dress showing scene is the swoon of the chapter. "He didn't make this *for* you. He made it *to* you." I gasped a little, honestly — that names the exact thing Vee cried over in the sun porch, and hearing it in Randi's mouth, from the woman whose *lover* made the dress, has a doubled edge the page doesn't acknowledge and doesn't have to. Randi's fingers on the bias, "Charmeuse," spoken "not to Vee; to herself, or to the dress" — she reads a cut the way a seamstress's lover would. Of course she does; she's watched a man build a dress onto a woman before. Vee doesn't know why that hand at the shoulder seam goes somewhere else "for a breath" — but she *notices*, reads it as jealousy of Pace's attentiveness, and wants for Randi "so much it hurt." That misreading is almost too poignant: Vee's generosity aimed at the one person who doesn't need her pity — or needs something else entirely. The tears wanted to come.

The shoe stores, then, and I just plain had fun. The yeti shoe, the pink rhinestone platforms, "one brisk walk from becoming a sandal" — Randi's real laugh, "the one that costs her something to give" — that's friendship on the page, real warmth, and I fell for it exactly as Vee does. Which is the trap, and I know it's the trap, and I fell in anyway. Then the boutique, and the money. Oh, the money. This chapter took Vee's oldest wound — reading the menu right-side-first, Thursday arithmetic — and escalated it with terrible tenderness: the debit card held between her fingers and put back, the credit card laid down, "There was nothing under it. She knew that even while she did it." The most she's ever spent on anything but tuition or rent. And the justification she builds — "The shoes were not a want she had to own as a want. They were what the dress was owed" — is Vee's whole machinery in one sentence: the want laundered into an obligation so she doesn't have to name it wanting. The book has been tracking exactly this since the frosted plum toes, and now it's a number sitting on a card. That landed as dread dressed as triumph.

And then the shoe-store knee, the moment I have to name plainly: Randi kneeling, taking Vee's foot, the thumb across the plum-painted toenail — once, light — and it goes "all the way up the length of her" and Vee's hand shoots out for Randi's shoulder. Randi looks up, smiles, says nothing, and bends back to the box. That is the hottest five lines in the chapter, and the most dangerous, because it's deniable as fitting-room courtesy — and Randi *saw* the polish, the polish she herself decoded last chapter as painted-for-a-man. Randi knows exactly what she touched. The heat between these two is now on the page in daylight, and Vee still has no shelf for it. Also: "Same size, same everything. We can share. God help us both, it's practically a marriage." Randi saying that, given everything I know — I don't know what to do with it, and I mean that as the highest compliment.

Where I've landed on suspicion: my wariness of Randi *deepened* not because she did anything wrong here — she was generous, funny, and the loafers-on-the-counter beat is her wealth rendered casually, not cruelly — but because of the pattern of her silences. The long breath at the shoulder seam, the blank face in class, the nothing-said after the thumb. And a guess I want to mark clearly as a guess from here: Saturday's shopping trip might have been Pace's and Randi's design too — "how long are you going to take?" — the plan pushing Vee further open, further spent, further *dressed for* the induction, on credit. I can't prove that from the page; what's on the page is a friend spending a golden afternoon on her. That's the book's whole trick and I'm inside it.

Ending: the boxes on the sidewalk, the laugh that bends her at the middle, "she had never once in her life had a friend like this." I believe her completely. That's what makes this book's unease honest rather than cheap.

The title, "Sorority" — interesting, because the sorority itself barely appears; no Brooke, no house. It's Randi's world pressing in at the edges: the early-to-class habit, the induction the dress is *for*, the girls in "the one dress she owns that has to do for everything." The title points forward to the induction night, and to which world Vee will belong to when she walks in late and stops the room. Book title still: *With a Long Spoon, Book One — A Polite Invitation* — the supper-with-a-long-spoon proverb, dining with the devil at arm's length, and this chapter is arm's-length intimacy everywhere: Randi's finger on a toenail, Vee's secret kept zipped in its bag, "shown to not one living soul." The invitation gets politer and deeper at once.

What I want next: the induction. The dress worn in company for the first time. What I dread: the bill — literal and otherwise — and whatever Randi's next "date" produces. And whether Cassie says another odd, final goodbye.
