# Cold read (grounded) — Not Enough

*scene: scenes/not-enough.md · model: glm-5.3 · memory: ck-ch050 + raw ch051..ch052 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

The chapter title does its quiet work before the first paragraph is done: *Not Enough*. Last night's diner chapter was Sheri telling him, in every way but the word, the same thing Vee told him on the porch. Now he wakes to the morning after and the title is a verdict on the whole book, or at least on him — the smaller truth wasn't enough, the whiskey wasn't enough, and even grief, eaten cold with a shaking hand, isn't enough.

What landed on me first was physical. This is a body chapter — his, for once, instead of hers, and it's the least glamorous body in the book. Tongue stuck to the roof of his mouth, the house tipping, the bleach smell reaching the bedroom, the mop and bucket still in the hall with their crust of foam. I've spent fifty chapters watching this man as the one who is never not in control — the thermostat set hours in advance, the plate set down only when she's aching for it — so seeing him sick, undone, his own house turned against him, hits hard. The bleach reaching the bedroom is the detail that got me. He was sick enough in the night to clean it up before he passed, or clean enough of it, and then still had to step around the evidence in the morning. Even his wreckage is tidy. That's the most Pace thing in the chapter.

And then the chicken and broccoli. I laughed — a sad laugh — at "prison food," the remembered woman, "no cruelty on either side and no part of the world altered by it." That's his whole romantic history in three lines: relationships that ended and left the world unaltered. Against that, the freight of "the air crackles" from the diner. This chapter is him doing the arithmetic of what Vee is, and the arithmetic keeps coming out the same. "The bowl held everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted" — that's him, in his own house, being fed by himself the way Vee was fed by her mother's casserole: nutrition that isn't a meal. The man who fed her a meal is now eating crackers. I don't think the book is being subtle about it and I don't care; it earned it.

The Vee passages are almost unbearable in the best way. "He missed watching her arrive. He missed the moment she looked up from the turn and found him waiting." And then the counter-image: taillights down through the cut, himself in the open door, not moving. That's the two ends of the same stoop, and he's stood on both now. The unguarded backside walking to the bathroom, the red hair over white cotton, the first bite always telling him — her pleasure arriving before her words — that's the book's whole erotic grammar (feeding, being watched, understanding what it's *for*) replayed from the other side of the table, and it ached.

The Daphne section: my checkpoint memory had this already, but reading it in full is different. "There had been no one to tell that Daphne was gone. Daphne was the person he would have told." That line is the wound in one sentence. And then the terrible child-logic laid bare: say the word, loss becomes un-survivable; keep it, loss stays survivable. He taught himself this. And the book lets him state the conclusion plainly: *"Whiskey River* was a liar" — the whiskey took his balance, his dinner, some hours, "and it had left every memory of Vee intact." The anesthetic doesn't touch the thing it was for. That's the chapter's whole argument and I buy it completely.

The truck. His head lifting before he knew he'd moved — that's the third time this image has reached me (the porch where she walked, the walkout taillights, and now), and it still works, maybe because it's not played for suspense. We know it's not her. *He* half-knows it's not her. The engine holds steady; it does not slow; and he gets one free second of her anyway — the car in the drive, the smile only he gets — before the house takes her back. Then the fork going down "before he dropped it," the breath that breaks, the sobbing bent over the table. And then: "He was not hungry. He ate anyway." That's the exact rhyme of Vee's mother's love — food eaten because it's offered and it's love and it was never the thing you were hungry for — except he's both the cook and the unwilling eater of it now. The man who never made her eat anything is making himself eat, and it's the closest thing to self-punishment I've seen from him.

Where I stand: this didn't move my feelings so much as confirm them at depth. Pace remains tender and frustrating in exactly the same proportion. Sheri isn't in the room but her verdict runs under the whole chapter — "not saying it matters more to you than having her" — and this is him living in the consequence. The mystery to me is what the word actually costs now. He's named the fear, named the lie of the whiskey, named that he chose small out of weakness. He knows everything except how to say three words. I believe he's at the bottom. The question the book keeps me holding open: does the bottom teach him, or does he just keep eating cold chicken?

One small friction: I had a flicker of déjà vu, like some of this (the truck, the sobbing, the eating anyway) had reached me before as a described after-image of the diner night — but read as the morning-after in full, it earns its own chapter. The "Not Enough" title gives nothing away; if anything it reframes *A Warm Reception* — the warm reception was the house's temperature all along, and this chapter is the house warm and empty.

What I want next: Vee. The term has to start. The Anthro class, Randi's face on day one, and the question of whether Pace has an hour now. I dread and want the first collision. Guess from here: the new term opens with Vee and Randi in Dr. Marsh's section, and Pace has to decide whether "no hour I could name him to" is something he'll fix.
