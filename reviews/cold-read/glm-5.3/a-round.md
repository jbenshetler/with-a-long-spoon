## Reader reaction

Well. I had to put the book down twice, and not because it was too much — because it was too exact.

Everything I've felt about Pace holds, and this chapter tightened it to a single image: a man on his knees with a pencil behind his ear and a measuring tape, taking an hour to look at a woman who has never been looked at like that in her life. Every beat of this scene is the same gesture the whole courtship has been — hand her the choosing, keep the surface polite, make the purpose exact. And it *works on me*, completely, even knowing what I know: that this house holds a bench I've seen and Vee hasn't, that the riser she stepped onto with such trust is furniture made by the same hands, in the same workshop, that built the other thing in the basement of this plot. The dress and the bench are the same object wearing different clothes. I keep having to remind myself of that, and the chapter keeps making me forget it, which I think is the entire design — but on the page, right now, he is real to me: he goes red, he fights a grin and loses, he kisses the pin-prick on her ribs *before he's decided to*. That's a man, not a script.

Vee. This is her chapter the way the truck was her chapter, and it goes further: not just choosing to be seen, but being seen *at her worst moment* — soaked satin, shame the size of a thumbprint spread past hiding, eyes screwed shut — and opening them to find him craning his neck *up*, waiting for her face, having never once looked down. "The whole tender hour had been a courtesy" — that's the fear, the whole fear, her whole life's fear, and it didn't come true. I actually got a little misty, honestly, at her shoulders coming back and her rising onto her toes without asking her body's permission. And then she spends the new power: the deliberate "Oops. Sorry." breast against his cheek, the turned-when-he-didn't-say-turn. That flip — from the nervous girl bracing to someone who's found out she has power she was never issued — is the best character movement in the book so far. The interiority here is the thing I read this book for: the shame arriving *as heat*, the maybes counted and not believed, "He wasn't doing anything. He was *making a dress*." That line is the chapter.

Randi isn't here and her absence hums — because I know what she doesn't: that Pace "did not say how he knew" her stomach was soft, and now he's kneeling with his face at the height of the worst of it. This is the payoff of the truck scene, and it's being banked somewhere. Fine. I'm wary of the architecture, not the man in the room.

Small frictions, honestly minor: the "muse" beat she sets down quickly felt like the author also setting it down quickly — telling me about a word she rejected is still giving me the word. And the mother's-voice invocations ("stripper," "floozie," "can he tell?") are dense here — earned each time, but I noticed the density. The title, "A Round," I take as the making of the dress *and* the drinking-vessel pun and maybe a round of the game — this is a turn, his round; I half suspect her round is coming (guess from here). Volume title *A Polite Invitation* grows teeth: two o'clock, "if you'll let me," tea and a blanket and no demand — the politeness is the invitation, and the invitation is deeper in than she can see yet. The house's too-hot air, prepared hours before she came, was the chapter's quiet tell for me — care indistinguishable from arrangement, which is the book's whole trick in one thermostat.

What I dread and want is the same thing: the bench. It's in this house. The tour walked her past every room and shut one door, and the book has never once let me forget what's behind a closed door in Pace's history. And "she'd chosen with hope" — the satin she put on *for* him being the fabric her shame shows through — that's the tidiest piece of cruelty-as-tenderness I've read in a while, and I mean that as praise of the prose, not the man.
