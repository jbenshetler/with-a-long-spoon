## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

I spent this whole chapter with my stomach in a strange knot, and it took me a while to name why: I know something Vee doesn't. Randi was texting "some boy" with absorbed attention; Randi's hands kept going to her coffee cup and *not sipping*; Randi watched the whole encounter with a "go on, this is nice" smile and then said, plainly, "You should go out with him." I read this book's earlier chapters. I know who Pace is. I know who Randi is to him. I know about the pointing game and the "go get her" and the bench question ("Do you think she'll like the bench?"). So watching a pleasant, ordinary-faced man cross a coffee shop to "help with math" — while Vee's heart goes like she's pushed off the deep end — I'm not watching a meet-cute. I'm watching a delivery. And the text knows I know: Cassie watching Randi watch him, Randi's hand in her lap, the twice-lifted cup with no sip. Whether Vee clocked it, the page left at the edges — she filed Randi's busy hand without filing it. That restraint is what makes the knot instead of a stamp.

And here's the thing that complicates my righteousness: I fell for him too. The seduction scene is genuinely lovely — the way he asks what her *professor* said rather than performing his own answer, the pencil handed back "at the exact line where she could solve it herself," the *may I* that lands on Vee like a word from a vocabulary no man her age owns. That's the same attentiveness that undid Randi on the bench, turned on a new girl, and it works on me exactly as it's designed to. I blushed with Vee. I wanted the phone to light up. And then I remembered the bench chapter and felt dirty about it, which I think is the chapter's actual effect: it makes you complicit, then shows you the machinery you were complicit with. "Substitution" — the title gives me a chill now. It works three ways: the substitution trick in the math (turn the problem into something you already know), Vee substituting Randi's practiced charm-bit for her own name ("V.T. Yes, like the university" — she *borrowed* the swerve she watched Randi do), and the cold reading: Randi substituting Vee for herself. The thing Vee can't see is that she's the answer to a question Randi's body already answered in bed.

The forearms, the hands "used for things besides writing," the shirt whose shape she "kept reaching for and it would not come" — I love how the prose lets Vee's body register him before her mind catches up, "a full second" behind. And the froyo scene undid me a little even with the knot underneath: "that's plenty, that's enough now" — the mother's voice *not arriving* today. After watching Randi's apple-and-calculation relationship to food, watching Vee build a leaning, too-much cup and "find she didn't care" is quiet and true. "Your tart's holding up" is a warm line. That's the tenderness I trust — the part that isn't the trap. Or is it? That's my dread: with Pace, the tenderness and the trap have been the same thing since chapter one. "I've thought about most things," said "without apology," set me down exactly where he meant it to go — the man plans; the bench was built in advance; the water bottle was left in advance.

Randi frightens me more than he does right now. Her one-word "Randi" to him — *no adjective bit, no parents thing* — was the most nakedly hostile thing in the chapter without being hostile at all: she refused to perform for him, which means she knows exactly who he is, and she handed him Vee anyway, and coached her ("The rule's for boys you're not sure about. You're not unsure"), and sold it ("He wants to see you naked"). That line from her mouth is the book's cruelest joke so far — it's true, and it's her own experience being passed along like a tip. The *n-minus-one* question was her poking at him, I think — testing him in front of Vee, or giving him a cue to establish "math PhD" (which matches exactly what she told Brooke). I'm not sure which, and I like not being sure.

Cassie is my ground truth. She watched Randi watching. She named how he looked at Randi ("the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt") — Cassie sees the geometry and says it flat, as she says everything. And her flat little "Goodbye, Vee" from last chapter echoes here; she said Vee should watch out, in her way, before any of this happened.

Heat: low in act, high in undercurrent — this is the erotic book at its most dangerous, all charge and no contact.
