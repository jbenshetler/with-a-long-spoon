**Felt read:**

My heart is pounding. This is the chapter I've been bracing for since the drawer and the flannel and the unnamed thing — Vee finally standing on the doormat and refusing to cross into the warm, and everything she says is everything the book has been building: "with no name on it the best thing in her life left her mouth sounding like that." That line gutted me. The whole speech — "Like my body comes with me attached. Like it's just fine as it is. Nobody ever made me feel that" — is the most honest she's ever been, and it's devastating that she has to spend it on a man who won't spend a word back on her.

Pace broke something for me here. I've trusted him for forty chapters — his care is real, the heat turned up before she arrives, the wine already poured, "the whole good evening laid out for her to take." But when she asks the simplest question in the world — *what am I to you* — he answers with a deflection ("Do any of your friends have a relationship you'd rather have?"), and then with the chilling "Words are cheap. Believe what I do." She's right and it's brutal: words are cheap, and he still won't spend them. The arms folding, the "still of a man holding a line at cost" — I read that as him protecting something (the Daphne wound? something about his mother? something he's never said to anyone), but the page doesn't tell me, and Vee can't live inside his silence anymore. "Not the hand in place of the word" — yes. That's the whole failure in one line.

The title — *Believe Me* — is his line turned into an indictment. "Believe what I do." The chapter takes his creed and shows its floor: deeds without words leave her nothing to carry outside the door. And the chapter beats her to the exit: she leaves before he can settle her with his hands, and his one word behind her is just her name. "Her name was not the word she'd come for." That's the loneliest sentence in the book.

Where was Randi in all this? Nowhere on the page, and that absence is enormous. The triangle that produced this relationship has evaporated from the scene of its reckoning. I don't suspect anyone of anything here — the text earned every beat — but I dread what she doesn't know colliding with what she just learned. The flight in the morning ("the suitcase for the morning's flight") means she leaves tomorrow, back to Ohio, to the mother whose voice says *floozie*. She walks back into the mother's territory with this wound.

The pulled-toward-pulled-apart rhythm killed me: the unlocked door, the two glasses poured, the towel over his shoulder — all the tenderness still running, still real, and useless. Her tasting "a coin under the tongue," shoulders at her ears: her body knew what she was about to do before she did it. I felt the wanting and the refusal simultaneously, and that's the hottest and coldest the book has run for me.
