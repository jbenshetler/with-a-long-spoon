## Reader reaction

Cassie first, because she opened the chapter and she's the one I trust most in the whole book. "You keep taking your shirt off in front of this guy and not getting any?" is exactly the right joke at exactly the right size — it lets Vee laugh before it lets her confess. And then the chapter quietly does the most devastating small thing: Vee tells Cassie everything *except* the secret. "The thing she had folded up the smallest and pushed the deepest — not Cassie, not anyone." Cassie gets her close, gets the geometry, gets "like I was supposed to be there" — and still doesn't get the truth. That gap ached. Cassie's "Goodbye, Vee" from chapters ago is still sitting in my memory, and her quiet attention here keeps that unease alive for me without the page confirming it.

Then Randi. I came into this chapter warm toward her — the smoothie shop, the capital she spent on Vee in front of Brooke, the low-voiced "ask and then ravish." This chapter put a thumb of ice on that warmth and I can't decide whether to trust what I felt. The Mercedes smile being "a thing spent rather than handed around" moved me — I *want* the real Randi to be the one Vee gets. And then the lunch. On a reread of my own memory it's masterful: the scarf story planted first (helplessness, "done right," exactly the door Vee is standing in front of with Pace), then "start at the beginning and don't you dare clean it up" — an instruction disguised as affection — then the ladder. "Nobody measures over a bra." "It's just how it's done." Each rung a kindness that happens also to be a lever, each "yeah" extracted one at a time, "all the best ones start at the left hip, which meant nothing at all and landed like a benediction." And then — the worst beat in the chapter — Vee's secret actually rises, her mouth actually opens, the wanting to *give* it is real and hers — and Randi says "You don't have to tell me." Gentle. A door closing softly. My stomach dropped with Vee's. Why did she stop it there, when she'd climbed the whole ladder to get it? The page leaves it ajar and I'm leaving it ajar, but I'll say plainly: my warmth toward Randi is now a suspicion, and the text earned it — the thumbnail on the glass rim (the bottle-cap gesture come back from chapter one, now under a smile), the too-bright keyed-up attention. She wanted the *getting*, not the having. Or she wanted it and couldn't bear to hear it. I genuinely can't tell which, and that not-knowing is the chapter's real charge.

The kiss at the end — I melted *and* flinched in the same breath. Physically it landed: the hand at the hinge of the jaw, the second press that had something in it the first hadn't. And then Vee's instant explanation ("*French,* it's a French thing") — that's her whole apparatus in one gesture, the mother-voice shame arriving as fast as the heat, and the fear of the windows made my chest go cold with hers. Two fingers to her mouth, undone, and the chapter ends on that. This is the hottest thing Randi has done in the book and it was fully clothed and three seconds long.

The lunch's money detail cut me. Vee reading the right side of the menu first — "she read the left side, the way a person reads who has never once had to find the bottom of a column" — and then paying anyway, tipping well, refusing to be the broke girl. I love her for it and it hurts.

The title, "How It's Done" — at first I took it as Pace's craft: the dress, the measuring, the man who does things all the way into them. By the end it's turned dark in my mouth. "It's just how it's done," Randi said, and that's the line the title hangs on — the phrase that opened Vee up. The book title still puzzles me a little — "A Polite Invitation" fits the Saturday text and the doors Pace leaves open, and the cover tagline wasn't given to me beyond the title line, so I'll say the volume title reads more ominous chapter by chapter.

I want the dress. I want to know what Randi is doing — whether the threesome plan is still live and whether Vee is being walked toward the bench by both of them now, one from each side. Next guess (from here only): Randi's "You don't have to tell me" was about her own appetite, not mercy, and the Mercedes was a claim, not a goodbye.
