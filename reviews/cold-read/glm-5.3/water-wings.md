# Cold read (grounded) — Water Wings

## Reader reaction

God, this one hit me somewhere the earlier chapters hadn't. After the bench, after the sorority gauntlet, after the long game with Randi pointing at girls — this chapter is a breath, and it's *Vee's* breath, and it's the first time the book has let me be entirely inside her without Pace in the room. I didn't realize how much I wanted that until I had it.

The felt thing first: the water. "The room switched off." That whole underwater passage made my chest ache. "She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem" — that line did something to me, because it re-colored everything I've watched Vee do since Chi Latte. The cardigan over the better shirt, the mother's voice calling the bar girls *floozies* while she looked a beat too long, the careful sizing-up of every room she enters — all of that is a girl managing a body that other people made into a problem, and the pool is the one place the problem dissolves. "Nobody up there could see her. Nobody down here wanted anything." And then she *grieves* a little, and I did too, because the book let me feel how much she's lost — synchronized swimming at sixteen, a body "when there'd been less of her to fill it," a whole uncomplicated belonging that got taken the moment she grew into the thing people look at.

So the title: *Water Wings.* I read it as the floats little kids wear — the help you need before you can swim — and it lands as irony, because Vee is the strongest swimmer in this book. The heckler's "you won't need floaties with *those*" makes the joke explicit and ugly, but the chapter takes the word back: what she actually needs isn't buoyancy, it's somewhere to be a body without being a display. The deep water is that. Maybe Cassie is too.

Cassie. My feeling about her has moved from mild affection to real love. "Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman" — delivered low, kept just for Vee, when she "could have pitched it up into the bleachers" — that's friendship as an act of shelter. And the water-gun trick from ten feet off, not moving a step closer, not asking: this is the same Cassie who said *goodbye* flat and finished and let it stand. She never pries, never soothes, just does the thing that reaches her friend without making the sadness bigger. "Asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger" — that's the most Cassie sentence in the book. And it finally explains the *goodbye* from the lecture hall chapter, retroactively: Cassie saw Randi pull Vee into the bright world and said the word for a longer parting, because she could see where it was going. I believe that even more now.

Which brings me to the unease I'm allowed to have: I keep noticing what the chapter does *not* contain. No Pace, no Randi, no phone. And I know — from my own reading, not from any peek — that Vee was pointed at. That the woman who walked into her on the lawn did it on purpose, as a game Pace was running with Randi, and that the sweetness Vee "had been chosen by" was a lure. Watching Vee find genuine peace in the one place untouched by all of them makes me protective in a way the book has earned line by line. The shriek-laugh at the end — "the ugly one" — is the freest she's been on the page, and I got a real flicker of dread under the warmth, because I like her too much to want what's coming. But that's a guess from here, held loosely.

Heat: none of it is erotic, and none of it needs to be — though the heckling scene is charged in its way, the exposure of the suit that "fit, and gave her nowhere to hide," shame arriving where a body is on display against her will. That's the same current as the Chi Latte cardigan moment, recurring — Vee managing being looked at. The walk "like an entrance" with her face put on is Randi's trick, borrowed; I noticed that too.

Friction: almost none. If anything, the chapter is shorter than I wanted it. Pace of the book overall: after the heavy machinery of the bench and the campus hunt, this small chapter feels like the book breathing out — and it's the first chapter where nothing is being *done to* anyone, which I noticed as relief.
