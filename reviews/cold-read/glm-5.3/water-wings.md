## Reader reaction

The felt read first.

This chapter is a breath held and let out, and I felt it in my own chest. After the bench, the sorority gauntlet, the pointing game, the wine-kissed *Tell* — all of it shot through with Pace's and Randi's separate machinations — the book hands me a whole chapter where nothing is being engineered by anyone, and it turns out that's the thing I didn't know I was starving for. Vee underwater, hanging in the green with nothing holding her and nothing to hold, is the first time this book has shown me one of its women simply *being*, and the grief that surfaces with her ("She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem") landed harder than anything since "There you are." I ached for her down there.

How I feel about everyone: Vee deepened enormously for me here. We've watched her from Randi's side — the lush body, the cardigan buttoned to the throat, the borrowed bit at the coffee shop — and this chapter gives me her interior, and it's a body-armor story. The synchronized swimming, the suit that "gave her nowhere to hide," the rolled towel carried like a baton because she knows it reads as confidence — *the walk of a girl who had meant to arrive*. And then the hecklers on the bleachers, and her managing them with a blink and an eyeroll, keeping the face on while walking the whole length of the gauntlet. That's Randi's trick, taught by the same world, and the rhyme quietly devastates me: these two women have the same scar tissue from different accidents.

Cassie — I love Cassie, and I loved her before, but here she's the best person in the book. "Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman" got the laugh out of me the same way it got Vee's. And the splash — the engineered little outrage, pitched at exactly the right distance, never moving a step closer, because "asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger" — that's the most precisely rendered friendship beat so far. Cassie is the one character who wants nothing *from* Vee, and the book knows it: "You can't grieve and fight your best friend for a mouthful of pool at the same time; the splashing won." I'm clutching that line.

Pace only exists here as an absence and a question, and the question is doing interesting work: "Don't answer tonight." Sitting on the bench in her towel winding a curl around her finger, wondering about his hands "besides math" — that's the hottest thing in the chapter and nobody touched anyone. He told her not to answer, and she's standing at a crosswalk with her thumb hanging over the keys while the light changes. The not-answering is working on her exactly the way the bench worked on Randi, and I noticed that parallel with a small chill. The wait itself is the instrument. Whether he knows that — whether he's doing it on purpose — the page keeps open, and I keep it open, but I felt the shape of it.

Which brings me to my one unease, and I want to be fair about it: the text has earned it. I watched him ask Randi "would you like to taste another woman?" mid-act to time her body's answer before her mouth could intervene. I watched him send Randi across a lawn like a released falcon. And now the same patience is being spent on Vee, a girl whose appetite already "got away from her" at the froyo counter, whose mother's voice lives in her head telling her *that's enough now*. There's a real question rising about whether Pace's attention is a gift or a harvest, and the book seems to know it's a question, which is the only reason I trust the book. The swoon and the suspicion are living in the same chapter for me, both honestly.

Friction: almost none. If anything, the locker-room towel beat telegraphs slightly — "which was really not the place" is a wink I could have done without — but it's a small thing and the walk to the crosswalk bought it back.

The title: "Water Wings" — floaties, from the heckler's taunt. It's a mean little line repurposed as tenderness, because the whole chapter is about Vee's relationship to being held up. She doesn't need the floaties; she sculls, she hangs weightless, the water is "the one part of it that had never been taken." The title recolors on second thought: the safety she can't accept on land she already knows how to make herself. Lovely. The volume title — *With a Long Spoon, Book One: A Polite Invitation* — still reads obliquely to me; "a long spoon" is the long-handled spoon for supping with the devil, and I hold that loosely, but the "polite invitation" is starting to look like Pace's whole method: *may I call you*, *don't answer tonight*.

Next: I want the Saturday date, and I dread it a little, because I know something Vee doesn't — that the man she's wound a curl over is the same man whose girlfriend picked her out on a quad and asked, quietly, whether she'd like the bench. That gap is where the whole book lives for me now. Guess, marked as a guess: the phone lights up before she reaches her dorm.
