## Reader reaction

**The felt read**

This chapter is a rest, and I sank into it gratefully. After the run of heat — the bet, the brunch, the Halloween hallway, the drawer — the book takes Vee to the one place it has kept sacred since chapter 7: the water. "The one part never taken," my memory calls it, and this chapter proves the phrase still true. Nobody sent her here. Randi didn't steer her to it, Pace isn't in it, nobody's watching. She takes lane three "not by any arrangement anybody had made," and that line landed on me hard — it's the only place in her whole current life about which that can honestly be said. Even her friendships, her dinners, her luck, were arrangements. This isn't. The book quietly knows it, and so does she, and neither says it out loud.

What moves me most is how the chapter frames the water as the anti-everything. Up on land her body "had opinions all day long every day of her life" — wanting, flushing, betraying her at brunch tables — but "down here" it "had none at all. It only worked." The pool is where her body stops being a text someone can read (Pace reading the wet, Randi reading her face) and becomes just an engine. After chapters of her body answering before her pride could, "her body, which had opinions... had none at all down here" read like relief I could feel in my own shoulders. And the callback to the hymn — "You were just already in it" — is lovely: the pool is her old liturgy, the one she was raised in before it got taken from her by curves and shame.

But the chapter isn't only rest — it's the answer she couldn't give Cassie, being worked through in the only medium that strips the words off. "Do you love him" gets in off the third wall "in Cassie's voice, flat," and it swims the set beside her, first as a question with a dread under it, then — as the work hardens — reduced to a pressure she carries rather than examines. The honest beat is the four strokes of doubt: "whether the man she was turning over was Pace or was somebody she had built out of wanting him." That landed harder than I expected, because *I* carry the answer she doesn't have. I know about the lawn and the pointing game and the reports in his bed. Her small doubt — is he that good, or did I make him that good? — is the truest thing she's sensed yet, and the swimming doesn't resolve it. It just files it under *a shape you could carry around all evening without it asking you for anything.* She doesn't answer the question. She gets strong enough to hold it. That felt exactly right for Vee, and a little heartbreaking for me, the only witness who knows the question is better than she thinks.

The "did he mind" thread — the drawer gave her the answer, the top drawer, his things pressed into one — but the water turns it to "nothing at all" within four pieces. Physical labor as the thing that makes scale corrections. I liked that the *drawer* worry dissolves first and the *love* worry lasts the whole set: she already knows how she's kept.

No heat here, obviously, and I didn't miss it — though I notice the book has now twice used water as the place her wanting can't follow (the rec-pool quiet with Cassie, and now this). There's a faint erotic ghost in the damp skin, the sweats, "arms feeling like they belonged to somebody generous" — generous is a word she's earned, and I felt the glow of it. And the chapter title, "Strokes": on the surface it's just swimming. But her whole courtship has been a count of strokes of another kind — the brush of a hand, a paddle, a swat, "his rough hand" — and "four strokes was all it got" makes the pun half-conscious. The title sits oblique for me, doing double duty quietly. It didn't give anything away; it withheld, which I appreciate.

Volume title: *With a Long Spoon · Book One — A Polite Invitation.* After this chapter the "polite invitation" reads more pointedly than ever — everything in this book is an invitation, "May I," the hand that rests and waits — and the long spoon (sup with the devil, needing a long one) sits there like a promise that the politeness is the danger. The cover-line promise of two people who "planned the best year of her life" still colors every quiet chapter like this one: even here, swimming alone, I couldn't fully forget that her happiness is somebody's project. That's the book's trick and it worked on me again.
