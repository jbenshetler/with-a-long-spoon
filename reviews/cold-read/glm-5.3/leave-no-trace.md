# Cold read (grounded) — Leave No Trace

## Reader reaction

The truck scene did me in. I have to say that first, before any of my wariness, because the page earned it: her kneeling up on the bench, rapping on the driver's window with her knuckles while he stands in the rain *on purpose*, giving her privacy he was never asked for — and then the crooked finger, *come in*, and the unbuttoning while she holds his eyes the whole way. That's the hottest thing in the book so far, and it's the hottest precisely because it's fully hers. The mother's voice arrives as heat instead of a wall — "the heat that knew the voice was right, that wanted to be the very thing the voice said" — and that inversion landed on me hard. She's not doing this despite the shame; she's doing it *with* the shame, wearing it as fuel. That's the interior life I read this book for, fully arrived.

And then the flannel. His only dry shirt, folded clean at the bottom of the gym bag under his briefs and his soap, and he hands it over and gets back out into the rain without a word. I melted. I've been half-trusting Pace this whole time — every "may I" real and every "may I" also a move — and this chapter moved me closer to him than any of the staged dinners did, because nothing about the flannel was tactical. He didn't know the rain was coming. He didn't know she'd take her shirt off. He just keeps a dry shirt in a truck for someone, and the someone turned out to be her. And the moment her arousal runs ahead into "the next thing, and the next thing was more" — and she finds his hand *shaking* — and her own want stops on a dime and becomes "You're freezing. Get warm. Please." That's tenderness I can trust, coming from *her*, and it recalibrated the whole chapter for me. She took care of him. She's not only the one being worked on.

The heat peaks at the window and the unbuttoning, and it goes tender at the hand against her belly, and I want to say plainly: both worked. The moleskin scene earlier is its quiet rehearsal — her stomach clenching at "what kind of girl lets a man," and the clench "let go without her catching it go." He fixes the boot and asks nothing, and the pattern of him never making her small keeps holding. The cherry pit is the same beat done smaller: she spits, he picks up her pit without looking at her doing it, and she "had it and he hadn't made her small to give it to her." I keep bracing for the moment his courtesy curdles into technique, and the page keeps refusing to hand it to me.

Where the chapter chilled me is where the chapter is *about* being chilled — the title. "Leave No Trace." On the surface it's the hiking ethic: the pits on the wax paper, "we take nothing and leave nothing." But the chapter ends with him driving off under police lights and her standing under the overhang *in his flannel*, and I couldn't help feeling the title turn over in my hands. The siren "took the rest of her sentence with it" — the big thing she was going to say, gone, interrupted. A trace left (the flannel, the moleskin still holding on her heel) and a trace not left (the words). I'm not sure the book means it darkly yet, and I won't force it — but the interruption felt deliberate, the world stepping in exactly where the feeling was about to become language. My guess from here: this book's pattern is that her choices are real and the situations aren't, and "leave no trace" is making me wonder who, eventually, will want this to have left no trace. Randi? The plan itself?

Because the plan is never named in this chapter — and that's the strangest thing. Randi appears only as a word in Vee's head ("*Ravish.* Randi at the white table") — the picture Randi hung up is literally the thing Vee climbs the mountain inside of. The seduction is now working through materials Randi supplied. And Pace built the sandwiches, the moleskin, the flannel — or did the weekend's script just happen to catch rain? I can't tell, and the not-telling is the book's whole engine. I noticed the dress beat, too — the russet silk she puts back on the shelf, and his "That sounds like a beautiful dress," weightless, and then he lets it lie. Under a colder read, that's a filed fact. Under the page's own light, it's the only kind thing he could do. I hold it ajar.

Small frictions: the summit vista is gorgeous but familiar in its moves ("it did not care that she was standing on the edge of it"), and the horseradish sandwich is doing a lot of the same sensory work the book has already taught me to expect — though "The hike had made her hungry first. That was the trick of it. That was what it was for" is a line I'd underline twice, because it's also the book confessing its own method. The cop arriving is almost too convenient as drama, but it spared me a goodbye speech I didn't need, so I forgave it.

Volume: *With A Long Spoon, Book One — A Polite Invitation*; cover tagline from my earlier packet memory of the jacket's promise — "how real it would get." "A Polite Invitation" reads truer every chapter: the flannel, the turned back, the held eyes. The invitation keeps being extended, and she keeps being the one to accept it. I dread and want the same next thing: the house, off six-fourteen, where the table with the bench under it waits. And I dread the reader-advantage meeting the page — the evening Vee learns the trail was scratched before she stepped on it.
