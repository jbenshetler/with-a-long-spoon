## Reader reaction

The bench chapter left me braced for whatever came next, and instead the book gave me this — a day of wind and horseradish and rain, and it might be the chapter that's undone me most so far.

I felt this one in my legs and my skin. The whole climb worked on me: the wrong boots, the "hot place" at her heel, "Push and lift. Push and lift. The blue shirt ahead." I could feel the burn. And Pace — I keep half-suspicious of him, the way he's always reading people, always three steps ahead — and this chapter mostly dissolved that. The blister scene is the most quietly intimate thing the book has done: he takes her sock off, the exact scenario her mother's voice has a verdict ready for, and he handles it like a bandage and nothing else. "The clench had let go without her catching it go" — that's the whole book's method in one line. He fixed the thing and she never had to be ashamed in front of him.

The summit undid me. "It was huge, and it did not care that she was standing on the edge of it… and that not-caring was somehow the most beautiful part of all of it." And him looking at her face instead of the valley and letting her catch him. I believe him there. I believed the flannel too — his only dry shirt, and then standing out in the rain so she could change. That's a man whose care is structural, not performed.

Then the truck. This is the hottest scene in the book for me so far, and nobody touches anybody. Her kneeling on the bench, crooking her finger at him through the glass, undoing the buttons while the voice keeps saying *three dates, your mother, floozie* — and the shame arriving as heat, her thighs pressing "once, hard, on their own." I've rarely felt a character's want and her policing of that want rendered so simultaneously. "The voice was the heat and the heat was the voice" — yes. Her wanting to be the very thing the voice forbids, with him watching. That's real to me, and it lands me right back in the territory the book has been circling: desire braided with the internalized mother. And the fourth-button beat — "She looked down anyway, and his eyes went with hers" — letting her own gaze drop so she can see herself as he sees her. God.

The interruption broke my heart a little. Her hand on his knuckles, and it's *shaking* — he's been shivering while she performed for him, and the discovery that his cold was real snaps the scene from heat into tenderness in one move. "She brought his hand in against her belly, under the flannel." That's the tenderness I trust. Then the cop's siren eats her sentence — the big thing she'll never get to say — and the taillights going down the road in the rain. That image is going to stay with me.

The Cassie ending: "Vee," said soft, and then "Keep the shirt." Cassie remains the book's cleanest soul. And I love Vee "reporting what she observed" about his back — she's learning her own desire in his language, cataloguing.

Suspicion check: honestly, none earned here. Pace's restraint read as genuine this time, not strategic — though I notice I keep saying that, and I know he and Randi have Vee on a menu. I hold that knowledge in one hand and the flannel in the other.

"Leave No Trace" — I read the title three ways: the cherry pits and the wax paper, Pace leaving no trace of himself (the birthmark, the pale line on his jaw that "nothing grew" along — something about him resists scrutiny), and her mother's rule: good girls leave no trace, no evidence. She left the biggest trace of her life — the shirt — and kept it. The volume title *A Polite Invitation* keeps gaining teeth; everything with Pace is invitation, never demand.

Next: I want the dinner, the induction, the black dress. I dread and want the two threads colliding — Randi's bench, Vee's flannel. Guess only: the dress conversation planted a seed that will not stay on the rock.
