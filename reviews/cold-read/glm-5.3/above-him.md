## Reader reaction

The drawer. Of course it's the drawer. I finished this chapter with my throat tight, and it wasn't the sex — the sex was lovely, warm, almost companionable, a week's wanting spent fast at the door and then unhurried on the couch with wine still in them — it was a man folding his entire ordered life down into one overfull drawer so that her hand reaches the easy one first. "There was no note. There was never a note." That's him, that's the whole book's grammar of care: he never explains, he just makes a place.

And the title. *Above Him* — I half-thought it would be about the heels again, or the bet, or some new escalation. Instead it's her, on her side, watching him sleep, literally above nothing — level with him, tucked along his side, whispering "Peter" to the shape of him. The chapter is her arriving at the word *love* in her own chest, unspoken, "where she couldn't take it back," and tallying all the ways he says it without saying: "the door, the spin, the slowness, the salmon, the warm tomatoes, the coming back to her." That inventory undid me a little. She's counting evidence, and the evidence is real — every item on her list is something I watched happen on the page. That's what keeps cutting me about Pace: the plan is real *and* the care is real, and this chapter gives her nothing false to love him with.

The counter scene held my breath. Her kit colonizing his one clean surface, his eyes drifting down to it in the mirror, her waiting — "Not the deep thing. Only the surface worry of a guest who's left a ring on good wood" — and then the next Friday's small catastrophe when the scrunchies vanish and she assumes eviction and opens the drawer to find a home made for her, with room to spare, "room for more of her, laid out like something prepared and waiting." Even knowing what I know about the plan — that he and Randi are building toward something she doesn't know about — the drawer is the most destabilizing thing in the chapter precisely because it's the tenderest. If this is the game, the game has walls made of this. I keep saying to myself: *he gave her the top drawer.* That's not an endgame move. That's a man settling in.

Vee herself: she's eating like a field hand, losing weight without asking to, keeping her bras looser as her secret. And she's stopped being hungry for the saying — "she could live clear inside the everything-he-did." That's either peace or a girl building a house on a floor she can't see under, and I honestly can't tell which, and I don't think the book wants me to yet. Her saying "Peter" into his ear while he sleeps — she keeps that name like a private treasure, and I remember she only got it a few chapters ago. She's whispering the one thing Randi's sisters never learned.

My wariness hasn't moved an inch, and neither has my swoon — they're still running on the same wire, exactly as before. Nobody did anything suspicious in this chapter. That's what's suspicious, if anything is: the friction is off. Even her body's shame, the mother's voice, barely flickers here — one flash at the counter ("had he minded?") and the deep shame stays quiet. It's the gentlest chapter I've read. Which makes me lean forward, not relax. Something this smooth usually precedes a turn, and the induction is behind us, the dress has done its work, the "endgame" the two of them designed is still unnamed ahead of her. Guess, marked as a guess: the drawer is the book's way of making whatever comes next hurt.
