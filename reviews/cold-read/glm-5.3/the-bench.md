## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

Wait — this is not the woman the jacket promised me. The blurb is all about Vee, the starving junior, the girl Randi steers toward Pace. But the woman in this chapter is *Randi* — "There you are, Randi" — and she's already deep in whatever this is, months or weeks in, a standing arrangement with a bag she keeps packed. So either the book started somewhere stranger than the beginning, or the jacket's game is already underneath this scene and I'm only now feeling it. Opening cold on chapter one with the lovers already established unsettled me pleasantly — I came in mid-current, like the water was already moving.

What landed hardest: the last third. Everything up to the orgasm was exquisite — the apple slices cut thin "because she would eat thin slices and would not eat thick ones," the slow undressing on his knees, the bench revealed "which she had seen without seeing" — but it was craft I could admire while being seduced. Then she starts crying without knowing it, and then the bathroom, and the chapter becomes something else entirely. Her mirror monologue is the best writing in the piece: "Nothing happened to her. That was what the face was for." The idea that the face is a decoy that absorbs all being-looked-at so nothing reaches her — and that Pace looked *past* it and that's precisely what she can't survive — that turned the heat inside out. The hottest thing in the chapter became the most dangerous thing, retroactively. "He could have the face. The face was very good. The one he had found was going back behind it, and she was not coming out again." I got chills.

My feelings: I trust Pace almost completely and that itself makes me nervous, because the blurb told me there's a plan. His consent care is rendered beautifully — "Red," twice, "Tell me anytime," the check-ins she gets exasperated by ("They're *fine*, Pace"). The Daphne paragraph deepened him enormously: he violated a door once and it cost him everything, so now he won't knock. That's earned character, not technique. But then there's his read on the evening — "she had finally let someone feed her... All he had done was find it, and answer it" — while she's in the bathroom deciding to never come out again. His interpretation and her reality have diverged completely and he doesn't know it. That gap is where my dread lives. And "he was aware... that he was still hard. *That's not what this night was for*" — I believed his restraint, and I also felt the cost of it, and I wonder what it buys him later.

Randi I feel tenderly toward and afraid for. The detail that wrecked me: the water bottle cap pressed "under her thumbnail... where it would hurt. She did not seem to know she was doing it." And the lock turning over — "not a thing she had ever done before in his house." The chapter's whole meaning is in that small click.

Friction: a few moments tipped toward the author showing me the machinery — the repeated insistence on how exactly he'd built the bench, "the precision of the thing he had made it to have," felt a touch admiring-of-itself. And the please-escalation scene is brilliant but nearly over-narrated ("She was about to know") — the paragraph explaining the waiter's-water *please* versus the real one told me twice what one telling would have landed harder. Minor. Mostly the prose earned its length.

The title, "The Bench," is plain and right — the object as the pivot, the thing that's a table again by morning. The volume title *A Polite Invitation* now reads darker than the blurb's warm pitch: the invitation went out before she knew there was one, and this chapter ends with her issuing her own refusal-of-an-invitation. The series title, *With a Long Spoon* — I don't know it yet; something about dining with the devil at a long handle. I'm guessing, from here.

I want to know where Vee is. The blurb promised me her, and the woman on the bench is her glamorous friend — so this chapter is either a prequel-positioned portrait of the trap, or the "best year of her life" already running and Vee just offstage. I dread Randi keeping the face on "not ever." I'll keep reading.
