## Reader reaction

The morning-after chapter, and it starts with the zipper. That half-drawn zipper in front of Cassie is the whole chapter in miniature: the skirt comes halfway down and Vee stops, because the next thing to hit the floor isn't a story she can edit — it's the fact of the traded panties, and there's no telling Cassie that. "I forgot my," she says, gesturing at nothing. I felt that in my stomach. The book keeps doing this to me: taking a small domestic gesture and making it carry the entire weight of what a woman will and won't let herself be seen doing. The ladle line — "the way a ladle takes soup from the pot and the surface closes without a hole" — is the cleanest image yet for Vee's whole method of surviving herself.

And then the counterweight: "She had never in her life been so happy." Standing alone in the cold room, out of the skirt at last, the night still running through her. I read that line twice. After chapters of shame fused to heat, the shame running alongside everything, here it just — isn't operating. The mother's word can come; "she found she didn't much mind it this morning." Something finished in her, or began, and I'm not sure which, and I don't think the book wants me to be sure.

The bistro scene with Randi undid me differently than I expected. I went in braced — after the lunch in "How It's Done," where Randi drew the confession out of her and then shut the door ("You don't have to tell me"), I was watching Randi's hands the whole time. And what I got instead was a Randi I can't get a fix on. On the page she is warm, funny, genuinely glad for Vee — "That's the best thing there is, being that gone on somebody" landed on me as real, and Vee's flash of fear at how far gone she is, and Randi catching it — that's the friend Vee actually needs, and I melted a little. The sad-boy story, the theatrical rue, "the way she made everything that had cost her into a story that had cost her nothing" — that's as much confession as Randi ever gives, and it aches.

But: "Something had come into Randi's voice... the esses running long, the a's lifting... her eyes had gone liquid at the edges." The accent thickening. The eyes going wet. One mimosa, mostly untouched, so it isn't that — and Vee *notices*, and finds no explanation, and lets it go. That's the most deliberate unsettlement the book has handed me, because Vee doesn't read it and I can't not. I know what I know from the early chapters — the pointing game, the wanting Randi keeps behind her face — and so the question I'm left holding is whether this lunch is friendship or feeding, Randi getting the middle of the story by inches, the toenails, the new panties, the color matched to his silk. Randi extracts the entire confession and calls it being mean because she loves you. I honestly can't tell if the warmth is the truth or the instrument. That's the book working on me exactly as it wants to.

The French kiss at the curb again — the second press, the hand at the jaw — and this time Vee doesn't scan the courtyard for witnesses. That progression frightened me more than the first one did. She's being acclimated, or she's just happy, or both.

Cassie calling him "your boyfriend," kindly, certainly, wrong — a small wrongness I savored. And the half-second that crossed her face when Vee came in, gone before it found a name: whatever Cassie knows or fears, she's keeping it too.

The title: "Fed." It names the whole motif — Pace feeds them, thin apple slices, the fra diavolo, the wine opened early so it would be ready when she was; Vee shows up famished and says the word before she can stop it. But the word now covers Randi too, feeding on the story, and Vee fed by being listened to. Everyone at this table is being fed what they starve for. The volume title — *With a Long Spoon · Book One — A Polite Invitation* — the long-spoon proverb (sup with the devil) feels more apt by the chapter, though I still hold it loosely.
