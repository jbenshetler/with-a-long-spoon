## Reader reaction

The bedroom scene undid me a little. "You like the taste of yourself" — and then the question, mid-act: "Would you like to taste another woman?" Her body answering before she could — that orgasm arriving "far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it" — is the hottest thing this book has done, and the truest. It's the bench chapter inverted: last chapter he broke the performance out of her through restraint and repetition; here a single sentence does it in one stroke, and the shock on her face is the same shock, the same *there you are*, in miniature. I melted at him kissing her "for no reason but that he wanted to," and at his discipline: "He did not take it for her answer." That line is why I trust him. He asks it again in the afterglow, deliberately, when she's free to choose — that's the consent I read this book for, and it's not window dressing, it's the engine.

And then her recovery. "Only in a threesome. With you." I actually laughed — it's so exactly her, and the narration names it without sneering: "a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy." He sees the machinery and finds it beautiful rather than contemptible. That's what keeps me on his side even as he steers; he's not lying to her, and he's not lying to himself.

The walk is where the chapter earned its title. The game is deliciously built — "I point. You pick" — and I felt the turn in *her* exactly when the page wanted me to: "She would have told him she was being a good sport about his game; she had stopped being a good sport a quarter mile back. She was shopping." Her verdicts are little character essays (the strong "no" at the girl who "ran the room" told me more about what Randi fears than anything in the bathroom mirror). And the beanie girl's "Maybe" — and his kiss, her own taste reaching her on his mouth in public — that's where the charge peaked for me outside the bedroom. Heat on a public quad with everyone dressed.

Vee's entrance is staged like a coronation, and it worked on me completely: the laughing with her whole body, "laughing like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen" — against a book that has been entirely about a woman who cannot forget she's seen for one second, that landed as meaning, not just description. Randi's bare "Yes" — "with nothing around it" — is her second *there you are* moment, and this time she's the one looking. I'm rooting for this. I'm also a little frightened, because I remember her locked bathroom, the hum "the size of a key turning in a lock," and her vow that the found one "was going back behind it." Wanting Vee is the thing she cannot hold and cannot put back. I don't know if this is her coming out or her finding a new mask — "the kitten finding her feet" is right there on the page — and I'm honestly leaving it ajar.

Friction, small: the narration sometimes explains Pace's reads a shade too confidently ("he had not known what the idea might stir in her" followed by a taxonomy of how other women would have answered). And "He kept his own face exactly where it was" while narrating his own fantasy about the two gym girls is doing the showing *and* the telling. Nothing I didn't buy, just moments I felt the author's hand steadying the frame. The title, "The Pointing Game," is plain and warm on reflection — the game is literally pointing, but the whole book has been Pace pointing at Randi, and now Randi pointing at Vee. Book one's title, *A Polite Invitation* — it keeps recoloring: the unlocked door, "come with me," "Let's go feed you." Everything is invitation; nothing is forced. That's the promise and, given Randi's interior, the dread too.

What I want next: Vee, from inside Vee. We know nothing of her but copper hair and a stats class, and she's about to be aimed at. My guess — only a guess — is that the bench question ("Do you think she'll like the bench?") is Randi trying to hand Vee the same fall she survived. I'd also love a crack in Pace's composure someday; he's been right about everything for two chapters and nobody in this book is right forever.
