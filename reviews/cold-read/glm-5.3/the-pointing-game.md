## Reader reaction

The Bench left me in a cold hallway — Randi behind the bathroom mirror deciding the found girl was never coming out again — and I came into this chapter braced for distance, for the mask holding. And then the chapter opened in bed and knocked me sideways with how much of her came out anyway, and how much Pace engineered exactly that. That's the thing about this book that keeps unsettling me: he's not forcing anything open. He's just very good at noticing what's already leaking.

The bedroom scene landed hot and strange at once. "Would you like to taste another woman?" — and she comes *from the question*, before she can think. My whole body went with hers there; the page made me feel the wanting arrive ahead of the person, which is exactly her tragedy and her heat in one. Then the recovery: "Only in a threesome. With you." I actually laughed, half in admiration. He reads it perfectly — she's moved the appetite onto him, made herself the generous instrument — and he lets her have the camouflage. But it stung a little too, because I remember the bathroom. This is the same woman who decided *not ever*, and here she is, within weeks, letting the want out sideways. I don't know yet whether that's hope or danger, and I don't think the book does either. That openness is what I'm here for.

The walk itself: the sun, the burning maple, the frisbee — it reads like the whole book breathing out. After two chapters of interiors (the house, the sorority hall, her cold room), being outdoors with her hand in his felt like relief. And then the game begins and the chapter quietly becomes one of the hottest things in the book without anyone touching anyone. Her scanning ahead of his pointing hand — "a follow gathering herself a half-beat before the lead moves" — that image went through me. She stops being a good sport and starts *shopping*, and he doesn't even have to push. The rejections are doing characterization work too: "too expected," the sealed pairs, "God, no" at the girl who runs the room. She's ruling out every polished mirror of herself. Of course she is.

And then Vee. The page spends a whole unhurried paragraph on her — the red hair sparking copper, the head thrown back laughing "like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen" — and I felt Randi stop before the text said she stopped. That line is the whole chapter, isn't it: everything on this quad is curated, including Randi, and Vee is the one body on campus that isn't apologizing or performing. The bare "Yes," alone, "with nothing around it." That gave me a real ache — because it's the first true syllable she's said in daylight since the bench, and it's about a woman.

The staged collision was fascinating and slightly chilling. I watched a decade of training "folded down into three seconds of execution so clean it looked like nothing" and thought: this is the sorority machine turned to a new purpose, and Pace standing at his light pole watching it happen went to "the root of him." I believe he cares about her realness. I also notice he never asks — he points, she picks, he orchestrates the daylight *and* the collision and then sends her in alone. The consent architecture is still scrupulous on the surface — "I'm not the one being asked," he says — but the whole apparatus is his. When she asks, low, "Do you think she'll like the bench?", and he answers with "Let's go feed you," I felt both things at once: tenderness (he heard it, and the food-and-apples thread is the gentlest running thing in the book) and a small cold flicker — she asked about the bench and he changed the subject. Is that care or steering? The page leaves it ajar and I'll leave it ajar.

Title: "The Pointing Game" — before reading I'd have guessed a parlor trick; now it names the entire mechanism, the whole chapter is the game, and "I point. You pick." is its rulebook. It doesn't give anything away; it only looks dangerous in retrospect. Book title, *A Polite Invitation* — after the collision-that-looked-like-chance, "polite invitation" reads almost ironic: nothing about this courtship will be asked outright; everything will be arranged to look like it happened to her.

Friction: very little. Maybe the omniscient narration got a little too pleased with itself in the bed scene — "So. There it was." — one beat of author's flourish I noticed. And I don't fully know how much time has passed since "Standards"; "four weeks in" suggests the whole affair is still young, which recalibrates the bathroom vow for me — vows made at week four are thinner than they feel. Next: I want Vee on the page, actually talking. I dread the moment Randi has to be seen by her. That's my guess, from here.
