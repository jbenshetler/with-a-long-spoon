# Cold read (grounded) — Two Towels

## Reader reaction

The house did something to me before anything happened in it. "The only closed door in the house" — I felt that line land in my stomach, and then she leaned toward it wearing satin she'd chosen that morning *for* that door, and he walked her past it, and something in her dropped and she didn't let her face do a thing. That's the hottest moment in the chapter and nobody touched anybody. It's also the most quietly devastating, because I know what's behind that door — the bench is behind that door — and she doesn't, and the satin she put on "hoping the man won't hand her a reason not to show it to him" is the closest thing to dramatic irony as arousal I've ever felt. She dressed for a room she doesn't know exists.

But let me be honest about the swoon first, because there's a lot of it. The dress. The whole dress sequence wrecked me — the way she'd "put a thing back on the shelf and keep walking" at the summit, and he took another cherry and said nothing, and then weeks later there's a bolt of burgundy on a table and he holds it up near her face "as you hold a paint chip to a wall" and she catches them both in the mirror. He didn't match the dress. He matched *her*. And her reflex-disappointment — *oh, it's not the right—* before she sees it — is such a true beat, the woman with the shade fixed in her head for weeks, that the turn hit me at full force. I was blinking along with her. "No one had ever—" with no end to the sentence is the chapter doing exactly what this book does to me over and over: handing Vee (and me) a tenderness so precisely aimed I stop keeping score of the aim.

Which is the problem, and I know it, and the chapter knows I know it. Everything in this house is a move. The kiss at the door — her taking it, finally, the mountain-kiss — and then *he* stops it, "the stopping was his," and she registers that no boy has ever once been the one to stop. That read two ways for me at once and both were true: it's genuinely him (Daphne, the closed doors, the patience), and it's also the most effective thing a man courting a woman toward a bench could possibly do. The heat in the house is turned up too warm and she files it under "small mysteries." The satin, the shut bedroom, the dress that requires being measured — "down to what you'd wear under it, in good light, so I can see how it'll fall." The invitation is a fitting. The wanting is being given a legitimate door into her being looked at, which the book has told me since chapter one is Pace's actual erotic language: looking as the act. Her own framing seals it: "He couldn't make it without seeing her. That was the only thing standing between her and it — her own willingness to be looked at. / Yes, she said." I got chills, and they were not entirely sweet ones.

And still — the stuff of the man keeps out-tenderizing my suspicion. The MRI patent lit up like a six-year-old with dinosaurs; the point of his invention is a scared kid out of the tube faster. The error-correcting codes explainer — "Say it more than once so the noise can't kill it" — gave me the same pleasurable shiver as Euler's substitution in ch 5. The book keeps doing this: handing me a piece of math that describes itself. A message built so that even when pieces drop, the rest still tells you what was there. That's this whole courtship — redundancy of care, the signal surviving noise. Whether he designed the redundancy or just *is* redundant, I genuinely can't tell anymore, and I've stopped being sure the distinction matters to how he loves. The *parents* question closing a door "two rooms away" — a real flicker of unexplained history, filed with "finishing, more or less." He stays a man with locked rooms of his own.

Randi isn't in this chapter at all and is somehow in all of it — the satin, the wanting to be seen, the being-chose — those were Randi's beats first. I notice the book gave Vee this chapter alone with Pace, and I felt her absence like a held breath.

Friction, briefly: the shirt reveal ("no tag in it") is lovely but the setup felt slightly load-bearing — "filed under some category that made no sense and stopped thinking about it" is the author nudging me toward the drawer. And Kayla's "there's no meal at two" from last chapter promised me the afternoon would be *about* something; instead this chapter is all tour and dress, which I loved, but I notice the invitation's ambiguity got paid off softer than Kayla and I expected. The closed bedroom was the payment, and it was enough.

The title: "Two Towels" — I assumed it meant something erotic by the end and it's the most mundane detail in the chapter, two towels on the rod in the bathroom. But it snagged me. A man who lives alone, in a house kept too warm for a visitor, with two towels. He expected her. Maybe he's always expected someone. It recolors backward the way the good shirt did — an ordinary object that was a preparation all along. The book's title, *With a Long Spoon* — "a long spoon sups with the devil," needing distance to deal with something dangerous — sits more uncomfortably with me each chapter, because the devil keeps being so *kind*. Book one, *A Polite Invitation* — the invitation has now been issued, and it's burgundy silk and a measuring tape, and it's the politest thing I've ever dreaded.

What I want next: the measuring, obviously — I both want it and brace for it, because it will be the first scene where her body is entirely his subject, lights up, since the truck, and I don't know if my reaction will be the swoon or the alarm and I suspect the chapter won't let me split them. What I dread: the moment she learns the collision was choreographed, and whether the dress — a real, cherished gift made with his hands — becomes something ruined by that knowledge. And I keep waiting for Randi's "not ever" to crack.
