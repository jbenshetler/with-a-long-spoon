## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

I came into this chapter keyed-up, because I know what lives in that house — the table that becomes a bench, the room she's never heard of — and the whole first half I was reading every object twice. The tour is done so beautifully and so terribly: the closet floor of paired shoes ("Depends how many things he does"), the worn KitchenAid, the office with the graph paper, the closed bedroom door he names with "no weight on it, nothing withheld and nothing offered." That door knocked something over in me. Vee dressed in satin under everything *planning* for that door, and he walked her past it, and "Something in her dropped" — and I dropped with her, even knowing that in this house the refusal is the courtesies and the bench is the destination. The tension I feel isn't "will something happen"; it's the horrible lovely drag of *knowing what the house is for* while she reads it as a good quiet man's orderly life. And there was the heat — kept at a low setting, with the kiss she takes on the stoop ("the kiss she'd been wanting since the mountain") and then his stopping it: "no boy she'd ever been with had once been the one to stop. That had always been her job, never theirs." That landed in my body as much as hers. It's the most seductive thing he's done yet, and it's a move in the game, and both are true, and I can't sort them and the book won't let me.

The dress undid me. I felt the small dropping disappointment — *oh, it's not the right red* — and then the mirror turn: "He hadn't matched the dress she'd described. He'd matched *her*." That's the swoon of the chapter and maybe of the book so far; her eyes going hot and the sentence she can't finish ("No one had ever—") got me honestly. And it's also the trap sharpened to its finest point: he listened to a thing she let go of at altitude and is handing it back made real. Her longing was gathered under conditions she doesn't know. "That's okay. You don't have to" — the way every burden is lifted off her just before she feels its weight. Even the patent beat works this way: the MRI story, "somebody sick, somebody scared, a kid who can't lie still," him going six years old in the middle of the air — I felt my trust in him *rise* even with everything I know. That's the book's clean cold trick working on me exactly as it works on her.

Friction, small: "I didn't realize you knew Sanskrit" is a lovely econ-girl line, and the "parents" door-easing-shut is a nice held-open beat (Daphne? money? something else — I hold it ajar). Nothing telegraphed clumsily; the heat-not-yet of the measuring-to-come ("down to what you'd wear under it, in good light, so I can see how it'll fall") is doing its work through implication, which I much prefer to a thumb on the scale. My dread and my pull are the same thing now: the measurement scene. Her willingness to be looked at is the only thing standing between her and the dress — and I suspect being looked at is the actual product here. And somewhere behind the silk, the bench is waiting. I noticed the towels on the rod — *two* towels, and I don't know whose the second one is, and I'm not sure I want to know how fast she'd have found the answer if she'd asked. That title is quiet and perfect: the ordinary domestic detail that a person with my knowledge can't stop reading as evidence.
