### What I know that they don't
- **Vee doesn't know there's a plan.** The collision with Randi was engineered (ch 3: Randi drifted into her, made the redhead apologize). The coffee-shop meeting with Pace was staged — he crossed the room to her already knowing who she was; his questions, his "may I," his forgotten coffee were all part of a seduction begun before she knew he existed. She believes she's been lucky twice.
- **Vee doesn't know Randi and Pace are a couple**, let alone that she is their shared project — that they walked the quad shopping for her, that Randi said "I already do" (like her), that Pace promised "You'll like her," and that Randi asked, on the lawn, "Do you think she'll like the bench?" — the bench Vee has never seen.
- **Vee doesn't know about the bench at all**, or that Pace's house (which she hasn't visited) holds it — a table that opens into something built for a facedown woman, with a face cradle and handholds, which he made and on which Randi begged and wept.
- **Vee doesn't know** that Randi's warmth, though real in the moment, has a director's hand behind it — Randi can aim a decade of training at a person in three seconds of execution so clean it looks like nothing.
- **Pace doesn't know** Randi locked the bathroom after the bench night, cried, and decided in front of the mirror that the one he found is going back behind the face — "not ever." He thinks it was "a remarkably good evening" and that she's resting against his shoulder in trust.
- **Vee doesn't know** Pace's history with Daphne, or why he's so exact about doors and choice.
- **Cassie doesn't know** she's the only person in Vee's life with no hidden agenda — the irony cuts the other way: her flat "Goodbye, Vee" read as wrong to Vee and was never explained (open).
- Caring side, kept honest: Pace's tenderness on the page is real — he waits, he gives back the pencil, he leaves the last inch; Randi's want for Vee ran ahead of her own script and had to be held down with a visible effort; the blurb itself insists "Nothing was taken from her that she did not hand over." The trap, if it is one, is being built out of true material.

### Symbolism
- **Euler's formula (the crossed mark)** practically begs to be read as the book's own architecture: two things that separately won't give, put together at right angles, becoming "a single thing that's suddenly plain — easy to name, easy to know what to do with." The tutor's path and the pointing game are the same gesture twice: hand her the pieces, let her assemble the answer so the solving stays hers.
- **The bench hidden under a table top** — the whole courtship is a polite surface over an exact purpose; the invitation that reached her "long before she knew there was one."
- **The quad composed by men "who wanted the eye to travel exactly this way"** — the campus itself as a designed field of gaze, the book's theme made scenery.
- **Vee underwater** — the one place her body "had never been taken"; her desire and grief both surface from the green.
- **The tea in brass cages** — handles so you can hold what's too hot to hold: Vee's whole situation in an object.

### Story so far
**Ch 1 — the bench night.** Pace prepares his small, very warm house: a table whose top lifts off to reveal a purpose-built bench. Randi (secret girlfriend, sorority girl, glossy surface over a starved self) arrives after lying to her chapter about a paper. He slices an apple thin for her; she straddles him at the kitchen table; he reveals the bench. She reads it, unsettled and intrigued: "Show me." He undresses her slowly, kneels, folds each garment; she stands and lets herself be looked at as she never has been. On the bench — facedown, handholds, open middle, safeword "Red" — he spanks her with building patience, edges her repeatedly, and refuses her polite, performed *please* until the real one arrives, "from below," out of the apparatus of performance. She comes with a full-body shudder she couldn't have produced, and weeps without knowing it. He holds her, wraps her in her own cashmere, leaves water in advance. When he says "You're beautiful" she flinches; she goes to the bathroom and locks the door for the first time ever. In the mirror she confronts what happened: nothing ever reached past the face before; he found the one behind it; she decides — "not ever" — to give him only the face. She comes out smiling, the smile lying "as it had for years." He, unaware, thinks it was a remarkable evening; he stayed hard throughout and set it aside — "That's not what this night was for." She stays.

**Ch 2 — the morning after.** She slips into the sorority house in last night's clothes and is intercepted by Brooke in a smiling, seated, four-minute interrogation: the paper, the place on Wilson, the name "Pace" (not Chase), the white pickup, his math PhD, "not his section." Randi's cover mostly holds. Upstairs in her single (perk of Standards), she lies down fully dressed under the duvet and does not cry — the no-door not-crying of twenty-one years of practice — with "He's hard to say no to" replaying in a key she can't sing in.

**Ch 3 — the pointing game.** Another day at Pace's: she asks for "something different." In bed he asks "Would you like to taste another woman?" and her body answers before she can — she comes instantly. Afterward he asks it again gently; she flinches, then recovers into the safe costume: "Only in a threesome. With you" — handing the appetite to him as a gift. He accepts. On a golden campus walk he initiates the game: he points at women, she picks. She rejects the expected type, the matched pairs, the room-runners; a round-faced girl on the library steps gets a "maybe"; then out of the dining hall comes the one — tall, lush, laughing with her whole body, dark-red hair, freckles — and Randi stops mid-stride and says "Yes," bare, before costuming it. Pace: "Go meet her." Randi executes a flawless accidental collision that makes the redhead apologize; two minutes of names and warmth; the girls' name is Vee, and they share a stats class. Randi comes back lit up, kisses him in both registers, and asks, quietly: "Do you think she'll like the bench?"

**Ch 4 — Vee's side begins.** Wednesday stats lecture. Vee has spent a day running Randi's "we should sit together" both ways. Randi arrives glowing, sits beside her (Cassie on Vee's other side), charms, teases; Cassie answers her name-game bit with one flat word. Cassie declines coffee and leaves with the odd "Goodbye, Vee." At Chi Latte (the joke Vee finally gets to be inside of), Randi orders for her, takes her to the high table in the window; Vee watches the room's performing girls with a mixture of her mother's voice ("floozies") and a longer, unauthorized look of her own; Randi alone is not performing, and Vee's flat helpless want settles in her chest. The hour is the best in months; Vee can't retain a single word Randi said, only the fact of being chosen. "Love you, girl. See you later."

**Ch 5 — substitution.** A study session at the place on Wilson (also Randi's alibi to Brooke). Vee is stuck on a challenge problem; Cassie flags "that cute guy in line" looking at her; Pace crosses the room and offers help. He reads the problem, asks what her professor said, marks Euler's formula in the margin, hands the pencil back exactly where she can finish it herself — the tutor's path, not the mathematician's. Vee borrows Randi's name-bit ("V.T., like the university") and it lands. He answers Randi's stats question well but briefly (Cassie, dry: he looked at Randi "the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt"); he listens to Vee talk econ for five full minutes without touching his phone. He is a math PhD, "more or less" finishing. "May I call you?" — the *may* lands in her body. He leaves without his coffee. Cassie and Randi both read it as a man who wants her.

**Ch 6 — the long way.** Twenty minutes later he texts about frozen yogurt. Randi overrides the waiting rule ("The rule's for boys you're not sure about. You're not unsure"). Froyo: his layering system, her cup built past all stopping points with no voice arriving to stop her, his paying before she finds her wallet. Walking, he listens like her grandfather used to; she says the froyo is the nicest anyone's been to her in a long time and asks him to pretend she didn't; he doesn't pretend — "Your tart's holding up." It is.

**Ch 7 — tannin.** That same evening Randi comes straight to Pace's, locks the deadbolt, takes him on the couch and demands "Tell me." He reports the day in deliberate fragments (she built a cup "like nobody had ever told her when to stop"; "She's easy to listen to") while withholding the froyo-walk confession Vee made — "It's hers." Randi's want, held off her face, moves him; "I already do," she says, bright, and finishes his wine herself, mouth to mouth. He drank none; he could taste nothing else.

**Ch 8 — water wings.** Friday afternoon, the rec pool with Cassie. Vee's green-and-white synchro suit fits and gives her nowhere to hide; the bleachers heckle and she absorbs it with a long blink; Cassie keeps her dry pitch just for the two of them ("Tossers. They will never know the love of a woman"). Cassie plants herself ribs-deep; Vee crosses the dark stripe, goes under, and finds the green where nobody wants anything and the body isn't a problem — she grieves a little down there — then surfaces, and Cassie's deliberately-missable splash breaks it all open into the shriek-laugh fight, "back before any of it."

**Ch 9 — may I choose.** Friday evening his text arrives (Saturday, dinner, no pressure to answer tonight — the built-in quiet eases her). Cassie: "That's not a boy. That's a man." Saturday: he comes to her dorm lobby showered, shaved, in kept real clothes, not bought ones. A restaurant she'd never found in two years; Christine knows him. "May I choose?" — she hands it over with relief. He orders in soft rounded words she can't read, asks only if there's anything she won't eat. She is enormously hungry, eats without watching herself for maybe the first time ever, stops doing the work of the table, lets him carry the evening; the mint tea surfaces her, late and soft. His right hand, always the right hand. Outside he takes her hand — the first time, too much for what it is, "like something resolving." Under the dropped tree-shadow her old reflex produces the rule — "I don't kiss a man I've known a couple of days" — he simply lets it close and starts walking. Her feet don't go. "But I'm making an exception," and she kisses him; he answers only as much as she brings, leaves the last inch, and she takes it. The real kiss, with mint in both mouths, his hand to her face, her grin inside it. He asks her hiking; she said yes before it finished landing.

**Ch 10 — off six-fourteen.** Back at the dorm, the report to Cassie: the tea, the tree, "I made *him* stop. And I kissed him. Me." — "Nobody did anything to me. I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose" (the sentence the whole reader-advantage turns on). She spins, lands in the bean bag. Hiking west of town, off six-fourteen past the gravel; he lives out there; she hasn't seen the house; real shoes to borrow. Cassie, one last dry floor under the gladness: "Borrow good ones."

### Impression
I'm falling for it exactly as Vee is, and I know I'm being worked — which is the book's clean, cold trick. Pace I trust in the particulars and not at all in the architecture: every "may I" is real, and every one of them is also a move in a game whose rules Vee was never shown; his tenderness reads genuine, and that makes the design colder, not warmer. Randi I feel tender toward and wary of at once — the mirror scene is the most naked thing in the book so far, a girl deciding to go back inside a face forever, and then turning that same trained face, without missing, on a girl who only wanted to be chosen. My strongest steady warmth is for Cassie — the one person whose flatness is just flatness, who kept the joke low so only Vee would hear it — and her "Goodbye, Vee" unsettles me more than the bench does. Vee herself I like enormously: the hunger, the borrowed poise she tries on and hands back, the kiss she took on purpose. The trouble is that "on purpose" is only half true so far — the choosing was hers, the situation wasn't — and I'm braced for the evening the two truths meet.
