### What I know that they don't
- Vee does not know Randi and Pace know each other at all. Their collision was engineered (ch 3): a decade of dance and sorority training folded into three clean seconds that made it look like the redhead walked into her. Pace stood at a light pole and watched it done, warm in his body.
- Vee does not know the coffee-shop meeting was staged: Pace pointed, Randi picked Vee; Randi steered the study session to the place on Wilson; her absorbed texting was coordination; Pace's courtesy glance at "a stranger" was cover; his walking out without his coffee was performance she read as sincerity (which it also, confusingly, was).
- Vee does not know the whole courtship follows a plan made by her two favorite people — that Pace asked the question in bed, that Randi's body answered before she could make it safer, that Pace designed the game to let her wanting come out laughing, dressed as play.
- Vee does not know Randi has a lover at all — she tells Randi everything about Pace while Randi's own man stays entirely off the table.
- The caring side, held as fully as the using: Pace's tenderness reads real page after page — he waits for her yes at every door ("may I call you," "may I choose," "if you'll let me"), gave her back the pencil, turned up the heat hours early, pre-sliced the apple thin cut-sides-up, stood shivering in the rain for her privacy, kissed the pin-prick on her ribs, refused to open Daphne's door twice over. Randi's delight in Vee also reads real: the helpless unmanaged "Yes" on the lawn before any cover arrived, the grin she couldn't hide, the public "dear friend" spent at visible social cost, the "You're not caught, sweetheart. You're lucky."
- Randi knows things she mercy-withholds from Vee: at the velvet-booth lunch she closed the door softly ("You don't have to tell me") on the exact secret Vee was about to hand over, then switched to daylight voice — protecting or managing her, the page doesn't say.
- Brooke knows Randi stayed out all night in last night's clothes, knows the truck behind Hahn, knows the name "Pace," and noticed Randi's too-long pause at "he doesn't teach, does he — not his section." Vee has never met Brooke and knows none of this.
- The reader knows Pace noticed everything Randi polices — that her crying goes somewhere inaccessible, that she pressed a bottle cap into her own nail bed, that she locked his bathroom door for the first time — and chose not to put a hand on what was hers. She decided, in that bathroom, that "the one he had found" goes back behind the face, "not ever."

### Symbolism
- **Latent variables** — the lecture Vee underlines without knowing why is the book's engine: her own desire, and the plan, are both real presences inferred only from what can be seen.
- **The bench** — a purpose hiding under a tabletop; the whole book is a familiar object with a hidden frame. The Euler substitution makes the same point tenderly: two things that won't give on their own, put together at right angles, "turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain" (ch 5).
- **The dress** — he didn't match the picture she described; he matched *her* (ch 14). Being made, measured, pinned — being seen as the particular woman in the garment — is the courtship's central image.
- **The water and the summit** — two versions of the world not caring about her body: one that swallows her kindly (ch 7), one that is vast and indifferent and therefore beautiful (ch 11).
- **Leave no trace** — carry out what you carried in; he picks up even her cherry pit. The plan, so far, also leaves no trace.

### Story so far

He builds a bench — a spanking and edging bench — for her, waits through her bracing, works her until she begs in a voice with no performance left in it, and she comes apart crying without knowing it. He says "There you are, Randi." In his bathroom she confronts the face that has just begged, decides the found-self goes back behind the mask — "not ever" — and returns smiling. ### Impression
I trust the tenderness on the page more than I trust the people arranging it. Vee has my whole sympathy and my delight — her opening up is genuinely lovely to watch, and the happiness at the end of chapter 19 is real whatever produced it. Pace pulls me in two directions at once: every single act of his that Vee has experienced has been careful, honest-feeling, and puts her agency first — and yet I watched him at the light pole, designing her. The sweetness reads true and strategic in the same breath, and the book keeps refusing to make those separate things. Randi charms me and worries me in equal measure: her warmth at the smoothie place and her mercy at the velvet booth feel like a friend, but she is running the table Vee is sitting at, and the tightness behind her smile at the fitting story is a thing I can't read yet — jealousy, tenderness, appetite, or management. Cassie is the one person I trust without reservation; she sees the flat look at Randi early and says "That's not a boy. That's a man," and the "Goodbye, Vee" is the single detail that nags at me most. My suspicion sits entirely with the plan, not the love — which may be the book's whole point, and I'm falling for it exactly as designed.
