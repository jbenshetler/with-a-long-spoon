## Reader reaction

This chapter undid me a little. After three chapters inside the women's heads — Vee keeping her sleepless vigil, Randi building her fairytale out of borrowed warmth — going back to Pace felt like being handed the missing piece of a lock. And the piece is: he knows. He has known all along. He watched the answer reach her and fail on the porch — "He had watched the answer reach her and fail" — and he chose the smaller truth *on purpose*, and he can name exactly why: "He had hurt her because he was afraid of how much she could hurt him." That single line reframes everything I thought about his silence. It was never blankness. It was armor, and he built it after Daphne: say the word, lose the person; keep the word, keep the loss survivable. "He had taught himself that the words were the difference." That's the most honest thing this book has given me about him, and the cruelest: he's rationing love like a man who has been starved before.

The Sheri scene is the best thing in the chapter, warm and funny and brutal in the way only a real friend is. "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her" — she just lays it flat, no softening, and she's right, and he knows she's right, and the fact that he *goes home and drinks for the first time alone in his life* is the proof of how deep it went. I loved the texture of the diner — the amber tabletop Elvis, the double and the single swapped by a kid too new to know them — and I loved that the chapter opens with Pace deciding *before the door swings shut* that he won't bring her down. He's protecting Sheri from his weather the way he protects everyone. "He'd come in tonight needing it more than he'd have said" — Sheri is the one person who doesn't want anything off him, and that's why he can sit there.

The confession to Sheri also lands two things I'd half-suspected and now have confirmed: Randi hasn't come to him in six weeks. "Six weeks of that told him what the morning had not." So her vanishing after finding the green panties — that's her retreat, and he's reading it correctly and letting her have it, the same terrible discipline he gives everyone: the choosing is hers. And he's still carrying the threesome picture — the imagined morning-after kitchen scene with Vee in his sheet, Randi's feet up on the chair — "adding to it for weeks." That picture is starting to feel less like a plan and more like a man rehearsing a grief, and I can't decide which.

The hangover morning is where the chapter cut deepest. Prison food — "everything a body could need and nothing his mouth wanted" — is exactly the book's whole language of feeding inverted: the man who feeds everyone can't feed himself. And then the truck on the highway. His body gathering toward the cut in the hill before his mind catches up, the house "giving him Vee again," and then it passes and he sobbed, bent over the table, and I believed every second of it because the chapter earned it. "He ate anyway" — the last line — is him doing for himself what he does for others: command the body through. It's not comfort. It's discipline as survival.

Two things unsettle me, honestly earned. One: his accounting of why he can't say it is *rational*. The Daphne logic — words as the difference between survivable and unsurvivable loss — is a child's superstition grown into a man's rule, and the chapter shows him knowing it's false ("*Whiskey River* was a liar... left every memory of Vee intact") while still obeying it. That's not a plot device to me; that's character. Two: Sheri's "She's told you what you have to do. So say it." Vee has effectively said it, Sheri has said it, and the chapter ends with him *still not saying it* — eating instead. The door is still open, but I don't know if he'll walk through it.
