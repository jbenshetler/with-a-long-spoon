# Cold read (grounded) — The Usual

*scene: scenes/the-usual.md · model: glm-5.3 · memory: ck-ch040 + raw ch041..ch048 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

**The felt read**

A whole chapter inside Pace's head, and I've been waiting for this without knowing I was. For forty-odd chapters I've only ever seen him from outside — through Vee's eyes, through Randi's, through the things his hands do — and I'd built my own theory of him out of that evidence: patient, deliberate, possibly cold at the center. This chapter doesn't dissolve the unease, but it does something stranger. It makes him *lonely*.

The opening line stopped me cold: "he would not bring her down today. Whatever he'd carried in from the truck could keep." He's carrying something — something bad enough that Sheri asks "How are things, *though*" and he waits her out — and we never learn what it is. That's the first real ache of the chapter. He has "long practice at deciding a thing could keep." A man who shelves his own troubles as a discipline. It rhymes with everything I know about him — the scar he wouldn't explain, the parents he doesn't go back to — and it makes me feel for him even as it confirms he's a compartmentalizer of the first order. A man this practiced at not-bringing-things-down is also a man practiced at not-bringing-things-*up*. Like, say, declarations.

And that's the heart of the chapter: **"I can't."** When Vee asked *what am I to you*, I read his silence as withholding, maybe cruelty, maybe the machinery protecting itself. Now I've been inside it, and it isn't calculation — it's a wall he can't get over even when he wants to. He laid it out brick on brick to Sheri and *heard himself doing it* — "every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something" — and Sheri, glorious Sheri, called it exactly: "that's a hell of a lot of reasons for a man who's already got the girl. She didn't ask you for reasons." That's the truest sentence anyone has said in this book. Vee didn't ask for a case. She asked for the word.

Sheri herself is a gift. I trusted her from the checkpoint — the woman who sat with Vee all night after the dance fight — and here she's even better: "she didn't want him, she wasn't impressed by him... she just liked him and gave him hell." The one person in his life with no hook in him, and the only one who can say "from this seat, that feels pretty shitty" and have it land as love. Her Paige story hurt — the girl with the bird prints, the big laugh, the family of seven hollering, "lasted a couple months" — and the irony she names and he won't touch (grief isn't a choice) tells me what's under her: she's lost her people, whatever that means, and Christmas is the bruise. The Elvis song playing itself while they say nothing over it — that's the chapter's quiet craft.

Where it pulls at me: I wanted to climb into that booth. The amber tabletop, the fogged glass keeping the cold where it belonged, the fries stolen without asking. And Pace laughing — "the first full one of the night" — at *her*, not performing warmth, receiving it. When he says "the air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles," I believed him. That's not a man running a con; that's a man in love who cannot say the word, and the distance between those two things is the whole tragedy right now.

But — and I hold this with an open hand — the reader in me who knows about Randi notices what *isn't* in the booth. Sheri asks straight out: "She find out about the brunette, then?" and he says "No. Not like that." He doesn't say *there's nothing to find out.* The affair with Randi continues, unmentioned, unrenounced, while he tells Sheri "I want Vee. I miss her" — all true, all incomplete. And "not like that" is doing quiet work in the sentence. What's it like, then? I don't know, and he isn't telling Sheri either. My suspicion stands, but it's changed shape: less a schemer's mask, more a man who keeps two honest rooms and never lets them see each other.

Also — "he thought, before he could stop it, that Vee would either love this place or never see it at all, and he wanted very badly to know which." He's homesick for her in a diner. That landed warm.

Friction: almost none. If anything, I wanted one more beat on what he "carried in from the truck." And the Sheri-riffing-about-Vee's-body stretch ran a little long for me — funny, in character, but I felt the chapter lingering where it had already made its point. Minor.

The title, "The Usual" — the waitress who doesn't ask, the plates that come as arithmetic: Pace's whole life as seen from this diner is a thing ordered and received without declaration, which is precisely the thing Vee is starving for. He can have "the usual" with Sheri because nothing is at stake in it. With Vee there is no usual until he says the word. The volume title, *With a Long Spoon, Book One — A Polite Invitation*, still sits obliquely for me — long spoon, keeping distance while supping — and this chapter is nothing if not a man managing distance.

What I want next: the reunion. The term has started, Randi's back, Vee is reaching with no answer coming. After hearing "I can't" from inside his own head, I want to see what he does when she's in front of him — whether he finds a way around the wall or builds it higher. And I dread it, because I like him more now, and liking him more makes the Randi-shaped omission heavier.
