## Reader reaction

I'm sitting with this chapter the way you sit with a bruise you keep pressing — not because it hurts exactly, but because you need to know the edges of it.

**How I feel about each character right now.**

Randi. I am wrecked for her. The previous chapter ended with her choosing the face — I watched her choose it in the bathroom mirror, watched the smile settle, watched her walk back out and perform. I thought I understood what that meant. This chapter shows me I didn't, because I didn't understand the *habitat*. I understood the face as a decision made in a bathroom. I did not understand it as an ecosystem, a language she speaks fluently in a room full of women who are also speaking it, where the fluency is the point and the meaning underneath is the thing that must never surface.

Brooke. I am more unsettled by Brooke than I expected to be. She's *warm*. That's what undoes me. If she were cold, if she were a villain, this would be easier to process. But she's genuinely friendly. She's doing her job. She's the chapter president maintaining the social fabric, and the questions are soft, and the release is clean, and the whole thing is still a kind of exquisite pressure chamber. "I was about to be scandalized" — the relief she exhales is *real* relief. She's not performing concern. She's actually relieved. And that relief is the bar Randi has to clear every single time she walks through that door. The fact that Brooke is nice makes it worse, not better, because there's no one to be angry at.

Sloane and Kinsey I barely know yet, but Kinsey's "short-cute" made me flinch. The casual taxonomy. The way these women sort men into categories that can be *placed* in a social landscape, and Pace doesn't fit any of the categories, and Randi's laugh matched Kinsey's pitch before she could choose it — that's the detail that actually got me. The automaticity. The body performing belonging faster than the mind can decide whether it belongs.

Pace is absent from this chapter entirely, and his absence is the loudest thing in it. The duvet that is "not — it was a duvet." That dash. That almost-naming. She doesn't let herself say *it's not his blanket*. She doesn't let herself say *it's not the cashmere one I brought to his house because I get cold after*. The duvet is just a duvet. It is adequate. She lies down on her back in last night's clothes with her shoes still on and pulls it over herself and it does nothing.

**Swoon / pull.**

The swoon here is retrospective and aching. It's the echo of last night pressing against the present. When she hears "he's hard to say no to" in a different key — not the key she sang it in when the girls asked, but the key it actually lives in — I felt that land in my chest. The line she'd said casually, performatively, to explain why she was dating him, and now hearing it back from herself as truth. She doesn't let the thought continue. The thought stops where the words stop. And I wanted to reach into the page and say *continue it, you can continue it, you know what it means*. But she can't. Not here. Not in this room with this duvet and these shoes still on.

The moment that pulled me hardest was the closet. She opens it, stands before it, hand partway lifted toward the row — and can't locate what she came to do. She closes it. That's the whole chapter in miniature. She has come to her own life to do something and cannot find what it is. The closet is orderly. Everything earns its hanger. The palette runs bone to camel to oatmeal with one disciplined oxblood. It is a closet that belongs to a woman who knows exactly who she is. She closes it.

**Trust vs. suspicion.**

I have no suspicion of anyone. That's what's remarkable about this chapter. Brooke is not sinister. Sloane's laugh "that meant nothing on its own and was therefore available for anything" is a social tool, not a weapon. Kinsey is earnest and slightly too eager. None of these women are trying to hurt Randi. The system is not malicious. It's just — total. It's the water she swims in, and the water is warm, and the water is the thing she cannot breathe outside of, and I don't suspect anyone of anything except being exactly who they are.

If I have a suspicion, it's directed inward at Randi herself — not suspicion exactly but dread. I dread what the not-crying is doing to her. "The way a held breath stops feeling held. The breath was held." That's not a metaphor I can let go of. She's been holding her breath for so long it feels like breathing. And last night she exhaled. And now she's holding it again, and the holding doesn't register as holding because it never has.

**Erotic charge.**

The charge is all negative space. It's the body still carrying last night — the cardigan slightly wrinkled at the hem, the silk top showing briefly at the second step. It's the duvet that isn't his blanket. It's the shoes still on, the body not yet released from the night before, not yet showered back into the person who walks through common rooms. The wanting is there as absence, as the shape of what's missing. It's a 1 — simmer at the edges, the ache of a body that was opened and has now been closed again.

**Friction as a reader.**

None. This chapter is doing exactly what it's doing and I don't want it to be different. If I had to name a single moment of slight resistance, it might be the line "a woman who would one day be some CEO's third wife" — it's a judgment on Brooke that feels slightly too knowing, slightly too authorial. But even that works, because it's Randi's register, the way she categorizes the women around her, the same taxonomy they apply to her. She's doing to Brooke what Brooke is doing to Pace. Everyone in this house is sorting everyone else.

**The titles.**

"Standards" — I thought, when I saw it, it might refer to the bench, to the standards of craftsmanship, to Pace's standards. It doesn't. It's the Standards board. It's Randi's seat. It's the thing she does in this house, the role she holds, the function she performs. She is the one who maintains standards. She is the one who keeps the line. And the chapter is about what it costs to be that person when you've just been somewhere the line doesn't exist.

The volume title, *A Polite Invitation*, reads differently now. Last chapter I might have read it as Pace inviting Randi into something. Now I read it as the house inviting Randi back into its terms — the polite, warm, inescapable invitation to be who she is here. Brooke's "go" is an invitation. The common room is an invitation. The closet is an invitation. Everything in this house is a polite invitation to close the door you just opened.

**What I want / expect / dread next.**

I dread that she'll succeed. That the face will hold. That the hum will quiet and she'll come back to Pace's house next Friday and be the woman who straddled him in the kitchen and ate the apple slices and never once let the begging voice surface again. I dread that because it would be believable. It would be the realistic outcome. She's twenty-one and she's been doing this for eight years and the machinery is *good*.

I want her to fail at it. I want the hum to get louder. I want the duvet to not be enough. I want her to drive back to his house on a Tuesday with no excuse and no paper due and stand at his door with the overnight bag and not be able to say why she's there.

But I don't expect that. Not yet. I expect the face to hold for a while. I expect the held breath to continue. And I expect this book to be patient about it, the way Pace was patient on the bench. Waiting. Not forcing. Letting her come back when she's ready.
