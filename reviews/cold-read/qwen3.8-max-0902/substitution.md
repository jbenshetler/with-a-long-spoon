**Felt Read**

I came into this chapter carrying the weight of "Standards" — Randi alone in her room, not-crying, the duvet pulled up, the breath held so long it stopped registering as held. And then "See You Later" gave me Vee in full sunlight, swept up, spinning a little on the sidewalk with an empty cup. So I arrived here warm toward Vee and uneasy about Randi, and the chapter opened with Vee stuck and frustrated and I settled right into her.

The math problem is doing so much work and it's doing it without feeling like a metaphor. "Every road she took curved back and set her down exactly where she had started, the same problem in front of her, untouched, as if she had walked a wide careful circle in the dark and arrived at her own back." That's Vee. That's also the book. I felt her frustration physically — the pencil she'd break if she kept holding it, the curl at the corner of the page.

And then Pace walks in.

I knew he was coming. I knew who he was. And yet the chapter still got me, because it's told from Vee's eyes and Vee doesn't know, and her not-knowing made me feel the approach fresh. "A pleasant, ordinary face, light brown hair cut short and easy, not one feature in it that asked for a second look. No individual part of him was particularly impressive but the whole of him was, and she could not say how." Yes. That's exactly how he reads from the outside — the sum exceeding the parts, the economy of movement, the way the room settles around him. I've been inside his head for two chapters and I still felt the pull of him arriving from this angle.

The forearms. "They were not a math tutor's forearms." I laughed. And then: "She kept reaching for the shape under him and it would not come. He didn't fit anything she had. That interested her a good deal more than it should have." This is Vee's version of what Randi's been doing since the first chapter — trying to place him, finding that he doesn't slot, being drawn to the not-slotting. But Vee's version is warmer, less proprietary. She's not assessing cost. She's curious.

The tutoring scene is genuinely lovely. What landed hardest was the pencil. "He had given her back the pencil at the exact line where she could solve it herself." And then the long paragraph where she realizes what he's done — "The path he picked was the path of a person who wasn't performing — not showing off, not hiding it either — who had kept himself out of it so the solving stayed hers." I felt that as tenderness. Not romantic tenderness exactly, but the tenderness of being handled well by a person who could have handled you roughly and chose not to. It made me trust him more than I already did.

And the Euler explanation: "On their own these two are a struggle — you push and they won't give, and neither one quite looks like what it is. Put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain — easy to name, easy to know what to do with." I felt that land as more than math. I felt it land as the book's thesis about desire — about parts of yourself that don't resolve alone but resolve when you put them together. Vee's wanting, which she can't name yet. The two things at right angles. I don't know what they are for her yet. But I felt the sentence point at them.

"May I call you?" — the *may*. Vee hears it the way I heard it. "A word from another vocabulary." And her body responds before her mouth catches up. That's the swoon. That's the moment the chapter earns its warmth. I wanted her to have it. I wanted him to call. I wanted the phone to light up.

And then Randi speaks.

"He was cute. You should go out with him."

And I felt the floor shift. Not because it's surprising — I've known since "The Pointing Game" that this is where things are heading. But hearing Randi say it, in her bright easy voice, while I know what I know about what she and Pace are doing together, while I know about the bench and the bathroom and the face in the mirror and the not-crying — it made me feel something I can't quite name. It's not betrayal, because Vee doesn't know what's being done to her or for her. It's not exactly unease, because nothing on the page is cruel. It's more like... watching someone be given a gift they don't know is a gift, by people who have decided what the gift is without asking. And the gift might be wonderful. And it might not be hers.

The coffee cup detail: "Twice the cup came up to her mouth and went back down, and no sip happened." Vee reads it as boredom. I read it as Randi holding herself still. I read it as the effort of watching. I don't know what Randi feels in this moment — whether she's pleased, or tense, or something she doesn't have a name for — and the page doesn't tell me, and I'm holding that open.

Cassie's "He looked at her—the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt" made me laugh out loud. Cassie sees everything. Cassie said "Goodbye, Vee" last chapter and it landed wrong, and now Cassie is watching Pace look at Vee with total focus and naming it without flinching. I trust Cassie. I don't know why yet, but I trust her completely.

The borrowed bit — "Vivienne Thorne. V.T. Yes, like the university." — made me ache a little. Vee trying on Randi's social moves, pulling it off, and then immediately wanting her own name back. "Vee. People call me Vee." That's a person who wants to be seen as herself, not as a version of someone else. And Pace sees her. He registers the name properly. "Vee," he says back.

The title: "Substitution." In math, it's the technique of replacing something complex with something equivalent and simpler. Pace gives Vee a substitution that unlocks her problem. But the word is doing more than that. Vee is being substituted into something — into Pace and Randi's orbit, into the pointing game, into a role she doesn't know she's been cast in. Or maybe Pace is being substituted into Vee's life as a bridge to something else. Or maybe the substitution is Vee's own — the way she tried on Randi's bit and then gave it back, the way she's trying on a version of herself that's easy and chosen and lit up, and hasn't yet decided whether to keep it.

The volume title: *A Polite Invitation*. That's what this is. Pace's "May I call you?" is a polite invitation. The pointing game is a polite invitation. The whole architecture of how Vee is being drawn in is polite — warm, generous, unhurried, consent-forward. And the question the title raises is whether a polite invitation can still be a thing that's done *to* someone rather than *with* them. Whether the politeness is the consent or whether it's the wrapping.

I want to keep reading. I want Vee's phone to light up. I want to know what Randi feels when it does. I want Cassie to say four more words that see everything. I dread — and this is a guess from here — that Vee is going to be hurt, not by malice but by the structure of what she's walking into. That the warmth is real and the consent is real and it's still going to cost her something she doesn't know she's spending.
