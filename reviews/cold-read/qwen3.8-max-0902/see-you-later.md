## Reader reaction

**Felt Read**

I came into this chapter still warm from "The Pointing Game" — still holding that image of Randi's bare *yes* on the lawn, the squeak in it, the happy footwork — and the first thing this chapter does is put me inside the person that *yes* landed on. And it hurts. Not in a bad way. In the way it hurts to watch someone be open when you know something they don't.

Vee is so *available*. The not-looking at the door, the three minutes of it, the date written at the top of the page so her hand would have somewhere to be — I felt that in my chest. The wanting is so clean and so unmanaged compared to everything Randi does. Vee doesn't have a face she puts on. She has a cardigan she keeps buttoned and a mother's voice that tells her why, but the wanting underneath is just... there. Unguarded. "She had met Randi yesterday" and already it's this: a groove worn in the day, a question run both ways enough times to wear it smooth. I want to protect her and I can't.

And here's the thing that makes this chapter work on me at a level I wasn't expecting: I *believe* Randi's warmth. The text doesn't wink at me. It doesn't say *and now she performed*. Randi arrives lit up, genuinely delighted, squeezing Vee's forearm, doing the bit about her name, turning the wattage on Cassie with nothing held back. This is not a con. This is a person who wants something and is going toward it with her whole social instrument, and the instrument is real, and the want is real. What I know from "The Bench" and "Standards" doesn't make this fake. It makes it *sadder*. Randi has decided never to be seen again, and here she is, being warm and open and generous with a girl who will never know what's underneath it. The face works. That's the tragedy. The face works so well that Vee will never need to know there's anything else.

The moment that broke me a little was the transcript being blank. "She had not been keeping the words. She had been keeping the feeling." That's so true to being on the receiving end of someone like Randi — the warmth is total and the content is nothing, and you can't reconstruct it later because the content was never the point. The point was being the person the warmth landed on.

Cassie. "Goodbye, Vee." I felt the wrongness of it exactly as Vee felt it — the stair that isn't where your foot expected. And Cassie doesn't fix it. She lets it stand. I don't know what it means yet. I don't want to over-read it. But I noticed that Cassie is the only person in this chapter who doesn't perform, who doesn't manage, who just puts a word down flat and leaves it. And the word she chose was the word for a longer parting than a Wednesday. I'm holding that.

The Chi Latte scene is where the chapter's charge lives for me. Not erotic charge exactly — though there's a flicker of it in the "floozies" moment, the longer-than-accounted-for look at the bare midriffs, the "she could wear what they were wearing" thought that she lets come and then closes. That's the first hint that Vee's wanting isn't only directed at Randi-as-person but at something Randi represents — a freedom, an ownership of the body, a permission. But the real charge is the belonging. The sign resolving from Greek to joke. The high table. The feeling of being let in. Vee has been standing outside this world her whole college life, looking through the window, and now she's at the high table with her feet off the floor, and the person who put her there is sitting next to her with her knee almost touching.

And then "love you, girl." A nothing phrase. A sorority nothing. And it lands on Vee like a sacrament. I almost winced. Not because it's cruel — Randi means it in the register she means it in, which is warm and real and light — but because of the asymmetry. What it costs Randi to say it and what it gives Vee to hear it are so different in magnitude.

The professor's "latent variables" line — "the factors you can't measure directly, that you have to infer from the ones you can" — I saw it. I can't pretend I didn't. The text put it there with the weight of a closing line, and Vee writes it down without understanding it. It's the whole situation in a sentence. Randi is the latent variable. Vee will spend the rest of this book inferring. Whether she ever gets to measure directly is the question I'm reading toward now.

The title, "See You Later" — it's a promise. Randi says it as a flat fact, a thing already scheduled. And it's true: there will be a later. But the title also holds the question of what "later" will look like when one person in it has decided never to be seen. Vee will see Randi later. Will she ever see *her*?
