## Reader reaction

I'm going to be honest: this chapter is small, and I love it for being small. After the intensity of the bench scene, after the social knife-work of "Standards," after the gorgeous daylight machinery of the pointing game, after the full-on rush of Vee's interior in "See You Later" and the math-tutoring scene in "Substitution" — this is the exhale. The quiet after the bell. And it earns its quietness completely.

What lands hardest for me is the frozen yogurt cup. "She built her cup without thinking, and it got away from her — too much, the cone of it leaning. Usually she heard her mother before she'd finished, *that's plenty, that's enough now.* Today there was nothing." That's the whole chapter in four sentences. Vee is a woman who has been portioned her entire life — by her mother's voice, by her own internalized governance — and here she overfills a froyo cup and the voice doesn't come, and she finds she doesn't care. It's not a revolution. It's a Tuesday. But it's the first crack in the portioning, and it happens over frozen yogurt with a man who has a system for tart bases. I'm moved by it in a way I didn't expect to be moved by frozen yogurt.

And then the confession: "This is the nicest a person has been to me in a really long time. And it's froyo." The *and it's froyo* is doing so much work — it's her trying to shrink the thing she just said, to make it smaller by naming how absurd the vehicle is. She's embarrassed not by the feeling but by the ratio between the feeling and its cause. And Pace — "He didn't pretend. He didn't make it large either." He just lets it be true, and then redirects to something concrete: "Your tart's holding up." Which is also, quietly, a reassurance. *You overfilled and it's fine. The structure holds.* I don't think he means it metaphorically. I think he means the yogurt. But the page lets it be both, and I'm grateful for that.

My feeling about Vee has deepened considerably across these last two chapters. In "See You Later" she was luminous and slightly heartbreaking — the girl who can't remember what was said, only that she was chosen. In "Substitution" she was sharper, more present, more herself — the econ mind, the woman who notices how a man handles a pencil. Here she's both at once: the wanting girl who reaches for the flaw ("Is he desperate?") because sitting in the wanting is harder, and the clear-eyed woman who hears her mother's voice over a yogurt cup and notices its absence. She's becoming someone I want to protect, which is a dangerous thing to feel about a character because it means I'm about to watch her get hurt.

Randi in this chapter is brief but precise. "He looked like a man who wants you." Then, grinning: "He wants to see you naked." Then, when Vee reaches for the waiting rule: "The rule's for boys you're not sure about. You're not unsure." Every line Randi speaks here is a push — gentle, warm, but a push. She's moving Vee toward Pace. And I know why. I know about the pointing game. I know Randi chose Vee. I know this is orchestrated. And that knowledge sits under Randi's warmth like a stone under water — visible, but changing the current around it. I don't distrust Randi exactly. But I'm watching her hands.

Cassie's "As long as he doesn't make you roll a healer again" is a gift — a tiny window into a shared history, a semester of Vee staying up until two keeping a business major alive in a video game. It makes their friendship feel lived-in, and it makes Cassie's dryness feel like love.

Now — the thing I'm holding with both hands: Vee does not know Pace is Randi's boyfriend. She does not know about the pointing game. She does not know she was *selected*. She is walking into this with her whole chest open, hearing her grandfather's voice in Pace's questions, overfilling her yogurt, saying the truest thing she's said to anyone in months. And I am reading this knowing what I know, and the tenderness of it is inseparable from the dread.

**The title:** "The Long Way" works on the surface — they walk the long way, not toward anything. But it's also the way Pace is doing this: not the direct route, not the efficient one. The long way around to something. And maybe it's the way Vee has been taking the long way to herself — through the portioning, through the mother's voice, through the careful management of how much she lets herself want. The long way is where the chapter lives.

**The book title — WITH A LONG SPOON · BOOK ONE — A POLITE INVITATION:** The spoon proverb is about keeping your distance from something dangerous. "A Polite Invitation" — that's what this is. Pace's text is a polite invitation. The frozen yogurt is a polite invitation. Everything about how Pace approaches Vee is polite, measured, respectful. And the book is asking whether politeness is a form of care or a form of distance. From here, I think it might be both.

**What I want / expect / dread:** I want Vee to be happy. I expect she won't be, not simply. I dread the moment she finds out what she's walked into — not because I think Pace is cruel (he isn't, the page won't let me believe that), but because the *shape* of it will matter. Vee is someone who needs to know the shape of things. She's an econ mind. She reads structures. And the structure she's walking into is one she cannot currently see.

**Guess from here:** I think Vee will find out about Randi and Pace relatively soon — not because someone tells her, but because she's observant, because she reads rooms, because she noticed Randi's hand on the coffee cup in "Substitution" and filed it. I think the discovery will be the hinge of the book. And I think it will not go the way any of them expect.
