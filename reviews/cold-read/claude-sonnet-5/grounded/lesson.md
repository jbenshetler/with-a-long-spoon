# Cold read (grounded) — Lesson

## Reader reaction

There's such a lovely, teasing sweetness in the opening — the dorm-hallway scene with Meg, "If you want to, I'll teach you," said word for word, that little detail delighted me. It's such a small thing, and it tells you everything about how gone Vee is: she's not even embarrassed to be caught savoring his exact phrasing. And then the dance lesson itself. I melted a little watching Pace teach her on that cold concrete pad with a boom box that "belonged to a decade she hadn't been alive for" — it's such a specific, unglamorous, patient kind of romance. "That's what gets you stepped on. The space you're keeping." I felt that line land as more than dance instruction — it's the whole book's argument about Vee and closeness in miniature, and I don't think it's heavy-handed; it earns itself because it stays inside the literal lesson.

Then the bar. Oh, that turn hurt in exactly the way good jealousy scenes hurt — not because I doubted Pace, but because I could feel Vee's whole fragile new confidence curdle in real time. The blonde's line — "He's so good about getting a girl home safe at the end of the night" — is a masterfully ambiguous knife: it reads completely differently depending on whether you trust him, and Vee, primed by shame she's never fully put down, reads it in the worst possible light. I actually wanted to grab her by the shoulders. "I'll handle that myself" — I felt the humiliation of watching someone torch something good because an old wound got poked.

What got me hardest, though, was watching her sit in it — the floozies line recurring ("Floozies. She watched them"), her mother's voice doing its cruel work on other women now instead of just herself. That's an ugly, human turn and the book doesn't flinch from it or excuse it. She's not just hurt, she's briefly, genuinely cruel — to Pace, and in her own head to other women — and the narrative doesn't rush to make her sympathetic again. It just shows her doing it.

And then the bartender. That whole exchange floored me — "Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey." I actually gasped a little at that line. It's the chapter's real reversal, delivered by a total stranger with zero investment in either of them, which makes it land as more trustworthy than anything Pace himself could have said in his own defense. It reframes the whole bar sequence retroactively — not just the blonde's dig, but the "girls" Vee resented on the floor. It doesn't erase what Vee did, though. She still said the cruel thing, still left him standing there with an untouched beer.

**On Pace** — nothing here shakes my read of him. If anything it deepens it: unhurried with the crowd, taking hugs "the way he took everything," even, "no more for one than for another," walking out quietly rather than making a scene when she's cruel to him. He absorbed the insult and left with dignity rather than escalating. That tracks with everything the checkpoint told me — the man who asks before he takes, who doesn't push doors. I like him more, not less, for how he didn't defend himself in the moment.

**On Vee** — my sympathy took a real hit and then came back, which is exactly the kind of complicated I want from this book. I don't excuse the "I'll handle that myself" moment or the ugliness of "floozies," but I recognize the shame-machinery driving it (tracked since the checkpoint — "Somebody taught her to be ashamed of her body") and I trust the book to let her sit in the consequence rather than handing her an easy out. The final line — "She was going to fix it" — has me rooting for her without knowing yet what "fix it" costs or looks like.

**Trust vs. suspicion** — none of my suspicion here is new; it's the same structural unease the checkpoint left me with (the engineered origin of all this), but nothing in this chapter itself reads as false. The blonde's dig felt earned, ambiguous, plausible — not a plot contrivance, just the ordinary cruelty of bar small-talk landing on raw nerves.

**Erotic charge** — this chapter runs almost entirely on tension rather than heat, and that's fine — the dance-lesson scene has real physical charge (the frame, the push against his shoulder, "there it is, quiet") without being sexual, and it works on me as a different register of intimacy. No explicit content this chapter; the charge here is emotional, not sexual, and it's effective on those terms.

**Friction** — very little. The one small thing: the bartender's dialogue does a lot of narrative heavy-lifting very compactly ("Wasn't him they were jealous of, honey") — it's almost too clean a reversal, delivered by someone with no reason to lie but also very conveniently possessing exactly the information Vee needs. I noticed the machinery a little, but the voice earned it enough that I let it go.

**Titles** — "Lesson" is doing lovely double work: it's literally the dance lesson, but it's also, by the chapter's end, the lesson Vee learns about her own reflexive shame and cruelty — and possibly a lesson about jealousy she still needs to metabolize before she goes to fix things. It didn't spoil anything for me going in; it recolored nicely on the way out. As for the series/book titles — "A Polite Invitation," "With a Long Spoon" — I keep feeling the dramatic irony they promise (the reader ahead of Vee) pressing at the edges here, though nothing in this chapter touched the Randi/Pace secret directly. This chapter felt more like a stand-alone stress test of Vee and Pace's bond than plot movement on the central irony.

**What I want next** — I want to see what "fix it" means when Vee gets to Randi's — will she tell Randi the whole story, including the ugly parts? And I'm bracing (a guess from here) for Randi to use this moment to draw Vee even closer, given the checkpoint's pattern of Randi mining Vee's disclosures. I also want Pace and Vee to actually repair this directly, not just via Vee's private resolve — I don't yet know if the book will show us that scene or skip past it.
