## Reader reaction

God, this one landed hard. It's the chapter I've been braced for since the fitting, and it delivers real heat — the first time the book has gone fully explicit with Pace and Vee — but what actually stayed with me is that little narrative machine running underneath the whole thing: *floozie... floozie... shame ran the whole length of it and never closed her.* That's the sentence I'm carrying out of this chapter. Not the orgasm — the shame that doesn't stop the pleasure and doesn't get resolved by it either. It just runs alongside, the whole time, like a second soundtrack.

**Pace** — I like him more here, not less. The consent beat ("Would you be bothered if I was too hard?") lands as real, not performative — the text tells me she felt it as both a real question and something more, and I trust that read. His whole approach to her body — staying in the wet of her, glad of it, "nothing in how he held her wanted her to be any less than she was" — is the exact opposite of what her mother's voice is telling her she is. That's the chapter's actual argument, I think: his hands say one thing and the voice in her head says another, and the text refuses to let either one win outright. The scar on his thigh is new and unexplained — filed as a question, not a worry, just noted and moved past by both of them.

**Vee** — my heart is completely with her. The dressing scene beforehand — the pumps, the kitten heels, "they said out loud the thing she was trying not to," the nail polish drying at her desk — is such a precise, specific portrait of a young woman managing her own want in public even to herself. And then "Famished," landing a beat after his own line, unplanned — that's the swoon moment, the two of them accidentally saying the same true thing at once.

**Swoon / pull** — enormous. The whole build (the shirt gone thin with wear, the green dress fabric "cool going warm in her hand," the frosted plum matching the sun-porch silk) is doing real work — I felt swept along with her toward Friday exactly the way she was letting herself be swept. The ending — her folding the panties into thirds, "Trade," his shoulders going private — undid me a little. It's tender and strange and completely earns its intimacy without spelling out what it means to either of them.

**Trust vs. suspicion** — I want to flag something rather than bury it: this chapter is *so* good at rendering desire and care that it temporarily quieted the suspicion I was carrying about Pace from earlier chapters (the engineering worry). I noticed myself wanting to let it go. I'm not going to pretend that worry is resolved — the text hasn't resolved it, it's just absent from this page — but I want to be honest that the absence itself did work on me, and I should hold that as *my* susceptibility, not as the book's verdict.

**Erotic charge** — high, and it earns its length. The scene doesn't rush the shame away for the sake of a clean orgasm; it keeps both running to the very end, which is unusual and, for me, the thing that makes it more than just heat — it's heat *with a cost attached that never gets paid off or resolved*, which is exactly what I want from this kind of book.

**Friction** — none that bothered me. If anything the epilogue-at-the-table (the fra diavolo, the quiet, "the evening had used her well") is doing careful, unshowy work — a domestic comedown after the explicit scene that never once tips into sentimentality.

**Titles** — "Famished" is doing double duty: her actual hunger, and the word landing on both their tongues at once as a kind of accidental confession — of want, generally, not just for food. It's a title that gives itself away cleanly and I liked that it did. Against the book title *A Polite Invitation* — this chapter is the least "polite" thing that's happened yet, all doors opened at once, and I think that's the point: politeness was the container, this is what it was containing.

**What I want next** — I want to know what "Trade" means to her, if anything past instinct. I want more of the scar. And I still want, somewhere down the line, to know whether Pace's tenderness here is the whole truth or half of one.
