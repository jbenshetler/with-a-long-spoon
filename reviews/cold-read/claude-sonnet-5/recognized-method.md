## Reader reaction

Oh, this chapter got me. It landed some real swoon and it also landed something I'm going to be sitting with — and unlike last chapter, most of the disquiet here isn't the text's, it's mine, brought in from what I already know.

How I feel about Pace: warmer, more genuinely fond, than I've felt about him yet. The doorway scene — "Beauty like yours made for the gods and loaned out — I'm not going to insult the loan by complaining I have to look up" — is exactly the thing Sheri clocked last chapter, the extravagant thing said in the plain voice. And then "Thank you for telling me what she said," instead of any of the easy absolutions he could have reached for — that's a real moment of a man refusing to let an apology be about relieving his own discomfort. I liked him enormously there. I liked him in the kitchen too, dry and amused, "Two minutes," letting her lobby him and clearly enjoying being lobbied. But I am carrying the last chapter's "That's not finished" into every one of these tender beats like a stone in my shoe. He is doing all of this — the shirt folded at the end of the bed, "You're always beautiful" — while, as far as I know, still involved with Randi. The text doesn't flinch or wink at that; it just doesn't mention it, which is its own kind of unease, mine, not the page's.

How I feel about Vee: tender toward her, and a little wrung out on her behalf. The pie label, the rehearsed "clean machine" of the apology, all of it falling apart the second he just asks her, gently, "what happened" — that's beautifully observed, the way a whole prepared structure of dignity can't survive someone actually wanting the truth instead of the performance of contrition. And then the confession about the blonde girl at the bar — "All us girls love him... he's so good about getting a girl home safe" — is the reader's own dramatic irony landing directly in Vee's mouth. We know (from Sheri's chapter, from the bartender) that this is apparently true and unremarkable to everyone except Vee, who's carrying it as an assault on her faith in him. She names her own self-deception with real clarity — "I knew what she was doing and it got into me anyway" — which is exactly the kind of interior honesty I read this book for.

Swoon / pull: real, and in two different registers. The doorway apology is swoon of the classic kind — being seen, being forgiven without groveling, a man who won't let you disappear into your own contrition. The kitchen scene is a different, hotter kind of swoon — "Soaking is a recognized method" is genuinely funny and charming, playful in a way I haven't gotten from these two before, and then the spank turns the whole register on a dime. That's the erotic peak of the chapter for me: "Her nipples drew up tight, to aching points, so fast and so hard it was nearly pain... her body saying two or three things at once that she had not agreed to have it say." That's good, honest, unashamed writing about a body doing something before the mind consents to it — exactly the kind of desire-she-can't-fully-account-for the book keeps promising. And I buy it; it doesn't feel manufactured, it feels like an accident that happened to both of them and that he watches happen on her face without smirking about it.

Trust vs. suspicion: nothing here is off on the page — Pace is warm, present, generous, funny, attentive to a degree that reads as real rather than performed. My suspicion is entirely imported from the last chapter's "not finished." That's honest to name: the text hasn't earned distrust of this weekend in itself, but I can't unknow what I know, and it changes how I read "you're always beautiful" — is that a man falling for someone, or a man who says exactly the right extravagant thing to more than one woman? I genuinely don't know, and I don't think the page wants me to resolve it yet.

Erotic charge: works well, peaks at the spank and the moment right after it, goes tender rather than explicit in the "he let her walk him backward out of the kitchen" fade — which I like; it trusts the reader rather than needing to spell out what came next. The Monday-morning scene cools it back down into something purely tender rather than charged, and that's the right choice — not every beat needs to run hot.

Friction: very little. The one place I paused was the shift from "she went into the bathroom to do something with herself" crying over her hair — it's a small, almost childish collapse, and I bought it, but only barely; it reads as slightly overdetermined as a device to get to "You're always beautiful," a small thumb-on-the-scale moment where the emotional beat needed is engineered a touch too visibly. Minor, forgivable.

Titles: "A Recognized Method" — I love this one. On the surface it's just Vee's joke about soaking dishes, a moment of real charm and deflection. But it's doing double work: this whole weekend is Vee executing a recognized method — Randi's script, "go in sure, not sorry," the pie, the heels — and watching it dissolve the second real contact happens is the chapter's actual shape. The method (the rehearsed apology) fails; what works is the unrehearsed thing (the truth, the spank, the laughing). Sly, satisfying title, the kind that recolors on a second thought rather than spoiling anything.

Book/series title: A Polite Invitation and With a Long Spoon both still sit at a slight remove from what's on the page emotionally, but "a long spoon" (supping with the devil, keeping distance) feels newly relevant given what I'm carrying about Randi — there's a proverb-shaped warning hovering somewhere in this story that the characters inside it can't see and I, holding the spoon-length information about "not finished," now can.

What I want next: I want to see whether the book ever puts Vee in the room with the knowledge I have — whether she finds out about Randi being unfinished, and what that collision looks like. Guess from here, marked as a guess: given the title and the trajectory, I don't expect this to stay comfortably hidden much longer, and I don't expect it to resolve gently when it surfaces.
