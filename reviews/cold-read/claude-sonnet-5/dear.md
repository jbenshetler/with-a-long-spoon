# Cold read (grounded) — Dear

## Reader reaction

FELT READ

I came into "Dear" with a lot freshly loaded — the bench, the pointing game, the froyo date, the tree, the kiss — and it's doing something to me that I want to name plainly: I am watching a girl fall in love with a friendship I already know is a con, and it hurts in a way that's different from suspense. It's dramatic irony working exactly as advertised on the jacket — "the reader, ahead of her from the start" — and here, for the first time, I feel the weight of standing where the jacket put me.

Vee, first. I like her enormously and I ache for her. The whole chapter is her handing Randi the best two days of her life — "May I choose?" and the kiss under the tree — and watching her tell it, knowing what I know about the quad, about the pointing game, about "Her," Pace said, and pointed — is close to unbearable in the good, cruel way this book seems to want. She's not stupid. She's starved, and she knows exactly what starved feels like ("She had been fine for two years. This was not fine"), and I don't blame her for reaching. The line that got me most: "That was the strange part, how much she wanted to put all of it into Randi's hands." That's not naivety, that's need finding its object, and the object has already decided what it wants to do with her.

Randi is the one who's moved on me most since the recent chapters. The Bench and Standards did real work — I came out of those two scenes with actual sympathy for her, for the face-in-the-mirror scene, "she had been more alive on that bench than she could remember being, anywhere, ever... and it had not come from anything he had done to her body. It had come from being seen" — and then the deliberate closing of the door, "not tonight — and this arrived without words, quiet as a deadbolt going over — not ever." That's a person in genuine crisis, guarding something real. Then The Pointing Game recast her instantly — watching her stage a collision with Vee like a play she'd rehearsed, watching her hand her own suppressed wanting to Pace as "a gift... for us" rather than name it as hers — that's calculated in a way that chilled me even while I understood exactly why she needed the cover story. And now in "Dear" she is warm to Vee in a way that reads as completely sincere on the page — the hand on the table, "dear friend," standing up to Brooke on Vee's behalf — and I can't decide whether that warmth is itself part of the game, or whether the game and the real affection have simply become the same motion for her. I don't think the book wants me to be able to decide that yet, and I'm holding it open rather than forcing it one way. That ambiguity is the most interesting thing in the chapter.

Pace, offstage in this chapter but hovering over every line of it (he's who Vee is talking about), reads differently to me now than he would have cold. In the froyo and dinner scenes on their own he's swoon-worthy — patient, courtly, the "May I choose" is genuinely lovely, unhurried in exactly the way that makes Vee's guard come down. But I've also just watched him run "the pointing game" with Randi on the quad, coaching her toward Vee with the same unhurried patience he uses to court Vee herself — and it's the same instrument aimed at two different women for two different purposes, and he knows it, and enjoys it ("He had no name for the game yet, only the live private charge of something he imagined they could make together"). I don't distrust his tenderness toward Randi on the bench — that felt real, earned, and moving. I distrust what he's doing with that same tenderness now that I've seen it deployed as a technique.

Cassie is the one character in "Dear" I trust without reservation, and the chapter uses her sparingly and well. The moment where Vee catches "the look go past her, to Randi... and by the time Cassie's eyes came back to Vee's, whatever had been in them was folded away" — that's a small, precise wound, and Vee's own gloss on it ("it was fine to have your own people... nobody ever called that anything but healthy... Underneath it something turned over, small, and she let it lie") is the chapter's saddest sentence for me. Vee is doing to her own perception exactly what the whole book seems to be about: noticing, and refusing to look at what she noticed.

Swoon and pull: real, and I want to be honest about it rather than only suspicious. The recounted date — the tea in the little brass cages, "May I choose," the kiss she chooses to make happen herself under the tree — genuinely worked on me, and it's still working on me secondhand, filtered through Vee telling it to Randi at the smoothie place. That secondhand quality is its own kind of ache: I got the primary version in the earlier chapters and now I'm getting Vee's retelling, softened and proud, and the gap between what I know and what she's choosing to remember and say out loud is where the chapter lives.

Trust vs. suspicion: I have no doubt at all that something is being done to Vee, because I watched it get planned. What I don't have is certainty about motive or feeling for Randi — whether her tenderness toward Vee is real, is guilt, is appetite, is all three at once. I'm not going to resolve that for the text; it hasn't resolved it either.

Erotic charge in this specific chapter: none on the page — no sex, no real physical charge beyond a hand held on a tabletop and a described kiss reported after the fact. It's a social scene, all appraisal and status (Brooke's tour of Vee "top to bottom," the too-tall chairs, the wrong-size feeling). The heat in my head right now is coming entirely from what I already read in The Bench and The Pointing Game, carried over.

Friction: the only thing that nagged slightly was how fast and cleanly Randi produces the exact right rescue line at the exact right moment with Brooke — "dear friend," the hand on the table — it's a beautifully controlled beat, but it's controlled enough that I noticed the control, the way you notice a very good save in a game. It didn't feel like an authorial thumb so much as it felt like watching Randi's own machinery, which given what I now know about her, might be exactly the point rather than a miss.

Titles: "Dear" lands two ways for me and I like that it does both. It's the endearment — "dear friend," said out loud, spent in front of an audience, which the text itself frames as costing something ("it had cost something, that Randi had spent it on her without appearing to count it"). And it's the older sense, dear as expensive, as in something that will cost you dear. Both meanings are sitting right there in the same word and I don't think that's an accident given everything else this book has been doing with quiet double meanings. The series title, With a Long Spoon, keeps ringing in my ear too now — sup with the devil, use a long spoon — and after The Pointing Game I can't read Randi and Pace's collaboration as anything but that proverb in slow motion. A Polite Invitation, similarly: every door Vee walks through, she walks through because someone was polite about opening it.

What I want next: I want to see whether Cassie says anything real to Vee, ever, or whether she keeps folding it away — I think I'd trust the book less if Cassie just gets sidelined without ever landing a real warning. I want to know what Randi does with the wanting she can't look at — the jacket already told me it's coming ("Vee never lets herself notice how much she grows to want Randi, too"), so I'm braced for that turn and, honestly, wanting it, the way you want a thing you already know will hurt. And I'm curious whether Pace's care for Vee stays as unmixed as it's been so far, or whether it starts to look, up close, like the same instrument I watched him use on Randi on the quad.

Randi's hand on Vee's — new instance of a recurring gesture (the arm squeeze at the lawn, the hug at Chi Latte, now the held hand on the tabletop in front of Brooke), escalating in visibility and cost each time. "Goodbye"/parting words as a small tell — Cassie's earlier "Goodbye, Vee" recurs here as "I'll see you at the dorm" plus the folded-away look, same character, same quiet wrongness, smaller this time. The word "dear" itself, landing once, plainly, as the title.

Symbolism: the tall chair / shelf-seat reads as more than furniture — Vee keeps being physically elevated into visibility she didn't choose and doesn't want (the high table twice now), which tracks with the larger pattern of being chosen and displayed without being consulted.

Characterization: Randi deepens the most — the gap between her private collapse (Standards), her calculated staging (The Pointing Game), and her apparently sincere warmth here creates a genuinely unresolved person rather than a flattened schemer, which is the strongest writing in the run so far. Vee stays consistent and legible — her hunger, her self-editing, her habit of noticing and then setting the noticing down, all track cleanly across chapters. Cassie is thin on the page in this chapter specifically (one folded-away look, one exit line) but consistent with her established economy — she's never given more than she needs, and that restraint is itself characterization. Brooke and the unnamed woman are surface-only here, functioning as social weather rather than people yet, which feels intentional rather than thin.

Pace — within the chapter: even. The classroom-to-smoothie-shop transition is brisk and doesn't linger; the bulk of the chapter is the retold date, which has its own internal pacing (the froyo beat lands fast, "May I choose" gets weight, the kiss gets the most careful handling), and then the Brooke interruption arrives at a good moment — right when Vee and the chapter are both at their most unguarded.

Pace — chapter to chapter: this one is a comedown in intensity after The Bench and The Pointing Game, deliberately so — a quieter, social, dramatic-irony chapter after two very charged ones. It doesn't feel like sagging; it feels like the plan (Randi and Pace's, and maybe the book's) taking its next necessary step in daylight, in public, at low heat, while I sit there knowing what the low heat is covering.
