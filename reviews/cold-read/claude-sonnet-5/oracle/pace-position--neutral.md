**Question asked (verbatim):** What do you make of Pace? What's going on inside him, as far as you can tell?

---

Honestly, Pace is the person in this book I've moved on the most times, and I still don't feel like I've landed anywhere final with him — which I think is exactly what the novel wants.

My gut-level read, body before analysis: he's the most seductive presence in the book, and not because he's dominant or smooth — because he *notices*, relentlessly, precisely, and gives back exactly what he's noticed. The sliced apple in "The Bench," the boot fix on the mountain, the dress built from an offhand wish, the shirt with no tag, turning the heat up hours before she arrives. That noticing is what got me from chapter one, and it never stopped working on me even after I had every reason to distrust it.

But I came to him backwards, which matters. I met him first as the man on the bench with Randi — patient, expert, genuinely moving in the aftercare — and in that very same chapter I clocked something colder under the tenderness: "the small warm pleasure of having taken her further than he had thought she would go." That's not cruelty, but it's not uncomplicated love either — it read to me like a man cataloguing an achievement even while being kind. Then "The Pointing Game" confirmed it structurally: he turned a walk into an audition of strangers for Randi's desire, and when Vee laughed by the dining hall doors, he pointed at her like an item on a menu. So from Vee's very first "chance" meeting onward, I could never fully un-know that his courtship of her began as a technique, tested first on someone else, run with real relish ("he had no name for the game yet, only the live private charge of something he imagined they could make together").

That's the split I carried the whole book: everything he does for Vee individually is exquisite and, as far as I can tell, sincerely felt in the moment — I don't think he's cynical, I don't think he's performing contempt. But the same competence that makes him a wonderful lover also makes him a very good operator, and the book never lets me fully separate the two. "hills-and-valleys" was the chapter that really nailed this for me — hearing him, offhandedly, tell a cycling friend "Randi — that's not finished," present tense, in the same breath as rhapsodizing about Vee's thumb on a chair joint, made the double life feel administrative rather than tragic, like he's just managing two open items. That's the closest the text ever got to making me actually dislike him rather than just distrust the frame around him.

"Rock" gave me the most sympathy I ever felt for him — Peter, not Pace; the hallway punishment, math turned into torment; Daphne, the two-years-older/younger asymmetry he narrates as "the best two years I'd had" without seeming to know he's defending an indefensible thing. I don't believe his account of that relationship is trustworthy, but I do believe he believes it, and that's the horror of it — someone narrating their own hurt (or their own harm) as agency because the alternative is unbearable. It made his whole "May I—" courtesy, his obsession with consent and closed doors, read less like pure virtue and more like a system built by someone once opened without being asked, projected outward. I don't think that resolves him into either "predator" or "wounded good man" — I think it's genuinely both, unresolved, on purpose.

And then the back third of the book turned the screw again, differently: not through concealment but through withholding. "Seconds," "Under the Rug," "Believe Me" — three chapters running where Vee hands him something real (the cracker-and-meal line, her whole vulnerability) and he answers with something adjacent — "I love how good we are together," "believe what I do, not what I say" — praising the effect, never naming the person. That's the first time my suspicion of him became text-earned in the room, not just inherited dramatic irony. "Words are cheap" is a genuinely ugly thing to say to someone who just told you she's never had a full meal before you, dressed up as philosophy. Sheri, in "The Usual," said out loud what I'd been feeling for chapters — "not saying it matters more to you than having her... that feels pretty shitty" — and I trusted her flat, unglamorous read more than anything Pace has said about himself.

So where I land: I think there's real love in him — the boot fix, standing shivering in the rain so Vee could change in privacy, the drawer he cleared out, the voice cracking on "I missed you so much." None of that reads as fake to me. But I also think he's a man who has built an entire ethical architecture — the asking, the patience, the leaving-room-to-choose — partly as genuine respect learned the hard way (Daphne, whatever happened with his parents that gets a flat "I don't go back"), and partly as a very effective wall that lets him give everything except the two things that would make him legible and accountable: the name for what Vee is to him, and the truth about Randi. He's not simple, and by the end I trust his *feelings* far more than I trust his *disclosure* — he seems constitutionally allergic to the sentence that would cost him control, even when withholding it is actively hurting the person he loves. Whether that's a survival skill from being harmed young, or a convenient cover for keeping two women on two separate tracks, or — most likely to me — both at once, the book never makes me choose, and I've stopped expecting it to.
