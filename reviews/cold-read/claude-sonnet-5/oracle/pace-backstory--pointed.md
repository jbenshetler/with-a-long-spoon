**Question asked (verbatim):** (a) COMPREHENSION — How full a picture of Pace's past do you have: family, childhood, how he got here? 0-5 (0 = a blank; 5 = a full history). List what you actually know. (b) PULL — When the book holds his past back, does it pull you toward him or shut you out? 0-5 (0 = purely shut out / frustrated; 5 = purely intrigued, wanting more). (a) COMPREHENSION: 2/5

What I actually have, piecing it together from "Rock" mainly, with scraps elsewhere: his real name is Peter — "Pace" is a nickname whose origin I'm never given. He's twenty-two, younger than I'd assumed for most of the book. Something in his childhood involved punishment tied specifically to math — "long division as punishment for loving math too much," described as happening "in a hallway" — which the text renders as a real wound ("They took the thing you loved and made it the punishment") rather than an anecdote. There's a two-year relationship in his adolescence, with a woman named Daphne, that the book lays out with careful, unglossed arithmetic (his age, her age, when it started, when it ended coinciding suspiciously with the moment the world's rules would call it visible) — he narrates it as "the best two years I'd had" and defends it without seeming to know he's defending it, which the text lets sit as its own kind of evidence rather than resolving. There's a scar, high on his inner thigh, unexplained twice on the page ("Not tonight," then later touched again in "We Find Out" and "Above Him" and still not explained). And there's a flat, absolute refusal about his parents — "I don't go back" — delivered as a wall going up clean and precise, the one moment I clocked as genuinely cold from him. Beyond that: he's from Ohio (his mother had a spice rack), he does patent/MRI work and lights up like "a six-year-old" talking about it, he builds furniture and houses with his own hands, and Sheri (a cycling friend, plainly established, unshockable) is the one person who talks to him with zero performance and who half-knows about both Randi and Vee. That's the whole file. No account of siblings, no scene with his parents even in memory, no resolution of what Daphne actually was to him or he to her, no origin for the scar, no explanation of how "the pointing game" or the arrangement with Randi started from his side beyond what I watched happen live in chapter one.

(b) PULL: 4/5

It pulls me toward him, hard, almost every time the door stays shut. The withholding reads as earned restraint rather than a device — "Rock" in particular (the fireside disclosure of Peter/Daphne/the hallway) is one of the most moving chapters in the book precisely because it gives me just enough to reframe everything I thought I knew about his careful, permission-asking manner, without spelling out the causality. The scar with "not tonight" is the same move — I trusted the book more, not less, for refusing to cash it in immediately. I don't feel shut out; I feel like I'm being fed exactly the amount that keeps me leaning in, and each new scrap (the age, the punishment, the wall about parents) recolors prior chapters rather than just adding information.

The one thing I most want and don't have: what actually happened with his parents. "I don't go back" is the hardest, coldest four words he says in the whole book, delivered with a flatness nothing else about him has, and it's never touched again anywhere in my reading record — no scene, no story, not even a name for what he's protecting himself from. Given how much the book has already used his controlled, permission-based tenderness as something whose *origin* keeps recontextualizing him (Daphne, the hallway), that unexplained refusal feels like the biggest live wire left untouched, and I finished the book still wanting it.
