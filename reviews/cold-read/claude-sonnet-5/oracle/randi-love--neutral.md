**Question asked (verbatim):** Tell me about Randi and Vee. What do you make of how Randi feels about her?

---

Randi and Vee are, for me, the relationship the book actually made me sit with the longest and feel the most conflicted about — more than the Vee/Pace romance, honestly, because Vee/Pace at least got the courtesy of being staged as a courtship. What's between Randi and Vee never got a name on the page, which is exactly the point.

It starts in "The Pointing Game," where I watched Randi's whole body answer "Her" — "Yes," bare and helpless, ahead of her own permission — before she recovers and turns her own want into a gift for Pace instead ("Yeah. I like her"). That's the founding move of the whole relationship for me: Randi cannot let herself have Vee as her own appetite, only as something she curates, hands off, or manages on someone else's behalf. Everything after follows from that first refusal to own the want.

What I make of how Randi feels about her: I think it's genuinely love, and I don't think that's in tension with the fact that it's also control. The book kept refusing to let those two things cancel each other out, chapter after chapter — the toe-stroke in the boutique that "went all the way up the length of her," the practice-room confession where Randi's attention to Vee's arousal reads as "nearly a pressure, nearly a hand," the brunch extractions dressed as girl-talk ("nobody measures over a bra"), the "sticky fingers" line I could never resolve as innocent or not. Every one of those moments is warm and real on its surface and also, underneath, Randi taking something — information, access, a confession, a body's response — while giving Vee the feeling of being chosen. By "One Bite" and "Gone," where Randi describes Vee's body to Pace in bed ("What she keeps under there. It's criminal, Pace"), I had real, page-earned suspicion, not just inherited dread: Randi is capable of treating Vee's private self as something to be relayed, performed, even between the two people who supposedly love her most.

But "Fairytale" recast all of it for me, and recast it hard. Getting fully inside Randi's head on that ski trip — the hollow perfect night with John, "Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best," and then the line that undid me: something came off Vee across a table on "a nothing Tuesday" that no fairytale could counterfeit, and Randi knowing exactly what it was and being "not brave enough to lose her." That's the chapter where I stopped being able to call her a manipulator and nothing else. She is lonely inside her own competence, using her control of Vee as the one safe way to keep her close, because the alternative — naming it, risking it — is unbearable to a woman who locked a bathroom door in chapter one and vowed never to be seen fully again by anyone, even the person she loves most.

So what I make of it, finally: Randi's feeling for Vee is real, and its reality doesn't make it safer — it makes it more dangerous, because a woman managing you out of genuine love is much harder to see clearly than a woman managing you out of indifference. The engineering never stops (the coat plan in "My Friend Randi"/"Nothing Underneath" is Randi literally scripting Vee's reconciliation sex with Pace, down to the shoes), but by the end it reads less like predation and more like a person who has never learned another way to love that doesn't involve directing the scene. Vee, meanwhile, wants her back — the practice-room bathroom moment, the imagined gaze interrupting her own masturbation in "One Bite" — but she's never been allowed to know she wants it as a full, nameable thing; it stays "weather," never a decision. That asymmetry — Randi fully aware and afraid, Vee affectionate and unaware — is the saddest and most interesting thing in the book for me, and it never resolved by the last chapter I read. I finished still not knowing whether that's tenderness wearing control's clothes or control wearing tenderness's, and I think the book wanted me exactly there.</text>
