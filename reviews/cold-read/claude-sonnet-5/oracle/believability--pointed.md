# Oracle (grounded) — believability · pointed

*model: claude-sonnet-5 · probe: believability · tier: pointed · stage: end (whole book) · battery: oracle-battery.json*

**Question asked (verbatim):** Rate how believable each is as a real person — Vivienne, Randi, Pace — 0-5 (0 = a device/plot-construct; 5 = utterly real). One line each on what makes them ring true or false.

---

Vivienne: 5 — she never once read like a device to me; her shame-voice (the mother's word "floozie"), her private editing of what she tells Cassie versus Randi, her body outrunning her permission again and again, the invented blonde rival at 2am, the ladle image of "lifting a story out clean" — all of it accumulated into a completely specific, consistent psychology I could track chapter to chapter, including her growth (claiming the "oops," the induction walk, choosing honesty with herself in "Bare" even when it hurt more). She's the one I never doubted as a person, only worried for.

Randi: 5 — she's the character who moved and complicated the most for me, and precisely because she never resolved into either "schemer" or "genuine friend" — both were true of her at once, chapter after chapter, and the text let that stand (the bathroom mirror vow in "Standards," the extraction techniques in "How It's Done"/"Fed"/"One Bite," and then "The Outlier" and "Fairytale" cracking her open into real, unperformed grief). That refusal to flatten her into one note is exactly what makes her feel like an actual person rather than a plot function, even when her actions were serving the plot.

Pace: 4 — mostly rings true (the Daphne/hallway backstory gave his courtliness a plausible origin, the flinch at Vee's poke, losing control in "Under the Rug," Sheri's diner interrogation exposing "believe what I do" as possibly armor rather than only virtue) — I docked a point because for long stretches he reads more as a consistent *style* of loving (attentive, patient, withholding) than as an interior consciousness I got to be inside the way I was with Vee and, eventually, Randi; his most legible moments came from other people reading him (Sheri, the bartender) rather than from his own unguarded interiority.
