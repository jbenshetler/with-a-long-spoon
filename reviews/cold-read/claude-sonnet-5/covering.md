Vee, this chapter, is dazzling and awful in the same breath, and I felt both without either one canceling the other. The Saturday-morning stretch — the coat gag reprise, the "pleasure princess" reversal, the toast burning twice — is the most purely delighted I've felt reading this book in a while. She's funny now. Genuinely funny, self-aware, playing her own body like an instrument, and it's a joy to watch someone who spent forty chapters ashamed of wanting things turn wanting into stagecraft. I was grinning at "I got mushrooms." I wanted to stay in that kitchen.

Then the mouth-to-mouth moment lands and I felt my stomach actually drop with her — not because of what happened (nothing did, really; she pulled back, he stopped, they moved on) but because of what she does with it afterward: manufactures the tidiest possible explanation ("he forgot to wipe") and *knows* she's manufacturing it, and lets the real question — "how many times, and how carefully, and had there always been—" — go unfinished and stay lit, minor-key, under the affection. That's the chapter's best piece of interior work. I don't get to know what she almost knows. Neither does she, quite. It's suspicion earned entirely by her own body's memory (the tongue going hunting on its own, unsent), not by me importing anything from the checkpoint — though of course I'm carrying Randi/Pace's secret and it makes that unfinished sentence ring like a bell in an empty room.

Randi is where this chapter turned genuinely uncomfortable for me, more than any single prior scene. The "how much could he see" needling into the bathroom orgasm is the same engine as always, but "It's a good thing you got relief" after Vee comes back from the stall — said pleasantly, at a table, like weather — is the most naked the manipulation has ever been on the page, and it scared me a little on Vee's behalf, precisely because Vee reads it as intimacy ("she can read her through a floor") rather than surveillance. And then the boardwalk-boy story: it's the first time Randi has told something that isn't obviously calibrated for Vee's arousal, and it's the first time I actually ached *for* Randi rather than admired or worried about her — her mother laundering the underwear and serving it at dinner, "They're not mine. They're your color," the boy who quit rather than face it again. That's a real wound, offered almost by accident, and it reframes some of Randi's compulsive engineering-of-intimacy as something learned rather than merely predatory. I liked her more and trusted her less, both, in the same five minutes.

The neck-kiss at the end is the chapter's quiet flag for me — Vee kisses the spot Randi points to without thinking, Randi flinches and laughs it off as ticklishness, and I don't buy "tickles" for a second, though the text never says otherwise. That's the sharpest unspoken beat in the chapter and I'm holding it exactly that open.

The professor's gift lecture read, to me as a reader and nothing more, as eerily on-the-nose for this book — "the gift carries the spirit of the giver, and the spirit wants to go home" — but it's presented straight, as a professor's opening lecture, not commentary, so I'm noting the resonance rather than crediting the text with meaning it.
