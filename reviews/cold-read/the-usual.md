# Cold read — The Usual

*scene: scenes/the-usual.md · read after: old-acquaintances*

## Reader reaction

This is a quiet chapter and I liked being in it, which surprises me a little — I've spent the whole book wanting to catch Pace out, and here he's just a tired man eating a cheeseburger with the one person who won't flatter him. It's the most human he's felt. And it's still the coldest thing I've read, once I sit with it.

**Pace/Peter.** The opening line does a lot: "he would not bring her down today… he had long practice at deciding a thing could keep." So he manages Sheri too — decides what she gets and when — even her, even the one he supposedly comes to for the truth. That "long practice at deciding a thing could keep" is the whole man in a sentence. And then the Chili's memory finally gets rendered from *his* side: "every sound in the room arguing with him at once… Her voice was down under all of it. I couldn't pull it clear, however hard I tried." That moved me. It's the first time the loud-room thing read as pain rather than a tell — he wasn't bored of Vee at Chili's, he was drowning and couldn't reach her. "Here he could have heard her whisper. He wondered whether she'd let him." That's real longing. I believe he misses her.

But Sheri won't let me stay soft, and she's right. His whole defense is a ledger — "I'm there. Every weekend, I'm there… I told her she was the most important thing in my life. I said it, out loud." He literally hears himself "laying it up brick on brick and couldn't stop, because every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something." That's the tragedy and the evasion at once: he thinks provisioning *is* the declaration, and he cannot understand why the bricks don't add to the one word. Sheri names it clean: "not saying it matters more to you than having her… from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." He doesn't argue. So the reason I've carried — "there was never a note" — is real, and it's costing him Vee, and he still won't spend the word. My sympathy went up and my verdict got harder in the same scene.

**Sheri.** I love her. She's exactly the moral floor the book needed here — five feet of her, too much makeup worn like a dare, eats like she rides, steals his fries mid-sentence to close a subject. "Don't waste the good stuff on me. Save it for your redhead." She's the anti-Randi: wants nothing off him, isn't impressed, tells him to his face. And her own story lands the theme sideways — Paige, the bird-print girl from the loud seven-at-the-table house, lost because Sheri "couldn't get right with me not talking to my people." She and Pace are the same wound: both sealed off from their families, both losing love to it, and she's honest enough to call it "ironic" and flat enough to kill the word before it gets precious. "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice" — that's Pace being tender and, I notice, deflecting, because his own silence isn't grief, it's control, and he lets her think they're the same kind of broken.

**The brunette.** This is the cold underneath, dropped so casually I almost slid past it. "She find out about the brunette, then." / "No. Not like that." And later: "that brunette you brought dancing before Thanksgiving — the sorority one… She looked at me like I was one more of your harem applicants. Never did learn my name." So Sheri *knows* he's running two women, has met Randi, and treats it as ordinary Pace behavior — "one more of your harem applicants." That reframes him. He's not a man newly torn between two; he's a man with a known pattern, and the only novelty is how "gone" he is on Vee. The chill is that Sheri, the truth-teller, isn't scandalized by the two-timing at all. It's just weather to her.

**Erotic charge.** Low and I didn't want it higher — this is a talk, not a heat scene. The closest it comes is Sheri's crude affection, "Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you," and his face going hot. What actually pulled at me erotically was indirect: "The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles," which Sheri deflates perfectly ("You mean the sex is good"). And the food-as-love bit — "she won't just tell me it tastes good — she gets what it's for. The whole idea under it. The philosophy of it" — is the most naked he's been, and Sheri rightly hoots at him for it ("The philosophy of your food. Lord almighty. Get over yourself"). That deflation is the best beat in the chapter.

**Friction.** Very little. The one place I felt a faint thumb was "the true things were supposed to come to something" — it's a hair close to explaining his whole architecture to me, but it's his own thought in the moment and I'll allow it. Otherwise the restraint is the pleasure; nobody explains, the irony of the two matching sealed families just "sat on the table between them" and wasn't underlined.

**What I want next.** I want to know if Sheri's "so say it" actually moves him — she's the closest thing to a lever anyone's had on him. And I'm now watching the "brunette" more coldly: Sheri's casual knowledge tells me the two-women thing predates Vee and is a habit, which makes me dread how Randi's confidante game and this pattern are going to converge on Vee. *Guess from here:* the rupture won't hold, and he'll get Vee back without ever saying the word — provisioning his way back in, which is precisely the thing Sheri just told him won't do.

## Carry-forward state

**What happened.** Pace meets Sheri at their usual county-road diner (a converted double-wide) over Christmas break. Warm, blunt catch-up: Sheri's part-time now (no one funding her tuition), just lost a girlfriend, Paige, over Sheri refusing to speak to her own family — the same seal Pace lives behind. Pace admits the rupture with Vee: she wants him to *say* it and he can't. He defends himself with everything he *does* (weekends, presence, "most important thing in my life"), and Sheri cuts through it: not-saying matters more to him than having her, and that's shitty. He doesn't argue; he admits he wants Vee and misses her. Sheri reveals she knows about "the brunette" (the sorority one he brought dancing before Thanksgiving — Randi) and treats his two-women pattern as ordinary Pace ("harem applicants"); Vee still doesn't know "like that." Ends warm, the two of them laughing over the wreckage of two cheeseburgers.

**Who's who.**
- **Vee / Vivienne** — the redhead; ruptured, no contact; the woman Pace is "gone on" like Sheri's never seen; total reader sympathy.
- **Pace / Peter** — provisioner/maker; drowns in loud rooms (the Chili's failure is grief, not indifference); genuinely misses Vee; still cannot/won't say the word; runs two women as a known pattern; sealed from his family, which Sheri mirrors.
- **Randi** — the brunette/"sorority one"; Sheri's met her, disliked her, was looked down on by her; confidante/harvester; Pace's other woman; Vee doesn't know.
- **Sheri** — Pace's truth-teller; blunt, funny, wants nothing off him; lost her girlfriend Paige to her own family-silence, mirroring Pace; the moral seat in this scene. Names his evasion cleanly.
- **Cassie** — moral floor (not present here).

**How I feel.** Warmer toward Pace as a person, harder on him as a choice — both at once, which is the book's whole trick. Sheri is a delight and I trust her. The cold underneath is that his two-women habit is *routine*, not a crisis of the heart, and even his confessor isn't shocked by it. Golden-on-top, cold-underneath, holding.

**Open questions.** Will Sheri's "say it" actually land — does the rupture hold or does he provision his way back? How far back does the two-women pattern go, and how does it collide with Randi's confidante game aimed at Vee? Still unresolved: the scar, right-hand-only eating, the sealed family, the weight loss, the nude photo, the threesome, the locked bracelet's origin. New: what exactly Sheri knows and doesn't about how Randi fits.
