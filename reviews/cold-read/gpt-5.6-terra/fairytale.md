## Reader reaction

This chapter lands like a cold shock after Vee walking out into the night. I began almost seduced by it: Randi in Gstaad, the mountain, the champagne, the perfect dress, the white-tie ball, the sleigh. It is the exact polished life she can perform, and I felt the glamour working on me even while her breakfast with her parents made my skin tighten. Her mother’s “perfunctory hug, all edges and points” and Randi “eating her revulsion” make the family table feel like a place where she has learned to turn herself into a pleasing story.

John is attractive in the deliberately storybook way: competent skier, law student, white tie, champagne, a sleigh summoned on New Year’s. I understand completely why Randi says yes before he finishes asking. The skiing section is especially charged: he is better than she is but does not humiliate her with it, and “he had the run to spare and spent none of it proving it” is a very seductive kind of restraint. But the chapter then makes the cost of that fantasy brutally clear. He assumes the hotel room; Randi discovers that “tonight, she liked not being asked.” That line made me uneasy immediately because Pace’s constant asking is so present in my memory, and because Randi’s relief in not having to speak is not the same thing as being known.

The erotic charge is strongest in the ballroom dance and the hotel threshold, where Randi gives herself over to John’s unmistakable lead. It is sensual and bright rather than graphic. Then it goes deliberately cold after sex. “And it had meant no more than a good sandwich eaten fast on a busy afternoon” is devastating—not because the sex was bad, but because nothing in it reaches Randi. The image of her putting her face back on while John sleeps is the chapter’s cruelest one. Her makeup survives; her eyes remain untouched. She can still produce the smile, but it “meant nothing.”

I feel much more tenderness toward Randi now. I already knew she was controlling, withholding, and deeply implicated in the lie around Vee and Pace. None of that is erased. In fact, this chapter makes it more frightening: she decides not to tell Vee what she knows she feels because she cannot risk Vee’s face changing, cannot risk losing “the most important hour of her week.” “Let Vee arrive at it on her own” is chilling in this book, where Vee has already been arranged into so much without knowing. Still, I believe Randi’s love for Vee is real. “Vee was her gorgeous” made me ache. Her text is affectionate and false in precisely the old Randi way: she gives Vee the fairytale version, then slips in the true line—“miss your face most though, gorgeous”—under its cover.

Pace is absent but becomes sharply present through comparison. Randi thinks John is a better dancer: firmer, easier, unmistakable. Yet she also admits “Pace on his worst night reached further than this man had managed at his best.” That moved me. Pace’s peculiar attention is not merely a private fantasy Vee has invented; Randi recognizes its force too. But I cannot forget that Pace’s reaching is entangled with control, secrecy, and at least one moment where he privileged Vee’s body over her words. The contrast does not make him safe. It makes him potent.

Vee is absent physically but emotionally enormous. Her plain text from Ohio, “i’m here if you want to talk. or not,” feels like the clearest possible version of the care Randi cannot bring herself to accept honestly. I hurt for Vee because she is offering Randi a real opening while she herself is in pain over Pace, and Randi responds by preserving a false image. Yet Vee’s unornamented kindness feels like the chapter’s moral warmth.

“Fairytale” gives something away in a useful, sad way. Before reading, it announces glamour and romance; afterward it means the story Randi can convincingly live inside without being touched by it. The chalet, the ball, the daughter she imagines telling the story to—all of it is real and none of it is the thing. It also echoes the book title, *A Polite Invitation*: John’s invitation is less polite than Pace’s, because he simply assumes the next step, and Randi finds that assumption intoxicating. The cover line—“Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.”—feels newly painful here. Randi says yes freely, but she has learned to make yes easy by keeping her hidden self out of the room.

I want to know whether this is a memory from before the main story or something happening alongside it; the text gives me Randi in Gstaad while Vee is in Ohio on New Year’s, but I do not know how it fits the present sequence. I dread Randi continuing to let Vee “arrive” at feelings on her own when Randi already knows how much she wants. My guess from here is that Randi’s silence about loving Vee will become another hidden structure Vee has to live inside without knowing.
