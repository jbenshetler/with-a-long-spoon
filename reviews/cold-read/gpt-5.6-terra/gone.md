## Reader reaction

This chapter gives me a sharp, almost sickening jolt precisely because it is so intimate and so tender on its own terms. Until now I knew Randi and Pace were secret lovers and had begun this together, but hearing them in bed afterward, talking about Vee with this much access to her life, makes the trap on the cover feel close again. Vee is not in the room; she is being loved, desired, assessed, and discussed by the two people she trusts most, while she believes those relationships arrived separately and naturally.

I am moved by Randi’s affection. Her account of Vee doing “the full bigfoot,” then flushing when she realizes strangers saw, is genuinely funny and fond. “Nobody does both. She does both” lands as real admiration: Vee listens with full attention and then makes Randi care about “some tiny nothing about her Tuesday.” And “I love it” when she says Vee is gone on Pace feels startlingly naked. It confirms that Randi’s delight in Vee has not been a performance only. She really is charmed, attracted, and emotionally caught.

But I also feel more wary of Randi than I have. She calls Vee “a little pathetic” before saying “I love it,” and then she speaks about Vee’s body—“God. What she keeps under there”—to Pace. It is erotic, and I do feel the erotic charge in Randi dragging herself across him while describing Vee, but it is uncomfortable charge: Vee’s hidden body and shame have become material inside their private sex. Randi’s “She’s not ready, though” and “You’ll want to take your time” also land differently now that I know how much she has been physically and financially directing Vee. I cannot tell whether Randi is protecting Vee, trying to manage Pace, or both. The text earns that uncertainty in the pause after Pace says, “I want her to get there on her own.” Something “crossed her face and was gone.” Then later, when Pace says Vee makes a room brighter, Randi’s stillness is explicitly costly.

Pace is more complicated here too. I still find him attractive in the specific way he lets Randi be loose afterward, waits rather than filling silence, and says of Vee’s shame, “Somebody taught her to be ashamed of her body.” That is a clear, compassionate reading of Vee. His description of her entering a room—“Like somebody found the lamp”—is beautiful and feels like genuine love or something close to it. But the chapter also makes plain that he and Randi are sharing Vee as a subject of private knowledge. He knows her vulnerabilities, Randi knows what happened in the shoe store and what Vee says about him, and they can compare notes in the bed where their whole arrangement began. His statement that he wants Vee “to get there on her own” is moving, but it cannot fully calm me because he already helped create the road she is traveling without her knowing.

The sex works as heat, though it is not really what holds me. The heat peaks when Randi describes Vee’s body while using her own against Pace, and then again in the final paragraph, with Randi “small and pale above him” and Pace letting her set the pace. Yet the eroticism is shadowed by the conversation; I was more tense than transported. The chapter’s real intensity is the shifting ownership and jealousy or fear beneath Randi’s warmth.

“Gone” is an excellent title from where I stand. Most immediately, Randi says Vee “got past me before I could help it,” and is “so gone over” Pace. But Randi also seems gone herself: past the usual boredom she names, past the line of merely helping Pace select someone, past whatever control she normally keeps. Pace may be gone on Vee too, in his lamp image. The title has a lovely, dangerous double force: enchanted, carried away, no longer fully in command. It does not give anything away before reading, but afterward it makes all three of them feel less stable.

*A Polite Invitation* now feels increasingly ominous because the invitations Vee has accepted have made a life that is real to her, while the original invitation was never honestly offered. *With a Long Spoon* still points me toward the devil-at-table feeling: being close enough to something dangerous that one ought to keep distance, yet everyone is dining together anyway. And “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap” presses hard here. Vee’s yeses are real; Pace’s statement even insists on her arriving “on her own.” But her choices are surrounded by information and conversations she has been denied. This chapter makes that distinction hurt.

I want to keep reading badly, but I dread the eventual moment Vee learns that her sweetest experiences have been discussed in rooms she was not allowed to enter. My guess from here is that Randi’s feeling for Vee is becoming difficult for her to contain—both desire and something possessive—and that Pace’s open admiration of Vee has touched exactly that nerve. I also wonder whether the expensive shoes will matter soon, because this chapter’s private tenderness leaves Vee’s practical panic entirely outside their shared awareness.
