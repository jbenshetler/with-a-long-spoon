## Reader reaction

This one made my stomach drop. I was so happy with Vee at first: Meg’s brisk, practical help; Vee repeating “*If you want to, I’ll teach you*” because it matters so much to her; Pace waiting in those polished boots with the battered boom box. The lesson itself worked on me. “Give me something to push back against” could have felt loaded, but on the page it feels genuinely instructional and intimate at once. The moment she stops anticipating and lets the lead become readable is lovely—she earns the pleasure of being good at something new, and I was right there with her when he says, “There it is.”

Then the Rusty Nail turns that private triumph outward. I felt Vee’s glow in being introduced as “This is Vee,” and I also felt the seed of trouble in how easily everybody knows Pace. The blonde’s line—“Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to”—lands like a deliberately nasty little poison, because it takes the exact thing that had made Vee feel held and recasts it as something interchangeable. I understood the jealous flare before Vee even speaks. But I winced hard at “Does your *service* include getting the girls home safe from here, too?” Pace’s answer is so direct, and her walking away leaves him with no room to explain or meet her.

I feel for Vee more than I’m angry at her. This is shame looking for a target: she has just begun to believe she could be chosen, and the thought that she might merely be “next” is unbearable. Calling the other dancers “Floozies” hurts, especially because it is Vee turning the old word against women who look unashamed in precisely the way she is trying to learn to be. Still, the chapter doesn’t make her cruelty feel casual. It lets her sit in it through the emptied bar, the expensive cab, the sleepless room. Her realization is devastatingly exact: “She’d punished him for being the man she wanted him to be.”

My trust in Pace is actually stronger by the end, based on what the women at the bar say and on the fact that he leaves rather than pressing after Vee. But I’m not totally untouched by the blonde’s phrasing, either. The text earns the question of how much of Pace’s ease as a leader can feel seductive, especially to women who are hungry to be guided. What I do not feel is suspicion that he has been exploiting those women; the bartender’s account and his untouched beer point the other way.

The erotic charge is mostly clothed and kinetic: Pace’s hand at Vee’s shoulder blade, the firm frame, her letting herself be moved without having to calculate. It peaks when the dance finally carries her and she discovers that closeness can make her safer on the floor, not more endangered. After the fight, that same bodily memory becomes painful rather than hot—her body still wants the feeling even while her mind is punishing him.

“Lesson” is painfully apt. It is literally a dance lesson, but Vee learns something she cannot yet hold onto: receiving a lead is not the same as surrendering herself, and being cared for does not make her foolish or replaceable. The chapter also gives Pace a lesson in the cost of being misunderstood, though he is mostly off-page for it. *A Polite Invitation* still feels ominous in the way an invitation can be freely accepted and yet lead someone into rooms she did not understand; *With a Long Spoon* continues to make me think of closeness with something dangerous at its center. The jacket’s “Every yes was freely given. That was the problem” is ringing louder now—not because Vee has said yes wrongly, but because she is still learning how to live with what she has wanted.

I badly want Vee to go to Randi, as she plans, but I dread what Randi will do with this tender opening. My guess from here is that Vee wants help making amends, but may also be seeking reassurance that she is singular to Pace—and that Randi’s response will matter to her more than she knows.
