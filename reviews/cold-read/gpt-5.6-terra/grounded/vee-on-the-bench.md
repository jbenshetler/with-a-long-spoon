## Reader reaction

I felt overwhelmed by this one—in the intended way, but also a little bruised by its length and relentlessness. Vee’s wanting is so fully embodied that I was inside it with her almost immediately: the held-off text, “*Wear a skirt,*” the warm house, the grapes, the tap pants. Pace’s attention to her ordinary week before anything begins is still one of the most persuasive forms of intimacy this book has; he listens because it is Vee speaking. That softness made the reveal of the bench feel thrilling rather than abrupt. I wanted her to trust him enough to walk across the room, and I felt the charged bravery in her taking off the heels herself.

The erotic pull is very strong at first and peaks for me around the bench’s revelation and Vee’s realization that the grips are not restraints unless she keeps choosing them. “*She was the thing keeping herself there*” lands hard. So does Pace asking for her safe word, checking “*You still with me?*,” coming into her line of sight, and kissing her forehead. Those moments keep the scene emotionally held, not merely engineered.

But the heat becomes almost punishingly extended. The repeated pattern—Vee denies, Pace makes the denial collapse, then asks again—works for a while because it gets at her shame and self-deception. Eventually, though, I felt the scene making its point so many times that I began to feel the mechanism. “*Tell me it rained,*” is a wonderfully cruel, exact turn; several more variations of “tell me you don’t” afterward diminished its force for me. I believed Vee’s unraveling. I did not need quite so many proofs.

My feeling about Pace remains complicated but warm. He is astonishingly attentive, and the bench is built around care as much as exposure: the supported neck, the open hands, the safe word, the check-in when she is crying and overwhelmed. Yet he is also very good at creating conditions where Vee’s body has to answer before she can bear to speak—a power that is erotic here, but uneasy because she is so practiced at calling her own desire something else. The text earns that unease. He does not ignore her “red” or her welfare, but he knows exactly how to turn her “no” into something she must confront. I am swept up by him and wary of how much authority he has over the meaning of her reactions.

Vee deepens enormously. The chapter’s ache is not that she wants; it is that she has inherited a language in which wanting makes her contemptible. When she finally stops denying the wetness, the need, the sounds, I felt relief more than triumph. Her final choice to pull him onto the bed matters to me: she will not be put aside as an object after the bench. She wants to give, watch, take in his unguardedness too. That ending—her asleep on top of him, still joined to him—got me. It is tender and almost frighteningly complete in the moment.

The title, “Vee on the Bench,” is blunt and exact: she is literally placed on the object that has haunted this book’s imagery, but it also makes her the center of its purpose. The bench has become an architecture for her exposure, her choice, and her shame turning into pleasure. It recolors the earlier bench scenes; this is not a mysterious object anymore, but it is no less charged. *A Polite Invitation* still feels apt because every threshold is offered gently and Vee repeatedly enters it herself. *With a Long Spoon* feels darker here: I cannot yet say what it means, but the long, carefully made instrument between care and appetite feels ominous. The jacket’s “Every yes was freely given. That was the problem” is now painfully alive on the page. No cover tagline was given beyond the blurb’s closing line.

I want the aftermath next—not another escalation, but Vee awake in herself after this. Does she feel freer, frightened, ashamed, hungry, altered? And I badly want to know what she can say aloud to Randi, especially after a chapter where Vee has finally stopped pretending to herself.
