This made me ache for Pace more than I expected. He is not cold here; he is lonely, stubborn, and terribly convinced that the things he does ought to be legible enough to spare him the words. His thought that Vee might “love this place or never see it at all” got me immediately. He wants to share an ordinary, quiet place where he could actually hear her. And then he does not call. He does not say the thing. The warmth of his missing her keeps catching against that refusal.

Sheri is a bracing pleasure. I don’t suddenly distrust his feeling for Vee; his description of the air “crackl[ing]” and of her understanding “the whole idea” of his food feels helplessly real. But I am frustrated with him now in a more active way. He has made Vee’s need sound like a demand for a hollow lie when she has been asking to be met in language.

And Sheri’s own small story gives the chapter weight beyond being a conversation about Vee. Paige wanting her to call her family is not villainous, but Sheri’s grief is plainly not a thing she can repair by trying harder. Pace’s line, “People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice,” is tender, though I felt Sheri’s recoil from being given “the good stuff” by him. They know where each other’s sore places are and mostly do not paw at them.

There is no erotic scene, but the charge is still alive in how frankly Sheri talks about Vee’s body, and in Pace flushing while trying to say what Vee is to him. “The philosophy of your food” made me laugh with Sheri; it is absurd, and also exactly the thing I believe he means. Vee does understand what feeding her has meant. That makes it sadder that he cannot understand what naming her might mean.

“The Usual” lands first as their diner order, a little pocket of ritual where Pace can be known without explaining himself. By the end it feels more uneasy: his usual way of loving—doing, providing, withholding the vulnerable word—is exactly what has brought him here. The title did not give anything away, but it makes the chapter’s comfort feel like an old groove he may not be able to stay in.

*A Polite Invitation* still promises a welcome that may be more complicated than its gentleness first appears. *With a Long Spoon* feels increasingly ominous beside these people feeding one another, reaching for one another, and still keeping dangerous truths close. The tagline, “Every yes was freely given. That was the problem,” is painful here because Vee has freely given so much, and Pace seems to want her to accept his terms without naming what those terms leave out. My guess from here: he may finally have to decide whether his refusal protects honesty or protects himself.
