## Reader reaction

This hit me first as relief so intense it is almost frightening. Vee arrives wrecked, alone in the stripped dorm room, and Randi is immediately there: getting her out of Brooke’s sight, shutting a door, holding her while she cries. I felt the comfort of that hug—the “bitter orange, green at the edge” of Randi’s skin, the fact that she holds on “past where a hug stops.” And then I felt myself tense, because Randi’s comfort becomes interpretation so quickly. She does not merely soothe Vee; she tells Vee what Pace’s silence means, what he felt, what he will do, what Vee should do next.

I ache for Vee. Her thought that every mile home runs “toward a town that had Pace in it and not one inch of him for her” is brutal. Her fear of Sheri is plainly a fear-made picture, and Randi is right to make her admit that. But Randi goes much further than the page warrants when she says, “I know so,” about Pace not being done with Vee. I know Pace misses Vee from the last chapter, but Vee does not know that, and Randi does not appear to know it either. Her certainty feels soothing and dangerous: it gives Vee a story she desperately wants to inhabit.

Pace is absent but fills the chapter. I feel sympathy for him when Randi reframes Vee’s confrontation as “Name it or lose me,” because yes, Vee did put him in an impossible-feeling moment. But I do not accept Randi’s version that this makes the problem Vee’s fight alone. Vee asked a fair, direct question after being left with an intensely private relationship she cannot name. Pace’s silence was not nothing. His actual feelings may be deep, but Vee is not wrong for needing speech.

Randi is dazzling here—funny, tender, decisive, physically affectionate, observant enough to pull Vee back from inventing a blonde rival. Her “fear needs a face to point at” lands with real compassion. I also feel alarmed by how expertly she moves Vee from grief into arousal and a plan Randi has effectively authored: the coat, nothing beneath it, Randi’s black heels. “You just show up already being the answer” is hot, yes, and the scene knows exactly how Vee’s shame and desire tangle. But it is also chilling to hear Randi say, “Words are the whole war,” because words are exactly what Vee has finally learned she needs. Randi is advising her to answer Pace’s refusal of language with a spectacular sexual offering. That feels like a way of getting Vee back inside the beautiful private system that just hurt her.

The erotic charge is strong but not explicit: it peaks in Randi’s coaching around the coat and Vee’s admission, “And nothing under it.” The heat comes from Vee’s mortification flipping into wanting, and from Randi’s almost ceremonial permission. It works on me while making me uneasy, which is very much the book’s particular charge. Randi’s line, “You wouldn’t be showing him a thing he hasn’t asked you for on his knees,” is sexy in the immediate sense, but it also presses on the old discomfort: Vee’s body and Pace’s desire are repeatedly made into proof that she should override her own spoken fear.

The John story lands differently now that I know what Randi leaves out. Vee hears an enviable fairytale: a man naming children on the first day, a dress for a future daughter’s story, an easy lead, a future spoken aloud. It cuts her because it is everything Pace cannot say. Randi’s “Everywhere but the bedroom, and the bedroom’s the room I actually live in” is funny enough to make Vee laugh, but it is also a carefully cropped truth. Randi does not tell Vee that John gave her pleasure but left her emotionally cold, or that the person she was thinking about was Vee.

There is some friction in Randi’s logic. “That is not a man with another woman, gorgeous. That’s a man half-starved for the one he’s got” does not actually follow from Pace keeping his phone in another room. It is a seductive reassurance, not evidence. And “Pace is not going to open his own door, see what you brought him, and say no” feels like Randi putting words and entitlement into Pace’s future behavior. The scene earns my suspicion because Randi has already helped engineer Vee toward Pace without Vee’s knowledge, and now her warm authority is once again directing Vee’s choices.

The title, **“My Friend Randi,”** feels tender and ominous. On the surface, this is the chapter where Vee gets the friend she needs: Randi receives her crisis, makes her laugh, gives her a plan, and kisses her goodbye. But “my friend” also feels too small for what Randi is to Vee and too innocent for the amount of power Randi has over Vee’s emotional and erotic life. The title makes me hear Vee’s trust in her, and it makes me dread what that trust will cost when Vee learns the parts Randi has concealed.

**A Polite Invitation** keeps changing shape. The first invitation was into Pace’s house and bed; now Randi offers another invitation, not polite exactly but exquisitely persuasive: show up at his door bare under a coat and make peace without words. *With a Long Spoon* remains mysterious, though this chapter makes me think again of accepting warmth from someone while needing to be careful about what is being offered and why. The cover line—“Every yes was freely given. That was the trap.”—feels especially alive in Randi’s coaching. Vee will likely choose this plan; she is not physically forced. But she is in pain, hungry for Pace, ashamed, and guided by someone who knows how to turn all of that into a choice that feels like her own.

I absolutely want to keep reading, but I dread tonight. My guess from here: Vee will go to Pace in the coat and heels, and the reunion may be intensely erotic and temporarily healing. But it will not solve the question she asked. Worse, it may make her feel that she has to offer more body in exchange for the word Pace would not give.
