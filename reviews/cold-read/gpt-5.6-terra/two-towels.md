## Reader reaction

I felt a rush of relief and heat when Vee arrived and said, “I missed you,” then simply took the kiss she had been denied by rain and police lights. It works because it is hers: she has decided on the paved road that she wants it, and she acts. Pace meeting her—hand in her hair, “sure,” taking time—lands hard. But then he stops. That interruption makes me tense in the best way. Vee’s realization that stopping has always been her job with other boys is sad, and Pace stopping himself makes him even more attractive to me, while also keeping him strange and difficult to read.

The chapter’s erotic charge is sustained rather than explicit. It peaks first at the door, then becomes almost more intense at the bedroom door: Vee has intentionally worn satin underneath, leans toward the shut door, and waits. Pace does not open it. Her little private disappointment—“Something in her dropped”—hurt. I felt it with her. It is not that I think he owes her access to his room; rather, she has made herself vulnerable through expectation, and he leaves her holding that want. Then the dress offer transforms that same vulnerability into something enormous: he is asking to measure her in “good light,” down to what she would wear under it, so he can make something that fits her body exactly. The last “Yes” is deeply charged, because it is clearly not only about a dress.

Pace keeps becoming more impressive, and that is where my unease lives. His house is meticulous and warm; he cooks, makes furniture, has a serious mathematical practice, and holds a patent that speeds MRI imaging for frightened or sick people. His excitement about helping people get out of the machine sooner is genuinely lovely. I believed his joy there. His response to her asking whether his parents are rich—“No,” followed by “I license a patent”—also leaves a small closed place around his parents, but the text does not give me enough to make a suspicion out of it. It is simply another door he has not opened.

The house itself makes him feel startlingly real to Vee and to me: worn knives, the chipped old KitchenAid, orderly shoes, books with cracked spines, his own furniture, the unfinished chair. I liked that Vee can actually see and value what he has made. “Patience. Paying attention” is a very Pace answer, almost too perfectly Pace, but it lands because his whole house demonstrates it. And his explanation of error-correcting codes—“Say it more than once so the noise can’t kill it”—stopped me. It feels like a line I will carry forward, especially in a book where Vee has been handed so much truth mixed with concealment. At this point it reads as moving rather than ominous.

Still, he is too perfectly prepared for this moment. He remembered a dress Vee mentioned casually on a mountain, found silk, apparently knows how to sew a shirt and a gown, and has waited with everything arranged. Vee experiences that as care, and I feel the force of that care. I also feel the familiar threat underneath it: he has made something so beautiful and so large that saying no would be emotionally difficult. But she does say yes immediately and warmly; the page gives me no sign she is coerced in that instant. The cover tagline keeps pressing on that distinction. Every yes is still hers. The trap, whatever it is, cannot be a simple absence of consent.

I feel especially tender toward Vee here. She begins by worrying she is “bad at this” when she misses the driveway, braces for a kitchen to reveal some deprivation or performance, and nearly lets the burgundy silk disappoint her because it is not the exact magazine red. But she looks in the mirror, sees herself in relation to the color, and changes her mind. That is a small but beautiful act of perception. Pace has not copied the image she named; he has looked at *her*. And then she is allowed to want the gown openly. Her “I can’t sew” is such a poignant reflex: she hears a gift and immediately imagines the labor or inadequacy that might make her unable to receive it.

The title, **“Two Towels,”** initially sounded domestic and perhaps sexual—two people expected in a bathroom, perhaps. On the page it becomes a startling detail in Pace’s plain clean bathroom: “two towels on the rod.” I do not know what it means yet. It could be simple preparedness, or a small sign that he has made room for someone else; given what I know about Randi, it immediately catches at me. Yet Vee does not react to it, and I have no page-grounded reason to decide whose towel it is or whether it is significant at all. The title did make me notice it before I otherwise might have, so it slightly gave that detail away.

**A Polite Invitation** now feels very literal: Pace says “Come in,” then later gives the open-ended, careful invitation to let him make the dress. He does not force open his bedroom door or push Vee toward it. The series title, **With a Long Spoon**, still points toward a devil or danger I cannot name from the story itself; this house is warm, beautiful, and full of things made carefully by hand, which makes the ominous phrase feel more unsettling rather than less. The tagline is loud in this chapter. Vee’s final yes is eager and unmistakably free, but it comes after Pace has created a gift so personally attentive that it reaches straight into her oldest hunger: to be seen, cared for, and made beautiful without being shamed.

I badly want to keep reading. I want to see the measuring scene, and I dread how vulnerable it will make Vee feel. I want to know whether Pace will finally open his bedroom door, whether the two towels mean Randi has been physically present in this house, and what he is withholding about his parents. My guess from here is that making the dress will become both an intimate act and a threshold: Vee is moving farther into Pace’s private world, but she still does not know that Randi helped choose her.
