## Reader reaction

This chapter made me feel trapped in Vee’s panic before I could quite name it. Her question—“Is this what we are now?”—comes out ugly and loud because she has been holding so much down: not only the word *love*, but the fear that she is being exquisitely cared for without actually being chosen. I ache for her. She wants a public dinner, a name, some evidence that Pace exists with her outside his house and outside sex. Those are not unreasonable wants. But then she turns his genuine overwhelm in Chili’s into proof that he is ashamed of her, and I felt the painful inevitability of her making herself cruel before he can disappoint her.

Pace’s answer at first startled me with relief: “Where do you want to go tonight?” He does not punish or mock her for asking. Then, at Chili’s, the chapter lets me watch him fail in a way Vee cannot initially read. The fork-squaring, eyes drifting to the television, and flash of irritation make the noise and visual clutter feel physically unbearable for him. When he explains, “In a room like that I listen as hard as I know how and I still can’t hear you,” I believe him. I believe he was trying. That makes Vee’s accusation hurt, especially because she knows, underneath it, that *she* withdrew after the Rusty Nail.

But I do not feel completely soothed by his explanation, because it answers only one layer of what she asked. He can listen in his house. He can make room for her body, her appetite, her hair things, her flannel, her sleep. Yet when she asks whether they are “just sex,” whether they will “ever be seen in public again,” he solves the immediate practical request and then explains the sensory difficulty. He does not give her a relationship word. By the end, her realization that she cannot call him boyfriend, cannot explain him to her parents, and cannot tell whether he belongs to her as she belongs to him feels devastatingly earned.

The erotic charge is very high here—Heat 3—and it works on me for much of the scene because it is so entangled with Vee’s hunger to be wanted beyond patience and care. Her wish for him to lose control is intense, and when he finally does after bringing her to orgasm, it lands as the release she has been craving. But the chapter then makes that release emotionally bleak: she gets his body, his urgency, even his surrender, and still lies awake needing the word he has not given. The sex does not resolve the conflict; it drowns it temporarily. That is effective and sad. “She came with his name in her mouth and not the other thing” is the sharpest line in the chapter for me.

I trust Pace’s account of Chili’s more than Vee does. The text earns that through his behavior: he is visibly effortful, not smug or withholding, and relief returns in the truck because the overstimulation is gone. Still, I remain suspicious of the wider situation because Pace is such an expert at meeting the need directly in front of him. Vee asks to go out; he takes her out. She needs reassurance; he says he is listening. She needs sex; he gives her extraordinary sex. But the question beneath all those needs—what are they, and does he love her?—goes unanswered. Given everything I know about the hidden beginning and his earlier use of her body as evidence against her words, I cannot let his tenderness erase that.

Vee also has a difficult, newly visible possessiveness here: “She belonged to him,” she thinks, and the question is whether he belongs to her. I understand the longing, but it makes me flinch. Her language of being “his” is erotic and emotionally sincere to her, yet it is frightening because she is still so unable to claim an equal, speakable place in his life. She has “forgiven” him for a coldness he did not actually commit, partly because forgiveness lets her feel powerful and lets the night have “that clean shape.” Then the shape falls apart. I am worried by how quickly she repairs her own discomfort into a story where she is generous and he has done enough.

The title, **“Under the Rug,”** feels immediately clear by the end: Vee tries to sweep the whole fight—and the larger question of their relationship—under the rug by deciding she has forgiven him. The sex helps bury it for a while. But the unease keeps coming back underneath her, where she cannot settle it. It also makes me think of all the existing hidden things: the staged beginning, the unresolved moments she stores as “small flickers,” the words neither of them says. The title did not give the emotional turn away in advance, but it now colors the entire chapter as an argument that has been covered rather than solved.

*A Polite Invitation* feels darker from here. Pace’s invitation—into dinner, bed, his home, domestic life—can be sincere and still leave Vee unable to ask for the basic language she needs. *With a Long Spoon* still means little concretely to me, except that the title keeps making intimacy feel like something one approaches carefully because there is danger at the other end. And “Every yes was freely given. That was the trap” is painfully alive here: Vee says yes to going back to bed, to being his, to accepting reassurance, but she is saying yes while the central question remains unasked and unnamed.

I very much want to keep reading because this is no longer just a vague dread about the original manipulation. Vee herself is beginning to feel the limits of the lovely enclosed world Pace has made. My guess from here is that she will keep trying to make the relationship legible—perhaps through being seen publicly, perhaps through Cassie, perhaps through a more direct question—but I dread how much she may blame herself before she lets herself ask it.
