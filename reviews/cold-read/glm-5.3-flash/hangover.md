## Reader reaction

What a chapter to give me after the warmth of the last stretch — it opens as comedy and closes with my chest hurting.

The hangover itself is lovely. The melted ice water with the paper towel under it so it wouldn't mark the wood — that detail undid me before the bread even arrived. The whole morning is Pace doing care in advance, again: the undershirt he drew over her head while she was gone, the kiss on the forehead after he thought she was asleep. She reconstructs how much of the night was just him, quietly handling her, and I felt that land with her.

The Sheri callback is a gift. Her reeling drunk-sincere "I like her so much" a second time, sober and ruined, made me laugh out loud, and then Pace's easy "That way too" — he never pretends not to have caught what she means, and I love him for it. But then the chapter turns, and it turns on the plainest line he's said in the whole book: "Sheri's my friend. My one friend. There isn't anyone else."

That stopped me the way it stopped her. Because we've watched him work a room — the roadhouse, the loft, the butcher-paper sign, every person standing taller — and to be told the man who does all that has exactly one friend on earth, a five-foot woman who could never want him, is devastating. And his explanation isn't self-pity, which makes it worse. Women misunderstand the friendship. The men's window "opens once" and his never did — fifteen in a building of grown men, then eighteen already finished, the patent, the money, the house. "It isn't tragic. It's just a door that didn't open, and then you're grown." And Vee — and I — both hear the good order of it as the tell. He has made his loneliness into a neat explanation the way he makes everything neat, and the neatness is the wound.

And then Vee does the arithmetic I was doing, and the book lets her do the hardest part herself: the one who carried all of it was Daphne, and the only reason a nineteen-year-old had that much of a fifteen-year-old is the thing Vee keeps pressing back down under her tongue. The book refuses to hand her the word, and refuses it to me too, and holding the two true things stacked and unreconciling — the best two years he had, and the wrong shape they were — hurt exactly the way the page wanted it to. I ached for him at eighteen, the imagined empty Thanksgiving, teaching himself to cook because there was no one to eat with.

Her answer is exactly right and exactly hers: not sorry, not comfort-words, just crossing the counter and holding him the way Sheri held him in the taproom, whole weight committed. And he holds her back and looks over her head, thinking, neither happy nor sad, and the bread cools and nobody says anything. That's the ending the chapter earned. No declaration, no fix. The withheld word is still withheld, and now I understand it a little better and it's heavier for it.

What moved in me: Pace has always been the safe one — the care is real, I've trusted it completely. This chapter doesn't shake that trust, it deepens the sadness under it. He's more isolated than even I'd credited, and Vee is now, functionally, his second friend in the world. The weight of that on her — and she feels it, "nobody could hold that much of somebody" — is a new pressure on the whole book, and I felt it settle.

The title: "Hangover" is literal — the ciders, the headache — but it's also the morning-after of everything: of Sheri's story, of the intimacy accelerating, and now of this plain fact about his life sitting in the kitchen with them. Nothing about last night is fully metabolized yet.
