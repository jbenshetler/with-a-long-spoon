## Reader reaction

## The felt read

Oh, this chapter is small and quiet and it wrecked me. After the last stretch — the mixer, the truck at one in the morning, Pace extracting the truth inch by inch, "coming-due" and its live neck and the kiss she took on purpose — I braced for more escalation. Instead the book opens the door on Vee and Cassie, in a dorm room, still in their coats, and doesn't let either of them take it off. That's the whole shape of the thing: coats on, nobody undressed, the night still on their bodies.

And it's Cassie. It was always going to have to be Cassie. I have trusted her without reservation since the checkpoint, and this chapter pays that trust off without spending a dime of it — she doesn't lecture, doesn't interrogate, doesn't ask what happened. She asks **"Is this what you want?"** and that question is better and worse than anything anyone else could have asked, because it isn't about Randi, it isn't even about Pace — it's the one question Vee cannot answer, and we watch her actually go look for the answer and come up empty. "She reached for it. There was a shape there, close and large, and she came up against the edge of it and stepped back off before she could make out its size." That's the truest sentence about her whole situation the book has written. She can't even look at the size of it.

And what does she do instead? She does exactly what she did to Pace in "Boyfriend" — she grabs the nearest load-bearing thing and swings the matter onto the other person. "You don't like her. That's what this is." And Cassie — God, Cassie — just steps over the plank without looking down. "You didn't have dinner." I made an actual sound. That's love in the only language this book's quietest character speaks: not the question you can't answer, but the box of Cocoa Pebbles you can. And Vee's "Yeah" — "the first whole thing she'd said since she walked in, because here was a want she could find — a want the size of a bowl" — that broke me a little. The whole chapter is about the difference between wants she can name-size and the one she can't, and the cereal is the only one that comes in a holdable size tonight.

My feeling about Cassie moved, or rather deepened into certainty: the "worry under it, the part that wasn't flat" is the first time I've heard actual fear in her, and it frightened me more than anything Randi has done, because Cassie doesn't spend worry. And Vee — I ache for her. She walked in carrying the neck, the word *having*, the kiss she dodged and the one she couldn't, and she has no one she can tell this to, because the one person she tells everything to *is the thing*. The chapter knows that and makes the doorway hold it: "the question sat in the warm room like a third person nobody had offered a chair." And the paranoid spiral about how much Cassie saw on the walk — whether the hug "looked from a few feet off," the neck that "must show... like a postmark" — that's so exactly her, the girl who can't stop auditing what her body has told on her, from the coat lining to the stall at the Italian place.

No swoon in the melt sense, and none needed — this is the morning-after tenderness kind, the warmth of being known. The heat of the chapter is all residue: the burn she stays aware of, the night still on her skin. Which is exactly right. The erotic charge is a live wire with nothing plugged into it, and that hum is the point.

What I dread: that Cassie sees more than Vee knows, and that this is the beginning of the two people who love Vee truthfully closing around her while Randi steers. What I want: Vee, someday, standing on the question long enough to answer it. I don't expect it soon, and the book isn't going to rescue her into it. Fine. I'll stay.

The title — "some-of-mine." Cassie's cereal. The answer Vee can give. But I read the lowercase "some" against the whole of Vee: how much of her is some-of-mine to Pace, some-of-mine to Randi, some to Cassie, none of it whole to anyone — and Randi, who just got told "she's like that with everyone," who has been collecting her some at a time since the engineered lawn. And "Cocoa Pebbles" is Cassie all the way down: she doesn't offer to make anything, doesn't perform comfort, just opens what she has. First sighting of the cereal box as an object, and it lands like the apple did in chapter one — a want, rationed, answered.

Friction: almost none. If anything I wanted one more beat — I'd have taken another page of these two in this room. That's not a flaw; it's the chapter ending exactly where its honesty ends, on the box held out, before either of them has to be bigger than they were tonight.
