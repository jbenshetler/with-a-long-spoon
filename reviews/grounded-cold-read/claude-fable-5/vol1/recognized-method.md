The spank. I have to start there, because my body got there before my head did, exactly the way Vee's does. A tickle fight over dishes, her one good smack across the seat of his jeans, and then his hand — "warm and wet from the dishwater, open and flat across the curve of her" — and her whole body answering before she can get a thought in front of it: the nipples drawn to aching points, the "soundless *oh*," "her body saying two or three things at once that she had not agreed to have it say." I felt that arrive the way she does — no warning, no name ready. And then the line that pulled the floor out from under my warmth: "He had gone still. He was watching her face."

Because I know things Vee doesn't. I was in the bed two chapters ago when Randi said "She's not ready, though. She'll get there. You'll want to take your time," and he said "I want her to get there on her own." So when he turns off the tap — the tap, before the chase, that small deliberate click that changes the quality of the room — and then watches her face while the sting turns to heat, I can't read it as innocent play, and I can't quite read it as engineering either. The page holds it exactly ajar: it *was* play, she swatted first, and he let it become something, and then he said nothing and filed what he saw. That silence is his patience and it is also the method, and the chapter never once names it. My dread did all the work. That's this book at its best and its most quietly frightening.

And yet — the apology scene melted me, fully, no reservations. The HUMBLE PIE label, trimmed with the good scissors at three in the morning, "her contrition out in front of her where they could both look at it and not have to be too naked about it" — that's Vee to the bone, funny and terrified and self-aware a half-beat too late. Then the heels. She put on Randi's cognac heels as armor — "It helped to put them on, as a uniform helps" — and the armor betrays her at the door: she's taller than him, and she reads it instantly as "one more disrespect laid at his feet" and starts to fold herself down to undo the buckles on his porch. And he lifts the pie out of her sliding hands, stands her back up, resets the scarf — and says "Leave them on. Beauty like yours made for the gods and loaned out — I'm not going to insult the loan by complaining I have to look up." It's extravagant to the edge of too much, and it worked on me completely, because it's his register exactly — the true extravagant thing in the plain voice — and because "loan" lands on the borrowed heels without his knowing (or does he know?). Which brings me to the detail that gave me an actual chill: his two fingers laying the scarf back, "a half-second longer over the silk than the errand needed." Vee reads it as *the scarf is working*. I sat there wondering whether he recognizes Randi's silk under his fingers — Randi dressed Vee for this door, and he touched the silk a beat too long. The page doesn't say. I'm leaving it ajar and it's humming.

The best writing in the chapter is the apology collapsing into the real one. Her "small clean machine" runs perfectly — and he says "Okay," gently, and then "But what happened?" Because "he could not do the thing other people did where they let a closed door stay closed" — a line that made me flinch, given Daphne and the door he once violated and paid for, given his own "Not tonight" over the scar. He opens her door in a way he won't let his own be opened, and it's also, on the page, pure love: he genuinely wants to know, "which was worse." And what comes out of her is the true thing, disordered, the blonde's lilt in her own hated mouth, ending on the sentence that broke my heart: "She handed me a lie and I picked it up because some part of me was just standing there waiting for one." That's the mother's voice, the floozie machinery, named by Vee herself more nakedly than she's ever managed. And his answer — "Thank you for telling me what she said" — such a strange, perfect, un-closing thing to be thanked for. The final image of the apology, that he "simply reached over and lifted it out of her hands, like the pie," is the kind of sentence this book earns and spends exactly right.

Where the unease genuinely lives for me — page-earned, not manufactured — is the Sunday-evening beat: her in his clothes, washed in his soap and shampoo, "nothing of her own left anywhere on her skin, and she had not minded it. She had gone a little still over it." The book flags it itself, with her own small stillness. Head to foot she smells of him. After the flannel-rationing, after "the last clean thing in the world" being one bra saved for Monday — the erasure is tender and total and I don't fully trust it, and I don't think Vee does either, for one still second.
