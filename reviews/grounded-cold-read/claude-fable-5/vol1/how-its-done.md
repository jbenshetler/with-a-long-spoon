# Cold read (grounded) — How It's Done

## Reader reaction

This chapter scared me more than anything since the bench, and it did it without a single door closing, without anyone touching anyone below the jaw. It's just lunch. Tomato soup. And I watched a girl get opened like a letter.

Let me be honest about the order I felt things. First: pleasure. The Mercedes sequence is gorgeous — the window that drops itself half an inch to meet the door, "like the car was tucking itself in around her," Vee's father's voice ("keep the mileage down, it didn't come for free") set down where she sets those thoughts. I loved being in that car. I loved the two girls on the Hahn steps and Vee letting herself "become, for that beat, a person who rode in cars like this with people like Randi." That's Vee — she keeps no transcript, only the feeling, and the feeling is glorious, and I can't blame her for one second of it.

Second: heat, and it's Randi's. The tickling story worked on me exactly the way it worked on Vee — not the beautiful idiot, who is nobody, but the picture of Randi "naked and helpless and laughing... that finished surface finally cracked all the way open," and the page names precisely what it did to Vee: "a heat she had no shelf for and would have died before naming at this table." That heat has been in Vee since the quad, since the daylight-forbidden register, and here it surfaces again and gets shoved under a joke ("you're banned from my whole bedroom") in one breath. And then Vee's real question slips out from "nearer than she liked" — "Weren't you scared, though? Not being able to move?" — and Randi hands her the philosophy across the table like a communion wafer: "A little helpless can be a very good thing. Done right." Reader, my skin crawled and I leaned in anyway. That is this book's whole trick and it has never been more concentrated than in that line.

Because — third — the dread. I know what I'm watching. I remember the bench. The tickling story is Randi's actual life with the serial numbers filed off: wrists bound, control gone, framed as a lark with an anonymous man, and framing herself single *again* (the "catastrophe date" all over). Whether the scarf story even happened, I genuinely can't tell — it might be true, it might be built for this lunch — and the page doesn't say, so I won't. But its function on the page is unmistakable: it teaches Vee that helplessness is a delight, and it opens the door for the extraction that follows. And the extraction is a master class. "Nobody measures over a bra... it's not a *thing*, it's just how it's done" — the title, right there, and it works because it's exactly the mercy Vee's shame was begging for. If it's just how it's done, "then she hadn't done anything, then she could say it." Randi absolves and harvests in the same gesture. "Which hip?" "The left one." "All the best ones start at the left hip" — the page tells me flat out it "meant nothing at all and landed like a benediction," and Vee is "warmed and confirmed and led, one small *yeah* at a time, down and down." I felt led too. That's the horror of it — the interrogation is also genuinely the most attentive listening Vee gets from anyone but Cassie, and some of it, I still believe, is real.

And then the moment that's going to stay with me: Vee at the very edge of her deepest secret — the wet satin, the thing she never told Cassie, "folded up the smallest and pushed the deepest" — mouth open, giving it — and Randi closes the door. "You don't have to tell me." "It was gentle. It was kind. It was a door closing very softly." I do not know what that was, and I'm refusing to decide. It could be technique — leave the want half-out with nowhere to go, keep the pressure banked. It could be the tightness from a page earlier finally winning: because there was a leak, and I saw it — "something had gone tight behind it, some muscle she was holding, and her thumb had found the base of her water glass and was working the edge of it." That thumb against an edge is the bottle cap under the thumbnail from the bench night, and it undid me. Something in Randi hurt, hearing Vee glow about being looked at by Pace — jealousy of him, jealousy of her, wanting one of them, wanting both — the page holds it open and so do I. Vee's own double-read of that face ("she thinks I'm awful" / "maybe Randi was *moved*") is heartbreaking because the page marks her choosing the kinder reading *because she wants it to be true*, and I honestly think both readings are wrong and neither is.

The check: Vee paying, "the one thing she would not do at this table was let Randi buy her seat at it," the debit card and the number she'll feel on Thursday, tipping well — I was so proud of her and so sad. Randi watching "warm and easy" and not reaching again — clocking the pride and permitting it. Even Vee's dignity gets read and filed.

And the kiss. God. The convertible singing washed me clean along with Vee — I was fully in the swoon, top down, "the best one of her life" — and then "Come here," and the hand at the hinge of her jaw, and the kiss that isn't the cheek thing: "a small drawing-back, the barest pause, and then back again with a little more, a second press that had something in it the first one hadn't." I sat straight up, because I've seen that exact kiss before — it's how Randi kissed Pace after the collision, the second one in her bedroom register. Vee gets the same signature and doesn't know what she's holding. And then the ending, which is as good as anything in this book: two fingers to her own mouth "without deciding to," the frantic explaining-away ("*French*... that's just how women like her say goodbye" — she has it "explained away almost before her mouth was her own again," what a line), and then the cold scan of the dorm windows, counting the dark ones, anyone behind any of them. Her first response to being kissed by a woman is desire's aftertouch; her second is surveillance-shame in her mother's church. That's Vee's whole interior in ten lines, and it ends the chapter on ice.

Small frictions, honestly minor: the narrator naming the technique ("led, one small *yeah* at a time, down and down") edges toward telling me what I could already feel, though Vee's later "unable in the swim of it to remember quite *how* she'd been caught" earns most of it back. Sinatra doing "the one where he calls it witchcraft" is right on the nose — and I grinned anyway, so I can't fully complain. And a note rather than a complaint: this is the third retelling of the fitting in as many chapters (Cassie, the dining hall, now this), and it's the one that justifies the run — the same story, told to a different listener, becomes a different act entirely. The debrief beat was starting to repeat; this weaponizes it.

The titles. "How It's Done" is Randi's line verbatim — the easy authority that dissolves shame so the secret can be extracted — and it doubles, queasily, as the chapter itself: this is how it's done, the whole craft of opening a person. It didn't give anything away going in; it detonated on contact with the bra line. *A Polite Invitation* has never felt more literal — lunch, an invitation, flawlessly polite, and underneath it a recruitment interview — and *With a Long Spoon* keeps whispering that Vee is supping with something she can't see. My memory of the jacket's promise — that nothing would be taken from her she didn't hand over — is doing terrible work now: Vee handed over everything at that table, gladly, one small yeah at a time. The handing-over *is* the taking. That's the book.

What I want next, and dread: I assume (guess from here) everything Vee said travels back to Pace tonight — the fitting, "he looked at me and I was fine," maybe even the left hip — and I don't know whether Randi will deliver it as intelligence or hoard some of it for herself; the tightness makes me think something at that table stopped being fully the plan's. I want to know what the white-gold band at her wrist is — first time I've noticed it, "the one fixed thing," never off — and I'm trying not to guess. I dread Vee lying awake tonight doing arithmetic on that kiss. And I dread, specifically, that nobody was in any of those windows — because part of me has started wanting someone, Cassie, anyone, to have seen.
