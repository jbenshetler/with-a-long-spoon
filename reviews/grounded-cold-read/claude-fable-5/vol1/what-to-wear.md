## Reader reaction

This chapter is pure pleasure to sit inside — and it scared me more than anything since the bench, without a single thing going wrong on the page.

The pleasure first, because it's real. The athletic dining hall "went gold and forgiving," and the whole scene has that light on it. Kayla arrives fully formed in about four lines — "That's not a message, that's a ransom note" is the funniest thing this book has done — and Meg, who's been a walk-on since the collision chapter, turns out to be a person: level, fork-pointing, following "the Pace situation in installments like her shows." And Vee among them is a Vee I hadn't quite seen. With Randi she shrinks ("We have stats together"); with Cassie she's tender; here she's "laughing with her whole body, loud and easy, the kind that made the next table look over and then want in." She has a Theo she turned down kindly. She has a whole life. That widened world made me love her more — and made the machinery around her feel bigger, because now there's a third audience of people who don't know, and I count myself as the only one who does.

That phrase, though — "laughing with her whole body." That is, almost word for word, how she looked coming out of the dining hall when Pace pointed and Randi said "Yes." Seeing the exact image again, in a dining hall again, with strangers warming to her again, put a cold thread through the gold. That's the thing that got her chosen. The book didn't wink at it. It just let me recognize it, and I did, and it hurt.

The retelling of the truck story is technically the second time I've heard it secondhand (Cassie got it in Rye), and I'll be honest that my first reflex was "again?" — but the chapter earns the repeat by making the telling itself the subject: "She could hear the story getting better in the telling and did not care." That line delighted me and unsettled me in the same breath. So did "Vee felt the smile arrive before she'd cleared it" — that is Randi's grammar, the smile "arranging," and watching Vee acquire even a benign version of a face gave me a small chill I want to flag as real, page-earned, and very quiet. Same with the phone "faceup, and her eyes were on it before she chose to look" — chapter 5's Vee left it facedown and made herself wait. She's not waiting anymore.

New Pace data, delivered at distance: he's a powerlifter, "an actual one," lifts first thing, writes six hours and stops — "'My writing's no good after that.'" It recolors the forearms, the steady hands, Vee's "I think there's a whole situation under there." And it deepens the picture of a man of absolute self-regulation, which is exactly the picture that makes "He's not pushing. It's the opposite" so effective on her. Her monologue about that — "climbing the walls over a man asking me what I want for dinner… He's different. I know how that sounds" — is the truest thing in the chapter, and Meg's gentle answer is the chapter's one sting: "Everybody's different at the start… Sometimes they even are." Vee "let it go by. It didn't catch on anything." It caught on me. Meg has no idea how right and how wrong she is at once — Pace is different, and the difference is worse than the sameness she means.

Then the text. "Would you come to my house Saturday. Two o'clock." Reading that, my stomach actually dropped, because I know the house. I know what's in it, disguised as a table with its lid on, "waiting for the next time." Vee has "never once been through his door, and now there was a door" — the door motif walking right up to her — and the girls' table erupts in delighted scandal while I sit here knowing that the barest, politest message this book has produced is the book's title arriving in the flesh. Kayla's "He wants you for the afternoon. Bring a change of clothes" is meant as comedy and lands as prophecy, and I honestly can't tell yet which register Saturday will be in.

Erotic charge: nothing happens and it still simmers — the retold striptease, the shirt "folded on her pillow at this exact hour," Vee saying "It's making me lose my mind" into the table. Anticipation-heat, and it works.

Suspicion: none of the new people. Kayla and Meg are exactly what they look like. The dread is entirely structural — mine, not the scene's — plus those two hairline cracks: the story getting better in the telling, the smile arriving before she'd cleared it. And the absences register: no Cassie in this chapter at all, not even named, and no Randi. The friend who vets and the friend who engineered are both offstage while Vee's other world cheers her toward the door.

The title: "What to Wear" plays innocent on the surface — Kayla's scandalized "What did he say to wear?", Meg's closing question about what says his house without saying trying too hard. But it's been seeded all through Vee: the cardigan over the better shirt, his flannel worn home, the russet silk dress she set back on the shelf on the mountain. What she wears is her whole negotiation with being looked at. And it points straight at Saturday without telling me anything — the right kind of title. A Polite Invitation has never been more literal than that six-word text; and With a Long Spoon keeps ringing under it — she's being invited to sup, and nobody's handed her the spoon. My checkpoint's memory of the jacket — that she's "quietly starving," that nothing would be taken she didn't hand over — is exactly the tension of this chapter: she's going to walk through that door gladly, dressed on purpose.

What I want next: Saturday, obviously — I turned the last page hungry for it. Guesses from here: this is her first time in the house, quite possibly the consummation, and I half-dread, half-expect the bench to be in the room with them as furniture she doesn't understand — or Randi to be somewhere in the day, because the plan has always had three people in it. What I dread most is smaller: that Vee will set her bag down on a table that isn't a table.
