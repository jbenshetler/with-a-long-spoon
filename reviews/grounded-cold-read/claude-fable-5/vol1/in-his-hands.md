# Cold read (grounded) — In His Hands

## Reader reaction

This chapter had me purring for almost its whole length — and then one sentence put a cold finger on the back of my neck, and I've been circling it since.

The purr first, because it's real and it's most of the chapter. The opening is lovely in a way this book has earned: her folded down over him inside the red curtain of her own hair, then flat on her back with "no grace left to spend," and him rolling square across her to lay his head on her breast, feet off the far edge of the bed. That image — a big careful man arranged crosswise like a boy just to rest his cheek on her skin — melted me. Her little running theory ("*They're not about sex for him*") getting gently spoiled by her own nipple and then by his hardness "off to the side where his hips had come to lie across her" is exactly the register I love in Vee: fond, wry, a half-beat behind her own body, and delighted about it. "*He's all mine*" landed warm. I know things she doesn't that make that sentence ache, but in the moment I just felt her happiness.

Then the photograph. This is where the chapter's real weight sits, and it plays out with a precision that made me hold my breath twice — once in swoon, once in dread.

The swoon: "You don't have to. I like you exactly like this" — and he catches her wrist "easy, not to stop her, only to say it." That's this man in one gesture: a hand that could stop and doesn't, used only to tell the truth. And then the posing — refusing her self-arrangement ("No, not like that. You'll have to wait") and setting her himself, a kiss laid at each place his hands put her: shoulder, hip, knee. It's the fitting all over again — the riser, the tape, being handled into a shape and worshiped at every waypoint — and my body registered it the same way Vee's does. When he grins at her over the phone "like a boy who'd gotten away with something," I grinned too. The picture itself — "her as his eyes had her" — and her not wanting to give the phone back is one of the tenderest things the book has done with the being-seen thread. And then: "she believed him, and believing him was the brave part. She sent it... and gave him herself to keep." I felt the bravery. I also felt the tagline detonate quietly under the floorboards: every yes freely given.

Because here is the sentence with the cold finger in it. She sets her condition: "Only if you promise you'll never share me with anyone." And he answers: "Never. I'd never share your photograph."

Read it once and it's a promise. Read it twice and it's a *narrower* promise than the one she asked for. She said *me*. He said *your photograph*. This book has taught me — the bench that passed as a table, the answers that are exact without being whole, the pointing game itself — that Pace's speech is precise the way his joinery is precise. I know, and Vee doesn't, that "sharing her" is arguably the founding project of this whole romance: Randi chose her; Randi proposed a threesome; Randi is debriefed after every date. So when he substitutes "your photograph" for "me," I can't tell if I just watched an honest man make a sweet promise or a scrupulous man refuse to make a false one. The page doesn't resolve it — his "Never" comes first, broad and warm, and only then the specification — and I'm leaving it exactly as ajar as the page leaves it. But I noticed. The text earned that notice; I didn't bring it in with me. It's the most *A Polite Invitation* moment in the chapter: nothing false said, everything true withheld.

The kitchen scene is the book's feeding motif at full flower and I ate it like Vee eats now. "The pan said so, loud at first, then settling to a steady talk"; the lemon "in a fine bright rain"; the wine glugged into the pan and her genuinely sad about it — this is some of the best cooking prose in a book full of it. And the loop closes beautifully: he watches her first bite "with the exact look he'd described to her in bed" — the fantasy he confessed an hour earlier, enacted in front of her, and she recognizes it. "She had used to just eat. Now she tasted... and she knew exactly whose doing that was." That's the transformation thesis of the whole Vee arc in one line, and it's both gorgeous and — if I let the aggregate in — a little vertiginous. He has remade her palate, her posture, her drawer, her walk. She's grateful for all of it. So am I, mostly. That "mostly" is the book working.

Then Thanksgiving, and the second cold spot. "This is my home" is lovely; "I don't go back" is something else — "each word set down clean and apart from the last," the ease gone "level and exact." The checkpoint in my head has always held that the parents are a door that closes, but this is the hardest that door has ever shut on the page, and Vee does the wise, loving thing: takes her hand off it, lets the quiet be kind. The detail of him moving her wine an inch closer "as if that were the thing that had wanted doing" — his apology-language, wordless as the drawer — got me. And the Sheri beat is nicely handled: the jealous flicker "small and low and all her own," then "She's alone," and the flicker goes out. I like that Vee's jealousy exists — she's a person, not a saint — and I like that it's answered by his plain decency. (I, of course, know Sheri knows about *both* women, and that a Thanksgiving table with Sheri and Vee at it would be a table with a secret sitting in the third chair. The chapter doesn't say Vee's invited. I noticed that too.)

One more small thing that hummed for me: Vee at the mirror, working "until she'd found the version of herself she wanted him to keep." That's a miniature of Randi's face-making — the built face, the curated self offered instead of the wrecked true one — and Pace gently declines it in both women's cases, preferring the wrecked and the real ("I like you exactly like this"). Whether Vee's version is a healthy playfulness where Randi's is a fortress, the echo is there and it prickled.

Friction: almost none. The cooking passage runs long but earns it — it's characterization, not garnish. If I strain, "believing him was the brave part" tips slightly toward telling me what the act meant, but it's in her voice and I believed she'd think it. No thumb on the scale that I felt.

The title: "In His Hands" is doing at least four jobs and I admire all of them — her body posed by his hands, her phone in his hands as he shoots, the photograph now permanently *in his hands*, the meal made by his hands. And under all of it: her trust, herself given him to keep. It didn't spoil anything; it deepened on finishing. After that photograph promise, though, "in his hands" has a second weather to it — what's in someone's hands can be handed on. *A Polite Invitation* keeps meaning more each chapter: the picture was her own idea, her own offer, freely made — and the tagline ("Every yes was freely given. That was the problem") is starting to feel less like a warning about him and more like a description of how this whole thing is built. Nothing is taken. Everything is offered.

What I want and dread: I want Thanksgiving — Sheri and pie and whatever "I don't go back" is hiding. I dread the photograph. Guess from here, plainly marked as a guess: that picture, or the fact of it, reaches Randi — either shown, or confessed at a brunch where Vee gives it up herself one small yeah at a time — and the word "share" comes back to this book with its full meaning attached. Second guess: the parents and the thigh scar are one story, and "Not tonight" has a date on it that's getting closer.
