# Cold read (grounded) — Leave No Trace

*scene: scenes/leave-no-trace.md · model: claude-fable-5 · memory: ck-ch010 (no window) · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

This one got me. I want to say that first, plainly, before I let the rest of what I know back into the room: this chapter swept me up almost without resistance, and the place it peaked — Vee on her knees on the bench seat of that truck, unbuttoning her wet shirt with her eyes locked on his — is maybe the hottest and truest thing the book has done with her yet, and she did it. Nobody did anything to her. She did it because she wanted to.

Start with the climb, because the chapter earns the truck by walking me up a mountain first. The rendering is physical in the way I trust — the borrowed half-size-big boots (Meg's, and I remembered Cassie's "Borrow good ones" and winced), the hot spot working at her heel, "push and lift, push and lift." And then the boot scene, which quietly wrecked me: her stomach clenching because he's going to take her sock off, the mother's verdict rising — "what kind of girl lets a man" — and before the shame can even finish assembling itself, he's already done it, matter-of-fact, moleskin warmed under his thumb, boot re-laced a different boot, "Stand." And then he apologizes to her — "Should've done that at the lot. I'm sorry. I didn't think to ask about the boots." The clench "let go without her catching it go." That's the whole Pace pattern in miniature: competence arriving faster than her shame can, taking up no credit. I felt it work on me the way it works on her, and I want to be honest that I mostly just liked it.

The summit paragraph is one long held breath and I held it. The lake "holding the silver sky in it flat as a poured thing," the ridges burning, and the line that lifted the whole passage past pretty: the world "did not care that she was standing on the edge of it... and that not-caring was somehow the most beautiful part of all of it." For a girl who has been watched — by a mother, by a voice, by herself — the ecstasy of something vast that doesn't care is exactly right. And then he's caught looking not at the valley but "at her face in front of the valley," and he lets her catch him, and gives her the rest of the looking alone. That's the courtship in one gesture: witnessed, then given privacy. I melted. I'm supposed to say so.

The cherry-pit beat is the chapter's moral heart and it's where the title lives. She spits the pit, he says "We take nothing and leave nothing" — gentle — picks it up, lays out wax paper, and then eats a cherry himself and sets his own pit beside hers without watching her learn. "She had it now. She had it and he hadn't made her small to give it to her." Against a lifetime of the no one taught you voice, the common voice, that sentence is devastatingly tender. This is the thing Pace does that the mother never did: instruction without humiliation.

And the dress. She shows him a want — russet silk, "like the red of a brick... like the one" — hears the reach in her own voice, and puts it back on the shelf herself: "There's a black dress in the back of my closet." He says only, with no weight on it at all, "That sounds like a beautiful dress," and doesn't pick it up again. Here is where my double vision kicked in, and I think the text has earned it: I know this man collects her wants. The tutoring, the ordering for her, the whole architecture — he is a cataloguer of what she reaches for and sets down. Something in me went cold and warm at once: I would bet (a guess from here, nothing more) that the russet dress comes back — bought, produced, arranged — and that when it does I won't be able to tell whether it's love or craft, because the book has taught me it's both.

Now the truck. This is the scene I'll carry. He hands her his only dry shirt — the flannel packed for himself, because "he showered at the gym and had no house here to go to" — and then gets out and stands in the rain with his back to the truck so she can change unwatched. "His shirt, and then the truck. Both." The doubled gift, warmth and privacy, and he says nothing about either. And her response is not gratitude — it's desire, and desire of the most interesting kind this book has shown me: I want him to see me. What follows is the interior I read for. The mother's voice comes up with everything it has — "Girls in trucks. What kind of. Three dates. Your mother would" — and instead of stopping her, "the voice was the heat and the heat was the voice." She wants to be the very thing the voice names, with him, watched. When floozy lands "in her mother's exact word" and her thighs press once, hard, on their own — that's the chapter saying out loud what it's been whispering since the froyo cup: her shame and her want are wired into the same circuit, and she has found a man in front of whom she can throw the switch herself. It's explicit without a single act of sex, and it's scorching. Her control of it is the eros: "she put her eyes on his and held them there," the crooked finger, the fourth button she could have done by feel — "She looked down anyway, and his eyes went with hers." She's directing him. After ten chapters of May I, she is the one granting permissions, and he receives it perfectly: "his face was want and it was gladness and it was a stillness she had never seen on anyone. He didn't lean in. He didn't lift a hand." That restraint read as reverence and I believed it.

And then the turn that made me ache: she reaches for his hand to start the next thing, and it's shaking. He'd been shivering — the whole time, through the whole gift of his dry shirt and the whole gift of her body, "a long way from the question of whether he was cold." She pulls his freezing hand against her warm belly under the flannel and says his name small. For once she is the one who notices, the one who warms, the one who says "We should go. Get warm. Please." The care ran the other direction and it landed like a first.

The ending is a small cruelty done well. She turns to him at the curb with something "bigger than any words she had for it," gets out one syllable — "Pace—" — and the campus cop's siren bark takes the sentence. He's pulled away before she can finish; she's left under the overhang in his flannel, wet shirt balled in her hand, moleskin still holding, looking at where the truck was. Whatever she was going to say stays unsaid, and I don't get to know it either. I felt robbed in exactly the way she did, which I take as the point. The flannel smelling "of soap and of a man and of the inside of a gym bag" — she walks inside wearing him.

Trust and suspicion, honestly tallied: within the four corners of this chapter there is nothing off. Every gesture of his is kind, asked-for or silently offered, and her yes is the realest yes the book has staged. My unease lives entirely in what I carry in — that this trail is off six-fourteen, near the house where the bench sits under its lid; that Randi, entirely absent from these pages (not even a mention — her total absence is itself loud after last chapter), co-authored the runway Vee is sprinting down so happily; that the dress-want has just been filed somewhere. The chapter itself never nudged me toward any of that. It let me have the swoon whole, which almost makes it worse.

Friction: nearly none. If I reach — the sandwich gets a long paragraph of sensory inventory, but I'd be lying if I said I wasn't hungry by the end of it; the horseradish ambush ("her eyes ran, and she didn't care that they ran") pays it off, another entry in the book's ledger of Vee eating unwatched. Nothing felt like a thumb on the scale.

The title: before reading, "Leave No Trace" sounded like hiking-manual boilerplate. On the page it's the cherry-pit ethic, spoken gently — "We take nothing and leave nothing." But it won't hold still. He leaves traces all over her: the moleskin square on her heel, the flannel she goes inside wearing, the lacing trick, the pit-catching she'll do forever now. And the darker recolor I can't unhear: the whole courtship — his and Randi's — is built to leave no trace of authorship, every intervention designed to read as weather. Take nothing and leave nothing is either the most honorable thing this man believes or the operating manual of the plan, and the chapter lets both stand. A Polite Invitation keeps sharpening — everything he does is invitation, never demand, and this chapter she issued the invitation herself, which is either the promise kept or the trap's finest hour. With a Long Spoon: the shared water bottle, his mouth where hers had been — the supping is getting very close in. My memory of the jacket's promise — that nothing would be taken she didn't hand over — is doing heavy work now: she is handing things over faster, and gladly, and I still can't tell if the promise is protection or loophole.

What I want next: I want the sleeping-with to happen and I want it to be as good to her as this was, and I dread that between here and there the machinery has to surface. Guesses from here: the russet dress reappears; the flannel has to go back, which means his house; and someone — Cassie, or Brooke's silent tall companion, or the cop's stop itself — is eventually going to put the white pickup somewhere it shouldn't be.
