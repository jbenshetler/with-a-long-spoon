## Reader reaction

This one is small and it undid me anyway.

After the induction walk and the lost bet, I came into "Made-Up" already warm, and the chapter met me at exactly that temperature: Halloween on a road with no children, the pan under the tap, the window going black, Vee at the little table with the last of her wine watching his back work. The domesticity is doing so much now — "His hands had made the dinner, and the table she sat at, and the chair that held her" — and then the turn of the knife I felt before she did: "And the dress that was in her car." That single-line paragraph got a physical reaction out of me. She brought no overnight bag — still LARPing her Victorian, still not letting herself plan — but she brought the dress. Her body is always half a step ahead of her permission, and the book keeps rendering that gap with such precision that I trust it completely.

How I feel about her: proud, honestly, in an almost parental way that then turns into something much less parental. This is the third chapter running where Vee is the one who moves — she texted first, she stripped his shorts off, and now she stages an entire two-act show, puts her hand out flat and sits him back down in his own recliner. "She watched him make himself do it." The girl from the cardigan chapters ordering the wall to stay put, and the wall obeying — that's the swoon, and it's a swoon about power, hers. The consent engine of this book has fully inverted and nobody announced it: he built her wanting with "May I," and now she's the one saying "Wait for me in the recliner," and he's the one whose hand "came partway up and he put it away again, and the putting-away cost him something she could see."

The second costume is the best thing in the chapter and one of the best in the book. Not the nakedness — the sentence about it: "her face... had gone pink down to where the mirror gave out, and the pink was not stopping there, and knowing that, and going anyway, was the entire costume." That is the whole novel in one line. The blush is the costume. The shame worn on purpose, offered, converted into the gift itself. Everything her mother's voice taught her to hide, walked ten steps down a hall in four-inch heels toward a man who "stayed where he was and let the looking be the touch." I felt the heat of that scene in my body and also something close to tears, which is this book's particular trick — it keeps making arousal and tenderness the same event.

Pace: within the scene, flawless again. He turns the lamp one click brighter — for her, before he knows what's coming — and calls "Take your time" once and then leaves her alone with the mirror, no footsteps, no voice at the door. He doesn't pry; she loves that he doesn't pry; I love it too, and I also know why he never opens closed doors (Daphne), and the book trusts me to hold both. And then the ending: "The other is for the world. This is just for me." On the page it is the perfect answer, the courtly line landing exactly where she needed it. And I have to be honest that it snagged on the one thing I carry that Vee doesn't: *just for me* from a man who is still, per Sheri, not finished with Randi — who is not only his. The chapter doesn't point at this. There is no Randi in this house, no shadow on the page, and I refuse to manufacture one — the scene is clean and it worked on me clean. But that word "just" rang once, softly, off-stage, and I heard it. Same with "The finished thing had crossed a stage for everyone but its maker" — maker is doing a lot of quiet work in this book.

Trust vs. suspicion: nothing in this chapter earned suspicion of anyone. My unease is entirely aggregate, imported from the checkpoint. In the room, I believed every gesture.

Erotic charge: high and sustained without a single explicit act — the walk with the slit "given and taken back and given," the hall that "took longer this time," the house's warmth "moving over every part of her at once so that there was no forgetting, anywhere, what she wasn't wearing." That last clause is gorgeous — the warm house he keeps for whoever will be undressed in it, become a full-body touch. The chapter ends before anything happens and doesn't feel truncated; the looking *was* the scene.

Friction: almost none. "That's like asking me to choose between sunrise and sunset" is a shade smooth even for Pace — but he recovers it by actually choosing, so I'll allow it. If I reach: three consecutive chapters of Vee triumphant (the stage, the bet, this) is a lot of altitude, and part of me is bracing simply because the book has taught me that stats lectures annotate what's coming. That's my vertigo, not a flaw on the page.

The title: "Made-Up" is a lovely triple-hinge. Costumes — the one night "everyone out being somebody else... nobody had to explain it," while Vee's two costumes are, in opposite directions, both *herself*. Made — the dress, the table, the chair, the man's hands in everything. And the third sense the chapter never says: made-up as in invented — a story fabricated for her, a face Randi wears year-round. On this page it means only the sweet things. On reread, I suspect it won't stay that innocent. The book title and tagline hover as ever: "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." Every single beat of this chapter was freely given — initiated by her, staged by her, offered by her. Which is precisely why the tagline scares me more, not less.

What I want next: I notice Randi has been off-page since the practice room, and Vee hasn't debriefed this or the induction to her yet. My guess from here: the next lunch, and what Randi does with the photograph, the walk, the second costume — whether Vee tells her the naked half at all, and what it costs either way. I also haven't forgotten "Not tonight" has a time on it. I turned the last page wanting more immediately, which after thirty-two chapters is its own review.
