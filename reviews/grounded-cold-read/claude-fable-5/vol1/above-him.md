## Reader reaction

This one got me. I want to say that plainly before I do anything else with it: "Above Him" is a small, quiet, three-part chapter with almost no event in it — a night, a morning, a drawer — and I finished it with my throat tight, which is exactly what happened to Vee at the sink, so fair enough.

The first movement is afterglow-as-inventory, and it's lovely. A week apart, and the reunion is all doorway and dropped coats — "They hadn't made the kitchen. They hadn't made the hall." — but what the chapter actually dwells on is not the sex, it's her cataloguing what kind of lover he is: "he never went straight at his own finish... it always came around to her first... He hadn't known a man could do that. She hadn't known a man would want to." And then the sentence I've been waiting for since Christine's restaurant arrives, in her chest and nowhere else: "She loved him. There. She'd said it, or not said it, but let it be true in her own chest where she couldn't take it back." Followed by the whispered "Peter" to a sleeping man — the name she took away from the fireside in "Rock" "to keep it," now spent in the dark where it can't be heard. That's the swoon, and I'm not going to pretend it didn't work on me. It worked completely.

And the drawer. God, the drawer. The little three-beat structure of it is perfect: the counter colonized, his eyes in the mirror holding on her sprawl "a second, taking it in," and the tail of worry she carries out — "Had he minded. She couldn't tell. He hadn't said. He never said." Then the next Friday, the bottom dropping out of her morning at the bare counter — I felt that lurch with her, the guest's dread of having been "too much" — and then the reversal: his whole ordered self "folded... down into a single drawer," and hers is the top one. "The easy one. The one your hand goes to first." And the line that closes the loop on everything this man has ever done: "There was no note. There was never a note." That's the shirt with no tag, the dress whose labor was hidden, the heat turned up hours before she arrives. He doesn't say things; he builds them. She reads it correctly: "He hadn't told her it didn't matter. He'd gone and made her a place instead." I melted. I'm reporting the melt honestly.

Now the other thing, which the page did not put there but my memory does, and I can't pretend it away either: the last hand-made thing in that house that was "more than it looked" was the bench. The man who builds his answers instead of saying them built a spanking apparatus disguised as a table and let a woman lay her underwear on it for weeks. So when Vee stands "with a hand on each open drawer" reading love in joinery, I believe her reading — and I also remember that reading this man's furniture is not a safe science. And there's a colder question the chapter quietly raises without asking it: this is the only bathroom in that house, and Randi still comes to that house — "That's not finished." Where do Randi's things go? Did she ever get a drawer? The cashmere blanket she brought is somewhere under that roof. Vee's place is real — I believe it's real, I think he may genuinely love her; "he felt it in everything he did and said it in nothing" is the most accurate sentence anyone has produced about Pace — but her place is being made in a house that already has a second woman's traffic in it, and the chapter's sweetness sits directly on top of that fact without once touching it. That's not suspicion I manufactured; that's the book's own standing arrangement, and the tenderer this gets, the worse the eventual arithmetic.

One small thing in section i that I found quietly astonishing and a little eerie: the weight. "her body quietly burning down whole weekends of him, and she'd never asked it to" — her bras riding higher, her good underwear "cut to fit her and not to a guess." The man is literally remaking her body — feeding her until she eats "like a field hand" and loving her until her clothes stop fitting. It's presented as pure flourishing, and it reads as flourishing, and it is also one more way that everything she is becoming is happening inside his house, his food, his robe, his drawer. She's thriving. She's also being absorbed. The page holds both and I'll hold both.

The one unsaid "I love you" — "he had never once said it to her" — she resolves it beautifully for herself ("she could live clear inside the everything-he-did and not go hungry for the saying"), and I noticed I didn't fully relax into her resolution. Not because the page showed me anything false in him — it didn't — but because a man who says it in everything and in nothing has, conveniently or not, never put the word on the record where it would mean choosing. I leave that ajar because the chapter leaves it ajar.

Friction: nearly none. The weight-loss paragraph runs slightly explanatory ("because the racks had never once believed a build like hers and her budget had never once stretched") — a half-beat of accounting in an otherwise felt chapter — but it also feeds the broke-girl truth I already care about, so I'll allow it. The chapter is slight on event and right on length; three vignettes, no fat.

The title: I'll confess my first association, before reading, was a body — someone above him, astride, the way I last saw Randi in "Gone." What I got instead was a drawer above his drawer, and her mouth above his sleeping ear saying "Peter." That recoloring — from the expected erotic posture to the domestic one — is the chapter in miniature, and I like it a great deal. As for the book and series: A Polite Invitation, With a Long Spoon, and the tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" — this chapter is the softest possible proof of the tagline. Nothing here was taken. A drawer was freely given, a love freely grown. That is the problem. The politeness of the invitation has never been more complete than a cleared top drawer.

What I want next: I want her to find him in that last line and I want the finding to stay this simple, and I know it won't. What I dread, as guesses from here: the bathroom is now a shared surface with two women's lives routed through it — sooner or later something of Randi's, or something of Vee's, is going to be in the wrong person's sightline. And Vee has now let "I love you" be true in her chest; the next time Randi goes fishing at a brunch table, that's the thing swimming closest to the surface.
