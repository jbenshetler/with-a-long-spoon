# Cold read (grounded) — School Nights

## Reader reaction

After three chapters of escalating heat and the drawer that undid me at the end of "Above Him," this little chapter is a glass of cold water — Cassie's two inches of cracked window, literally — and I drank it gratefully. It's barely a scene: Vee comes home past eleven "with the night still on her," Cassie is folded into the bean bag, and what follows is maybe the quietest chapter in the book. And it moved me more than I expected a breather to.

What lands first is Cassie. "You're doing the streetlight thing again. You're a public utility" — I laughed out loud, and then the chapter did its real work under the joke. Cassie "listened with no face on at all and let Vee talk herself out" — that no-face is doing so much. Randi's face is a made thing, the mask I watched her build in a mirror in chapter one; Cassie's no-face is the opposite, a person withholding judgment because she loves someone. And then the three flat sentences that are the chapter's spine: "You don't come home school nights." "I don't get it. You've had boyfriends. You never went like this over one." "Do you love him." Not a question, none of them, in Cassie's grammar. Nobody else in this book asks Vee anything without wanting something back. Cassie asks the cost question and the love question and takes nothing. Coming one chapter after Randi's brunch — "And did he take care of your fire?" — the contrast is almost brutal. Randi's questions walk Vee down into her body one confession at a time; Cassie's questions walk her back up into her life. Same friend-debrief shape, opposite instrument. I felt the difference in my chest.

The thing I keep turning over: Vee's answer. "Maybe," she said. "I think so." But I was there in the dark two nights ago (in "Above Him") when she let it be true in her own chest where she couldn't take it back — she loves him, she said "Peter" to his sleeping shape. So the "maybe" to Cassie is smaller than what she knows. The page doesn't tell me why she shrank it — shyness, superstition, the sense that saying it out loud spends it — and I'm not going to decide for her. But I noticed that she'll hand Randi her wet underwear in words and hand Cassie a "maybe," and something in that allocation aches. Cassie gets the edited version again; the middle stays ladled out. And then Cassie "let the maybe stand" — didn't push, didn't say *I know* — and went back to her book. "Go to sleep, streetlight. Some of us have class." That last line is perfect: love disguised as logistics, and also, quietly, a person who still lives on the school-night side of the line watching her roommate stop living there.

Because that's what the title is doing, and it recolored the chapter for me on the last read of it. "School Nights" — the nights you're supposed to come home, the childhood rule, the ordinary weekday self. Cassie's sentence is the title made flesh: Vee's own bed "had sat empty more nights than it hadn't," and she hadn't counted. Last chapter he emptied a drawer for her; this chapter I learn what's emptying on the other end. Nobody on the page calls it a warning, and I don't think Cassie means it as one — but the reader who knows the architecture (the game, the pointing, the staging) feels a very low hum under "It's all so fast." Vee says it herself and doesn't hear it. I did. That's not suspicion of anyone in the room — Cassie I trust totally, and nothing Pace did here is anything but tender (the cold cloth on the burn before she'd got the first swear out, the fussing for days). It's the aggregate hum the book has trained into me: every individual kindness real, the frame not hers. The tagline keeps doing its work — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" — and here even the absence from her own bed is freely given, uncounted.

Heat: barely any, and rightly. The one flicker is the baked-tomato burn — "the side of her breast," her hand going to the spot "without her knowing it moved," and Cassie's dry "There's only one way *that* burn happens. Not enough on." I grinned; Vee sails right past the implication that she cooks and eats at his house wearing next to nothing, and Cassie clocks it and lets it go. That's the chapter's erotic register: heat as residue, reported, worn home like the warmth of his car.

Friction: honestly almost none. It's very short, and it wants to be. If I reach: this is the fifth confidant-debrief beat in recent memory (Cassie, Randi, Randi again, now Cassie), and a less differentiated version would have felt like a rut — but this one earns its place precisely by refusing to be the Randi kind. The only line I'd flag at all is "the whole of himself turned on her until the rest of the room went dark around her" — gorgeous, but the streetlight/darkness imagery is starting to feel very deliberate, and I noticed the hand doing it for half a second before I went back under.

What I want next: I want Cassie to keep asking. Her watching has had grief in it since "Goodbye, Vee," and this chapter is the closest she's come to saying a true thing out loud. Guess from here: the "school nights" ledger — the uncounted absences, the bed sitting empty, the life quietly consolidating into his house and Randi's orbit — is going to matter, and Cassie will be the one holding the count when it does. And the "maybe" Vee gave her sits next to the "I love him" she gave the dark; sooner or later one of them gets said to a person who's awake.
