# Cold read (grounded) — Off Six-Fourteen

## Reader reaction

This chapter is sunshine, and I spent the whole of it holding my breath anyway.

On its own terms it's the purest, lightest thing the book has given me yet: Vee home from the date, still warm from the walk, spilling it all to Cassie before her coat is even off. The little brass tea cages again, the tree, the kiss retold in that dropped, one-word-at-a-time voice — "And I didn't go. Cass. I stopped. I made *him* stop. And I kissed him... Me." And then the spin, the clumsy whole-body turn that nearly takes out the closet door, ending in a heap in the bean bag: "*Look at me*." I grinned at the page. That spin is real — it's the exact cousin of Randi's "half-second of happy footwork she didn't decide to do" on the lawn, joy that gets to the feet before the mind clears it, and I love that the book gave Vee her own. She's earned it. After Water Wings, after the bleachers, after a girl who wore a cardigan over the better shirt on purpose — watching her be too big for the room she's in is genuinely moving.

And then there's the line that is the whole chapter for me, the one that made my stomach drop even as Vee said it glowing: "Nobody did anything to me. I wasn't talked into it. I did it because I wanted to and I did it on purpose."

At the level of the kiss, it's true, and it's beautiful, and it's exactly what I wanted for her — the book has been scrupulous about that. Pace let the no stand, started walking, and she pulled him back. Her yes was really a yes. But I was standing on the quad when Randi went hunting up the path after a ponytail. I watched Pace point, and Randi stage a collision so clean "it looked like the redhead had walked into *her*," and Pace "happen" into a coffee shop where Vee was stuck on an integral. Nobody talked her into the kiss. Somebody arranged the tree. The chapter never winks at this — it lets Vee have her joy completely, no authorial nudge — and that restraint is what makes the line land like a bruise instead of a device. She's claiming, with her whole chest, the one thing the reader knows was curated for her. The want is hers. The occasion was built. I don't know yet how much that distinction will end up costing, and the chapter doesn't tell me, and I felt the not-telling as pressure.

Cassie. Cassie deepened again, and she's quietly becoming the character I trust most in this book. Her care never arrives as care — it arrives as "How much did he give you to drink?", as "what's his name, so I know what to tell the state troopers," as the flat incredulous refrain the chapter is named for: "That's the whole address. *Off six-fourteen*." Every joke is a safety check wearing a joke's clothes, the same move as the splash in the pool — protection pitched low so the sad version can't get bigger. And here's what aches: her instincts are pointed at the wrong threat. She's checking whether the stranger from the coffee shop will walk Vee off a cliff, and the actual thing she should be checking is the wonderful new friend who says "love you, girl" — the one Cassie already didn't take to, the one whose bit she declined to answer with anything but her own flat name. Cassie's radar keeps pinging near the truth ("He looked at her the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt") without ever landing on it. "Borrow good ones," and back to her highlighter — I felt that as love, the kind that doesn't announce itself.

And the dossier. "He lives out west of town, off six-fourteen, past where it goes to gravel." Vee counts it off "like plenty," and it is nothing — no last name (the same first-name-only man whose "last name [is] unknown to her own sorority sisters," I remember The Bench saying), no house yet, an address that's a road. But I have been in that house. I know the warm even heat he keeps it at, the quartersawn oak, and the low table of odd proportions in the middle of the bedroom, "full of what it was... waiting for the next time." Vee is cheerfully planning to borrow real shoes and walk into a house I've seen from the inside, and she doesn't know Randi has a drawer's worth of history there, and Randi and Pace are the only two people who know the whole board. The dramatic irony in this little room is almost unbearable, and the chapter generates it with nothing but a girl being happy.

Small perfect touch at the end: the window's thin line of cold coming in over both of them, "and Vee lay back in the bean bag warm and did not feel it." I thought immediately of Randi in Standards, lying cold under the adequate synthetic duvet in her lovely old house, shoes on, not crying. One girl warm and not feeling the cold; one girl cold and not feeling the crying. The book keeps doing this — running the two of them as inversions — and it never points at it, which is why it works.

Erotic charge: none here, and none wanted; this is the exhale after May I Choose, and it's the right size — short, brisk, over before it wears out its welcome. Friction: almost nothing. If I squint, "Nobody did anything to me" does double duty — it's Vee's line and it's the book's thesis-adjacent line — but it grows so naturally out of Cassie's drink question that I can't call it a thumb on the scale. It's what Vee would say. It's just also true in a way she can't see.

Titles. "Off Six-Fourteen" is Cassie's refrain and it's exactly right: the chapter is about how little Vee actually knows — a first name, a road, "past where it goes to gravel" — offered up as if it were plenty. And it points, hard, at the house. Past where it goes to gravel: off the paved and mapped, into his territory. That's where the book is taking her next, and taking me. *A Polite Invitation* keeps accruing weight — this book is made of polite invitations ("May I call you?", "May I choose?", coffee, froyo, dinner, now a hike), every one of them genuinely gentle and every one of them a step deeper into something Vee hasn't been shown the shape of. *With a Long Spoon* — I keep hearing the old proverb about supping with the devil, and I still can't tell who's holding the spoon: Pace, Randi, or Vee herself. I wasn't given a cover tagline in my packet, so I've only the titles to steer by.

What I want next: I want the hike, and I dread it a little, which is this book's signature feeling now. Guesses from here: the hike goes to his house or near it, and Vee sees something — the furniture, maybe the bench-as-table she won't know how to read. And at some point Vee learns Randi and Pace are not strangers, and I don't know whether that arrives as betrayal, as the "threesome" being unveiled, or as something stranger — the page has kept Randi's own want (the bare "Yes" on the quad, the cup that came up and went down with no sip) unresolved, and I'm leaving it unresolved. What I dread most isn't the sex. It's Vee finding out that "I did it on purpose" was true of her and not of her evening.
