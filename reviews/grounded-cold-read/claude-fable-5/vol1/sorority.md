# Cold read (grounded) — Sorority

*scene: scenes/sorority.md · model: claude-fable-5 · memory: ck-ch020 + raw ch021..ch022 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

The chapter I've been waiting for since the fire-lane kisses: a whole day of just Vee and Randi, no Pace, no chaperone — and it did exactly what I feared and wanted, which is that I had a wonderful time and walked out lighter in the wallet than I came in.

Start with the swoon, because it's real and it's most of the chapter. The dress reveal in the dorm is lovely — Vee half-meaning to just hold it up on the hanger "which was the sane way to show a dress to a friend," and then "the wanting got there before the plan, the wanting to be *seen* in it as she'd been seen in his mirror." That's Vee now: the girl from the pool bleachers has become someone who performs a small ceremony of being looked at, on purpose, for a woman. And Randi's "He didn't make this *for* you. He made it *to* you" is the best line in the chapter — it names, in one preposition, exactly what made Vee cry in the sun porch, and Vee feels it land the same way I did. Randi is dangerous precisely because she's this good: she hands people the words for their own feelings.

The comedy genuinely worked on me. The yeti shoe, the six-inch pink-rhinestone platforms worn at "her usual altitude" with the deadpan "Do these go with burgundy?", the flat "one brisk walk from becoming a sandal" — and crucially, that last one gets Randi "the real laugh, the one that costs her something to give." I know that phrase's weight from the checkpoint: the real laugh has been leaking through the performance for chapters. Vee standing in the wreckage "feeling like the funniest woman in Virginia" is one of the purest happinesses the book has given her, because for once she's the one delighting Randi rather than being dazzled by her. That's the swoon: they're funny together. It reads like real friendship. It might even be one.

And the toe. Randi on one knee on the pale floor in her beautiful clothes — "I've got her" to the shopgirl, which is a line that means two things and I heard both — holding Vee's bare foot in both hands, looking at the frosted-plum polish (painted for Pace, ch18; now being read by Randi), and one thumb moved once across the nail, and Vee "felt it go all the way up the length of her, felt the floor of her tip… and her hand shot out and found Randi's shoulder to stop it." Randi looks up, smiles, and says nothing at all. That's the erotic peak of the chapter and it's a single thumb-stroke. Vee's want for Randi is now a physical fact that grabs a shoulder to stay upright, and Randi *saw it* and filed it, smiling. My stomach did the flip and the drop at the same time.

Because here's where the chapter quietly frightened me, and I think the page earned it: the money. Vee is the girl of debit-card arithmetic, TJMaxx math, menus read right-side first. She finds the shoe that *works* — tan, sensible, affordable, "it goes with everything" — and Randi doesn't argue with the shoe; she argues with the premise. "A dress a man made *to* you doesn't want to go with everything." And then Vee stands at the counter, puts the debit card *back*, and lays down the emergency credit card for the most she's ever handed across a counter, knowing "she did not have the next part, the paying-it-back part," and won't let it reach her face "because the one thing she would not do in this room was flinch." That paragraph hurt. It's written exactly like her other surrenders — the shame crossing into wanting almost before it was shame — except this time the thing being spent isn't shame, it's rent. And the very next beat is Randi buying loafers at "a multiple of Vee's" price without glancing at the number, wearing them out of the store. The book doesn't editorialize the contrast and doesn't need to. Vee carries her box against her chest with both arms "like it might get up and leave her," and Randi teases her for it, and they laugh, and it's warm, and I'm sitting here doing the arithmetic Vee refused to do. Was this steered? I can't prove it from the page — Randi may simply be a rich girl who genuinely believes the dress deserves the shoes. But "the shoes were not a want she had to own as a want. They were what the dress was owed" is Vee talking herself past her own guardrails in exactly the voice this book has taught me to distrust, and Randi supplied the logic. Last chapter's professor put it on the board: same numbers, nobody touched one of them, and the answer reverses depending on how you group them. Sensible shoe, gorgeous shoe — the answer depends entirely on whose frame you're standing in, and Vee has moved into Randi's.

"Same feet, baby. Same size, same everything… it's practically a marriage." Randi's delight there felt bigger than a shoe size, and "same everything" is doing a lot of work she may not even mean consciously — or may entirely. I also clocked "your *lover*," the word Vee had never let herself use, handed to her by the woman who shares him. That one only I can feel the full weight of, and it landed cold under the warm.

Cassie's absence is loud. Her bunk "made tight and square… nobody home" opens the chapter — the room described by who isn't in it — and the whole shopping day is exactly the outing Cassie would never join and would watch with the flat patient eyebrow. Vee's last line — "she had never once in her life had a friend like this" — is true, and I winced at it, because she has: two winters of the same fleece, watching only the friend.

Friction: almost none. If I press, the second-store sequence explains Vee's class-panic slightly more than it has to ("She ran the math she knew how to run, the TJMaxx math, the most-I-could math") — I'd already felt it from the shoe on its bleached-wood block with no price. But that's a quibble; the register is hers.

The title: "Sorority" — and not one sorority sister in sight. No Brooke, no Standards, no house. The title has been reassigned to what Randi is building with Vee: sisterhood, shared shoe size, "practically a marriage," the two of them laughing on a sidewalk. It's a lovely oblique title that goes a shade sinister on the second look, because I know what Randi's actual sorority does — Standards, the smiling interrogation, the face — and "sorority" is also the institution Randi lies to. Which sisterhood is Vee being pledged into? *A Polite Invitation* keeps sharpening: today the invitation was "Come on. I know just the place," and it cost Vee money she doesn't have, freely handed over, gladly. Every yes was freely given; that was the problem. *With a Long Spoon* — the spoon got shorter today. The tagline's "wanting the woman beside her, without quite letting herself know it" was literally staged on a fitting bench this chapter.

What I want next: the induction. The dress, the shoes, the walking-in-late fantasy Vee has now rehearsed twice — the book is loading that room. I want it and I dread it; her mother will be there, and possibly Randi, and I don't know if Vee's two worlds can be in one room without something showing. Guess from here: the credit-card bill is a planted charge — it will come due, in some month that runs long, and it will make Vee more dependent on the people who have money and made her want things. Second guess: Randi's "Mm" over the toes is not the last time she kneels in front of Vee.
