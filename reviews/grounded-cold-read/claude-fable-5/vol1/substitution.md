## Reader reaction

I read this whole chapter with my stomach slightly tight, because I know what Vee doesn't, and the book made me sit in that knowledge for every polite, charming second of it.

Start with the first sentence, because it detonated quietly on me a page later: "Randi suggested they move to the place on Wilson." The place on Wilson. That's the alibi Randi fed Brooke in the common room — "There's the place on Wilson that stays open" — a study spot invented to cover a night on Pace's bench. And now she's laundering the lie into fact, physically installing Vee in the fictional coffee shop from her cover story, and the man the alibi was covering for walks in the door. I actually put my hand over my mouth. Nobody on the page says any of this. The chapter never once states that this meeting is staged. It doesn't have to. Randi texting with "the private, absorbed focus of a girl writing to someone who had her attention" — and Vee thinking, amused, "Some boy" — is the cruelest little sentence in the chapter, because the boy is Pace and the text is the summons. Then Randi introduces herself to him with one bare word — "Randi," said Randi — no adjective bit, no parents-had-a-sense-of-humor, the routine she deploys on every stranger withheld, because he is not a stranger. Then the planted softball ("Why is it n-minus-one and not n?") so he can answer her briefly and Vee can file "he answered her well... and he answered me longer." Then the closing sales pitch: "A man doesn't forget his coffee over a girl he isn't going to call." Which is framing so dishonest it made my skin prickle — he didn't forget his coffee; there was never going to be coffee; the line was set dressing. Randi knows that. She says it anyway, warm, as girlfriend-advice, closing the deal.

And here is what the chapter did to me that I want to be honest about: the con is airtight, and the bait is real. The tutoring scene is one of the best seduction-by-competence beats I've ever read, and I swooned at it even knowing the frame was fabricated. "He picked up her pencil — asked for it with his eyes first." The monstrous formula written in the margin so she can shudder at it and be excused from it — "Nobody should have to carry that around." Euler's formula, the thing she'd "known for years and never once used," suddenly for something. And then the line that got me completely: "He had given her the question and given her back the pencil." Vee's own analysis of it — her econ eye reading how a smart person handles a problem they could handle several ways, concluding he "kept himself out of it so the solving stayed hers" — is both true and the trap's genius. The specific virtue she detects is real behavior. He genuinely didn't show off; he genuinely listened; he genuinely asked "What's drawing you to it," the question with room in it. The manufactured coincidence delivers authentic attention. I don't know what to do with that, and I think that's exactly the position the book wants me in. My unease about Pace, which The Pointing Game started, went up another full notch here — the same unhurried craftsman's patience that read as safety on the bench is now aimed at a woman who doesn't know a game exists. And yet the page holds open whether Vee is, to him, a person or a project, and I'm holding it open too. "May I call you?" — "She heard the may." I melted and shivered in the same beat. That is the book's whole method in four words: courtliness as instrument. It is a polite invitation.

Randi is the one I can't stop turning over. She has maybe five lines in this chapter and every one is surgical. But the cup — "Twice the cup came up to her mouth and went back down, and no sip happened." Vee reads it as boredom. I read... something, and I genuinely can't fix it: jealousy at watching her secret lover pick another woman out of the whole day and smile at her like that? The thrill of the operation working? The strain of holding the face while the two halves of her life sit at one table? The page gives me the busy hand and nothing else, and it echoes that water-bottle cap she pressed under her thumbnail after the bench — Randi's hands do things her face won't admit to. Whatever it was, it wasn't nothing, and Vee's brain "helpfully provided the explanation she's bored," which is the chapter in miniature: everyone supplying innocent readings of true data.

Cassie. Cassie is becoming my proxy in this book and I love her more every chapter. She's the one who spots Pace ("That cute guy in line is looking at you" — is he in line? he gives up his "place in line"; the line was his blocking, and Cassie clocked him before he moved). And her closer is the most dangerous sentence anyone has said in four chapters: "He looked at you like he'd forgotten there was anyone else in the room. He looked at her the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." Cassie is reporting evidence without knowing it's evidence — that Pace performed indifference at Randi, performed it so well it registered. After her flat "Goodbye, Vee" last chapter, I trust Cassie's instruments completely, and I'm starting to think she's the one who will see the shape of this first. "Cassie, at her table, watched the cup without watching it" — the same construction as Sloane "watching the exchange without watching it" in the sorority common room. This book is full of covert watchers, and Cassie is the only one watching on Vee's behalf.

Vee herself deepened wonderfully. Her borrowing Randi's name-bit — "V.T. Yes, like the university... Yes, they find it funnier than I do" — is touching and quietly sad: she's already remaking herself in Randi's image, trying on the borrowed poise, and it lands, and she immediately wants her own name back ("Vee," she added, quickly). Her class-reading of Pace's clothes — "he dressed like a man who fixed things for a living — except the cloth was too good for that" — is sharp characterization doing double duty; she keeps "reaching for the shape under him and it would not come," and neither can I, entirely. And her unfinished thoughts — "He is also—  The thought did not finish," twice — rhyme with Randi under her duvet, where "the thought stopped where the words stopped." Both women in this book have minds that halt at the edge of what they're perceiving. That pattern is now book-level and it's quietly devastating.

Friction, honestly minor: Vee's econ speech — "the gap between what they say they're doing and what they're doing... if you understood it well, you'd understand a lot" — came right up to the line of the author looking at me over the page, especially one chapter after the professor teed up "latent variables." It's in character, she was asked a real question, but it's the second theme-naming in two chapters and I felt the wink. Also "No individual part of him was particularly impressive but the whole of him was, and she could not say how" leans on told mystery — though Vee's failure to categorize him is itself characterized, so it mostly survives.

The title. "Substitution" is doing so much work I keep finding new floors. The surface is the math — integration by substitution, "turn the thing in front of you into a thing you already know how to do." But that sentence is also the con's method: Pace and Randi turning Vee into a problem they already know how to do. And under that — remembering The Pointing Game, where Randi took her own unsurvivable want and handed it to Pace, "the same act, a different owner" — Vee herself is the substitution: the body Randi's want can be routed through without Randi ever owning it. Even Euler's formula reads like a diagram of this if I let it: two things that "on their own are a struggle... put them together, at right angles, and they turn into a single thing that's suddenly plain." Two hidden lovers, combined, arriving at one plain answer. I don't think I'm forcing that; the chapter put a pencil in my hand. A Polite Invitation has now fully declared itself — "May I call you?" is the politest invitation imaginable, extended across a table built entirely of deception — and With a Long Spoon keeps whispering the proverb about supping with the devil; the spoons literally ringing in every cup at Chi Latte last chapter feel almost cheeky about it now. (I was given no jacket copy or tagline in my packet, so I'm reading the titles bare.) The title didn't spoil the chapter; it recolored it on the way out, which is the best kind.

What I want and dread: I want the phone to light up as badly as Vee does, and I hate that I want it. Guesses from here, plainly marked: Pace calls, the courtship is real and lovely and Vee falls entirely; the threesome architecture surfaces eventually; and the fault line is Randi's cup-hand — whatever that was, it will not stay contained. And Cassie will notice something true out loud at the worst possible moment. I dread the day Vee learns the collision on the lawn and the man in the coffee shop were both choreography — because the chapter's last image, Vee refusing to turn her phone over so she can "let herself want it to light up," is a woman practicing hope, and I already know the hope was manufactured for her. I turned the page wanting the next one immediately.
