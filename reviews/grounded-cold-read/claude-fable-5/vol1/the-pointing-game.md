# Cold read (grounded) — The Pointing Game

## Reader reaction

I came into this chapter still cold from "Standards" — Randi flat on her back under an inadequate duvet with her shoes on, having decided in Pace's bathroom mirror that "he could have the face" and the one he found was "not coming out again. Not ever." So the first thing this chapter did to me was whiplash, and I mean that as an experience, not a complaint: suddenly I'm in Pace's head, in bed, and he's telling me she's "not performing now," that she's "unguarded, as she only was with him inside her" — and I'm sitting there holding the deadbolt she turned two chapters ago, which he never heard. That gap is the electricity of the whole chapter for me. He reads her flawlessly moment to moment — the flinch, the costume coming up, the ownership-transfer of the threesome line — and yet I watched him, at the end of "The Bench," smile warmly at a smile that was lying to him and think it had been "a remarkably good evening." He has been wrong before, on the page, without knowing it. So every confident aphorism here ("He knew the difference between a woman performing release and a woman delivered to it") lands twice: once as seduction, once as a hairline crack I can't stop looking at.

And the body of the chapter is that crack dramatized, because Randi's body keeps outrunning the face. The moment that stopped me cold — genuinely one of the best beats in the book so far — is her coming *at the question*. "Would you like to taste another woman?" and "it took her all at once... far too fast for anything his body was doing to account for it." That is the involuntary "yes" she cannot arrange, the same species as the flinch when he called her beautiful. Then the bathroom-mirror machinery goes to work exactly as she vowed it would: "Only in a threesome. With you." And Pace's reading of that move is the chapter's sharpest writing: "not a woman who hungered for another woman, which was unsurvivable, but a girlfriend offering her man a gift, which was merely sexy. The same act. A different owner." *Unsurvivable* is a devastating word choice — one word doing the work of a whole psychology. This is the interior life I read for: shame and appetite braided so tight she has to launder her own wanting through him to be allowed to keep it.

But here's what I have to hold open, and the page holds it open too: Pace sees the laundering, admires it ("because it was beautiful"), and *cooperates with it* — "He let the camouflage stand." In "The Bench" his whole project was getting her out from behind the face; here he helps the want get dressed before it goes out. He tells himself it's a bridge, that "she deserved that gentleness," and I half believe him — the man does everything the careful way: he re-asks the question in daylight, when she's "gathered back into herself... free to weigh it and choose; that mattered more to him than the answer did." That's real consent-attentiveness and I felt it. And yet he asked it *first* mid-coitus, on purpose, knowing her body would answer before she could — "what the question had been for." Both things are on the page. I'm not calling it sinister; I'm calling it architecture, and after "Standards" I watch his architecture with one eyebrow up even while it charms me.

The pointing game itself swept me up completely. The escalation of Randi from propriety-offended ("the bald social wrongness of it") to *shopping* — "She would have told him she was being a good sport about his game; she had stopped being a good sport a quarter mile back" — is delicious, and every rejection characterizes her: not the mirror of herself ("Too expected"), not the sealed pair, not the closed project, "God, no" to the woman who runs rooms. He's not choosing a woman; "He was watching her learn that she could." That line got me. And the kiss on the path — his mouth still unwiped from bed, "he knew the moment her own taste reached her" — is the hottest clothed beat in the chapter.

Then Vee. The entrance paragraph is a set piece and it *works* — "lush in a way that had nothing curated about it and nothing apologized for," clothes "chosen to cover, and failing at an impossible task," and above all the laugh: "laughing like a person who had genuinely forgotten, for the length of the laugh, that she could be seen." I felt the whole book click when I read that, because being seen is Randi's catastrophe — she spent an entire chapter in a bathroom deciding never to be seen again — and the one woman who arrests her mid-stride is the one who doesn't guard against it at all. Randi's bare "Yes," alone, "with nothing around it," before the costume catches up — that's the third involuntary yes of the chapter and the biggest.

The engineered collision is where my swoon and my unease share a bench. As competence it's gorgeous — "a decade of dance and three years of whatever sorority actually taught," folded into three seconds so clean the *redhead* apologizes. I grinned. And underneath the grin: a warm, open, unguarded person has just been approached via manufactured chance by two people who spent the afternoon pointing at strangers, and Pace watches from his pole feeling "a deep warmth." Nobody on the page calls that anything, and I won't either — but I noticed I felt protective of a character who has said zero words to me. That's the series title humming, honestly.

Randi at the end moved me more than anything: the happy footwork "she didn't decide to do," the squeaked daylight "*Yes*," then the second "*Yes*" in "the register she only used in his bed and never in daylight." It's the most uncomplicated joy she's shown in three chapters, and it's joy in a wanting she's only allowed to have because it's been re-owned as *theirs*. I ached for her a little, feeling how conditional her own delight is. And "Let's go feed you" as the closing line — after the thin apple slices, after "I'm hungry" / "I can't eat that" over the cheeseburger — is perfect. The whole book keeps running on appetite she won't own until someone re-plates it.

Friction, honestly minor: Pace's interiority occasionally goes thesis-y — "If pleasure was also a bridge, then let the wanting simply feel good in her, good enough that the good feeling and the wanting became one fact" is the author's palm just visible on the scale of how I should read him. And I had to do my own bridging on time: "Four weeks in" — I don't know how many days sit between the frozen ceiling-staring of "Standards" and this loose, ardent afternoon, and the jump from that woman to this one cost me a beat of confusion before I decided the bathroom vow isn't contradicted here, it's *operating* — the flinch, the reframe, the costume are the face doing exactly what she promised it would do. I hold open whether that vow is eroding or holding; the page doesn't say, and neither will I.

Erotic charge: high and real. Peak one is the opening — entering her mid-orgasm, "all that grip and flutter," and the question that detonates her. Peak two is the unwiped kiss on the quad. It never goes clinical; the daylight stretch is simmer by design and the simmer works.

Titles: "The Pointing Game" told me nothing going in — good — and now names the chapter's invention exactly, with the little transgressive sting of *pointing at people* folded in. "I point. You pick." *A Polite Invitation* is getting richer by the chapter: this whole afternoon is a polite invitation — the question re-asked in daylight, "Go meet her," even the collision that makes the meeting look like chance. And *With a Long Spoon* — I keep hearing the proverb about supping with the devil, and this chapter made me wonder, for the first time, who at that table is going to wish they'd kept their distance. No tagline was included in my packet, so I have nothing to hold against the jacket.

What I want next: Vee, obviously. A stats class together — so Randi has a legitimate lane to her. My guesses from here: Randi will pursue this with the face fully deployed, and the thing she laundered as a gift for Pace will turn out to be the appetite that won't stay laundered; and Vee's unguardedness is going to do to Randi what the bench did — find her. What I dread, gently: that the warm open girl with the copper hair is walking into a machine two careful people built, and nobody on the page thinks of it that way yet.
