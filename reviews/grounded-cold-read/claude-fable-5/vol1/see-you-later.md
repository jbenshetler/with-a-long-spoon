## Reader reaction

Oh, this one hurt in the exact way I think it was supposed to hurt — a warm, sweet, sunlit chapter that I read with my stomach slightly dropped the whole time, because I was standing where Pace stood at the light pole: seeing all of it, hearing none of the innocence.

We're in Vee's head for the first time, and the first thing that got me is how the collision replays from her side: "it had genuinely not been clear, one of those collisions where both people apologize and neither one did it." I watched that collision get built — thirty feet of geometry "so clean it looked like nothing" — and here it is, laundered into chance by the person it was done to. That's the whole chapter in one line. Everything Randi does that I know to be craft, Vee receives as grace. "The right pen" — "a nothing thing to say, and it warmed her like praise she'd worked for." The bit about her name ("Yes, like the adjective") — I could see the machined shape of it, the slot where the other person's laugh goes, because Vee herself half-sees it ("It was a little bit, Vee saw. A nice one.") and loves it anyway. The dramatic irony isn't smug; it's tender and awful at once, and it worked on me completely.

How I feel about people now. Vee: I fell for her fast. She's not a naif — she reads rooms "all at once and in detail," she knows a bit when she sees one, she notices Cassie's "Goodbye" land wrong. She's perceptive about everything except the one thing, which is whether she's wanted, because wanting to be wanted is the hole in her hull. And her interior is already doing the thing the jacket promised — "quietly starving" — in her own key: the mother's voice arriving whole ("floozies," "that's not the kind of attention a nice girl"), the cardigan worn over the better shirt on purpose, the "longer beat of looking" at the bare-midriff girls that the word "floozies" doesn't account for. She polices her own appetite exactly the way Randi portions apple slices. Two girls raised by mothers with knives in their voices. That rhyme moved me.

Randi, seen from outside for the first time, is dazzling and a little frightening. The warmth is total, undimmed, generous even to Cassie who gives it nothing — and I know from The Pointing Game that the warmth is an instrument. But here's what I'm honestly holding open: I can't tell how much of it is only instrument. "There you are" — that's what Pace said to Randi on the bench, at the barest moment of her life, and here she is handing the same words to Vee at the end of a lecture row. Is that theft, echo, or leakage? The page doesn't say and I'm not going to say either. What I know is that her own "yes" on the quad was helpless and real before it got costumed, so the machinery and the wanting are running on the same rails, and that's more unsettling than pure predation would be.

Cassie. Four lines and she walked off with a piece of the chapter. "Goodbye, Vee" — "the whole word, flat and finished, the word for a longer parting than a Wednesday." And she "let it stand. She didn't fix it." Something passed over her face that Vee "would not have been able to name and did not try to." I don't know what Cassie saw — whether she clocked Randi specifically or just felt the shape of being replaced — and I'm leaving that ajar because the page does. But it chilled me more than anything else here. The chapter is titled after Randi's exit line, and Cassie's exit line is its dark twin.

Swoon: yes, real, and I want to be honest about it. The Chi Latte hour swept me up even knowing what I know — the sign resolving into its joke "the way a thing is funny only once you're allowed in on it," the high table, the lavender thing, the hug with "a hand flat between Vee's shoulder blades for one second." Vee's "flat helpless want" for Randi — "the want had nowhere to put itself except here, this, with her" — is the jacket's promise arriving on schedule and it still landed soft and warm in me. The blank-transcript paragraph is the best writing in the chapter: "the leaning-in was the whole content... the feeling was total and the transcript was blank." That is exactly what infatuation does to memory, and it doubles as a warning Vee can't hear.

Erotic charge: low and deliberately so — this is the charged-clothed register, and it simmers exactly where it should: Vee's under-look at the girls at the bar, the knees nearly touching at the high table, the want she doesn't examine. After the furnace of The Bench and The Pointing Game, the cooling is a choice and it works.

Friction, small: the professor's "latent variables — the factors you can't measure directly, that you have to infer from the ones you can," underlined once in Vee's notebook, is the one place I felt the author look up and meet my eyes. I grinned at it, so it mostly got away with it, but it's a half-step past what this chapter otherwise never does, which is explain itself.

And the spoons. "Spoons going against the insides of cups, all over the room, an irregular ringing, everyone stirring up the sweetness at the bottom of their drinks." The series is called With a Long Spoon — sup with the devil — and here is a whole room of girls stirring sweetness, and Vee walking into it under a Greek sign she's finally been let in under. That's the first literal spoon sighting in the book and it went through me. The book's title too: this chapter IS the polite invitation — "I'm not asking, I've decided" — and the tagline, "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem," sits right on top of Vee folding her eagerness down to "Yeah, sure, I could do coffee." Freely given. That's the problem.

What I want next: I dread and want the same thing — Vee meeting Pace, presumably steered "lightly, laughing, as if by accident." Guess from here: Cassie is going to matter as the one person outside the game's gravity, the control group, and her "Goodbye" will keep ringing. And I want to know what Randi feels when the game's object starts talking back — because "love you, girl" was a line, and I am not certain it stayed one.
