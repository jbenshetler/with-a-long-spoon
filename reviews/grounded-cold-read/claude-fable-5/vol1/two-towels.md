# Cold read (grounded) — Two Towels

## Reader reaction

The whole chapter I was two people, and I want to be honest about both of them. One of me was Vee — swept, wet-eyed at the bolt of silk, saying "Yes — of course, yes" right along with her. The other one of me was standing in that den with my fingers on a long low table in pale oak, going cold.

Start with the swoon, because it's real and it's enormous. The house itself worked on me the way it worked on her: small, post-war, older than she'd built it in her head — not the rich-boy reveal I half-braced for, but a house that's been *used* into shape. The kitchen "looked like it had a purpose." The KitchenAid with the chipped paint "worn to the shine of a thing used every day." The shoes paired and squared — "Depends how many things he does" — she's right that you could read half of him off that closet floor. And then the shelf of cracked-spine fantasy paperbacks, and the middle-schooler she conjures "flat on the floor on his elbows... gone clean out of the room" — that's the first time the book has handed me Pace as a boy, and it disarmed me exactly as much as it disarmed her. The patent monologue finished the job: "somebody sick, somebody scared, a kid who can't lie still... they're out of the tube in a fraction of it," and then, quietly devastating, "It does other things. That's the part I like." I did not know what to do with my whole chest either.

And the dress. God, the dress. I'd clocked the russet silk at the summit as one of those confessions the book plants — "Said it the way you say things you've already let go of" — and I still didn't see the bolt of cloth coming until Vee did. The turn where her disappointment flips — "The magazine shade would have fought her. This one didn't... He'd matched *her*" — is the single most romantic beat in the book so far, and the shirt realization right behind it ("I couldn't find a shirt I liked, so I made one") is perfectly played: the no-tag detail was already sitting in her memory, filed under "some category that made no sense," and mine too. This is the chapter's signature move — Pace retrieves a want she threw away and hands it back made real — and it lands because it's the same thing he did with the cheeseburger girl who couldn't eat, all along: he feeds abandoned hungers.

Now the other reader. Because I've been in this house before, in chapter one, and Vee hasn't.

She crouched and put her fingers on the joinery of "a long low table" in the den, and asked where he got it, and he said "I made them," and I believe — the page doesn't say it, so I'll hold it ajar, but the shape and the room are right — that she was admiring the bench. The restraint furniture he built for Randi, disguised as a table with its lid on, "waiting for the next time." If that's what her fingers were on, it's the most quietly horrifying image in the book: the craftsmanship she's falling for and the machinery underneath it are literally the same object. "It's cut to lock, and glued, so it stays" — that sentence goes through me twice.

And the title told me where to look. "Two Towels" seems like nothing on the way in — "a bathroom, plain, clean, two towels on the rod," a half-line in a tour. On the way out it's the loudest quiet thing in the house. Either it's hospitality — a second towel set out for the guest, the house prepared for her the way the heat was — or it's the standing trace of the other woman who showers there. I genuinely can't pick, and I don't think I'm supposed to yet, but sitting one chapter after one called "Leave No Trace" — we take nothing and leave nothing — the idea that Randi has left exactly one trace in this swept, ordered house, and Vee walked right past it, made the hair on my arms stand up. The title didn't spoil anything before I read; it detonated afterward. That's the good kind.

The suspicion the text actually earned, itemized, because I want to be precise: the house is "too warm," which she files away and I can't — he plans to ask her to stand in her underwear, and the heat was on before she arrived. The invitation said "two o'clock" and nothing else, and now I hear "in good light, so I can see how it'll fall" and understand the hour was chosen. He stopped the kiss — "the stopping was his," and Vee herself goes still and starts "paying attention," because "no boy she'd ever been with had once been the one to stop" — and he left the bedroom door shut while she leaned at it in her purpose-worn satin, and "something in her dropped." He is metering her want again, making the withheld thing the wanted thing, and the page knows it even when she doesn't. And the closing line of the sequence is the architecture said out loud: "He couldn't make it without seeing her. That was the only thing standing between her and it — her own willingness to be looked at." The truck striptease taught her she wanted to be seen; the dress makes being seen into a gift she can say yes to. Every step consensual, every step built. That's this book's whole ache and I felt it hard here.

What I *don't* suspect matters too: the flinch at "Are your parents rich?" — "a small closing, there and gone, a door easing shut two rooms away" — read as a real wound, not performance, and it's a brand-new one. Not Daphne. Parents. Noted, and held open.

Erotic charge: no skin, and still humming the whole way through — the doorway kiss that "went deep," the satin she wore "hoping the man won't hand her a reason not to show it to him," the lean at the closed door, and the measuring offer, which is the most charged thing in the chapter precisely because it's delivered in a tailor's flat voice. The chapter ends on a yes that is really a yes and is also the click of a lock I can't see.

Friction, small and honest: "The shirt he'd given her that she'd worn into the ground" — it's been maybe a week since the mountain; "into the ground" blinked me even as infatuation-hyperbole, and I had a beat of confusion squaring the gym-bag flannel with "the good shirt" with flat seams before deciding they're the same kept shirt. Also the error-correcting-codes riff, charming as it is ("Say it more than once so the noise can't kill it"), is doing so much thematic work — finding the signal in the noise is what this man does to women — that I felt the book's intelligence more than his for a second. Barely a complaint.

The book's title has never fit better: this entire chapter is a polite invitation — "would you come to my house," "If you'll let me," "If you want it" — every door held, every yes hers, the whole thing authored. *With a Long Spoon*: she is supping at his table now, literally touring it. (No jacket tagline came with this packet, so I'll only say the jacket line I carry in memory — that nothing would be taken she didn't hand over — has never been more exactly what's happening on the page.)

What I want next: the measuring scene, obviously — I want it and I'm braced for it, because it will be the most exposed she's ever chosen to be. Guesses from here: the fitting is the threshold — the bedroom door opens on or after it; the "couple of weeks" of dressmaking is also the runway to whatever Randi's re-entry looks like, and Randi has been conspicuously absent from Vee's head for a whole chapter; and someday Vee will remember there were two towels.
