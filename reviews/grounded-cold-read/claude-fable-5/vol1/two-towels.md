# Cold read (grounded) — Two Towels

## Reader reaction

The dress reveal wrecked me, and I want to say that first, before I say anything about what I know that Vee doesn't. "He'd matched *her*" — not the magazine shade, her — and the mechanism of it is exactly the mechanism I fell for on the summit in "Leave No Trace": she named a wanted thing in the same breath she let it go of it, he said "That sounds like a beautiful dress," took another cherry, and I thought that was the whole grace note. It wasn't. He kept it. "He'd gone and found the cloth and set aside the weeks and waited for her to come out here, so he could hand her back the thing she'd thrown away, made real." My eyes did the same hot-and-full thing hers did. That is the swoon, undiluted, and I refuse to pretend it didn't work on me.

And then there's the rest of what I am carrying into this house, which Vee is not.

Because I have been in this house. I was there in chapter one. The den with "a long low table" — she crouched down and put her fingers on the joint. On the joint. I actually went cold. That is almost certainly the bench, the "table" Randi laid her underwear on, the restraint furniture with its lid on, and Vee is admiring the joinery with her fingertips while he watches "her hand on the wood more than her face." The page never says it's the bench, and I'm holding that open — but the checkpoint memory of "disguised as a table with its lid on" was ringing so loudly I could barely read the paragraph as Vee reads it, which is: beautiful craftsmanship, a man who makes things. Both readings are on the page at once. That's the book's whole trick and this is its purest execution yet.

Then the title did its work. Two Towels. A plain, clean bathroom, "two towels on the rod" — six words, no weight, exactly like "my bedroom." Vee walks past it. I could not. Who is the second towel for? I know who the second towel is for. Or — do I? It could be nothing, a man with a guest towel. But in a house where the heat is left on ("Too warm — a close, even heat"), where I know a woman comes to be undressed, the too-warm house and the second towel read as Randi's residue, the other life sitting in plain sight, and Vee "filed it under the small mysteries of him and let it go, the way you walk past a wrapped box on the counter without asking who it's for." That simile is doing so much — she is the wrapped box. The title gave nothing away before I read; after, it's the quietest, most sinister title so far, and I mean sinister in the way this book earns it: nothing happened. She just didn't ask.

Her at the door — the kiss she'd "made up her mind to take somewhere back on the paved road" — I loved. She keeps doing this: the tree, the truck, now the stoop. Her yes arrives before his ask, every time, and it's hers, and that's precisely what makes the architecture underneath so hard to hold. And then he stops the kiss — "she had been the one to reach — and no boy she'd ever been with had once been the one to stop. That had always been her job, never theirs." That landed on me the way it landed on her: as erotic, as safety, as something in her going "still and started paying attention." I also know it's technique. He does not spend want; he banks it. "She went, the want still lit in her with nowhere to put it" — that's a sentence about her and it's also a description of the whole courtship.

The closed bedroom door is the chapter's cruelest small beat, and it's cruel in a way I felt in my body. She wore the satin out there on purpose — "the satin a girl wears hoping the man won't hand her a reason not to show it to him" — and leaned toward the door, and he said "my bedroom" with no weight at all and left it shut, and "something in her dropped." Mine too. I wanted him to open it — that's the honest report; I wanted it for her — and simultaneously I know the door stays shut because the door staying shut is the engine. Daphne taught him doors. And the measuring session is the door he opened instead: "down to what you'd wear under it, in good light, so I can see how it'll fall." Reader, I gasped a little. It is a completely legitimate dressmaker's sentence and it is also the most elegant possible request for her to stand nearly naked and be studied, and she said "Yes — of course, yes... no hedge in it" — the freest yes she's given yet, to the most designed thing yet. "He couldn't make it without seeing her. That was the only thing standing between her and it — her own willingness to be looked at." The jacket's sentence — nothing taken that she didn't hand over — has never been more literal.

The middle of the chapter, the tour, is where my guard kept dropping despite myself. The shoe closet ("Depends how many things he does"), the KitchenAid worn to shine, the fantasy paperbacks with "one with a knight and a girl in a long gown standing close on the cover" — a knight and a girl in a gown, on his boyhood shelf, in the chapter where he offers to build her a gown; that one's either lovely or chilling and I genuinely can't pick. The error-correcting codes bit is my favorite pure-character beat in the book so far: "Say it more than once so the noise can't kill it." That's also how he courts — the message arrives redundantly, shirt and sandwich and moleskin and dress, so no single doubt can kill the signal. And the patent — "he lit up like a six-year-old who'd just found out everything there was to know about dinosaurs," the MRI, "somebody sick, somebody scared, a kid who can't lie still" — for one paragraph I just liked him, no asterisk, and then "Something crossed him at *parents* — a small closing, there and gone, a door easing shut two rooms away" reminded me the man is mostly closed doors. New question opened there: what's behind the parents door. Also — "the shirt" she'd worn into the ground with no tag in it: so the flannel was his make too. That retro-charged "Rye" for me. She's been wearing his handwork on her skin for weeks without knowing it. Marked, in a sense, before she ever crossed the threshold.

Friction: very little. The only line where I felt the author's hand slightly was Vee's blunt "Are your parents rich?" — it's flagged as blunter than she'd choose, so it's covered, but it exists mostly to open the patent aria. I forgave it instantly because the aria is so good. And I did notice no Randi at all in this chapter — not even a text — and her absence from Vee's mind, in Randi's own theater, is loud to me in a way that's my knowledge, not the page's failure.

Trust vs. suspicion: unchanged in direction, deepened in degree. I believe every kindness in this chapter is real — and I watched the whole visit run on rails: banked want, withheld door, one open path (the measuring) lit up like a runway. "He's hard to say no to" in Randi's key played under the entire tour.

The book title keeps sharpening. *A Polite Invitation* — "would you come to my house Saturday. Two o'clock" is the politest invitation imaginable, and now "I'd like to make it for you... If you'll let me" tops it. *With a Long Spoon* — she just ate at the devil's table and admired the joinery. And the tagline — "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — this chapter is that sentence dramatized: her yes to the dress was the warmest, truest, most freely given yes in the book, and I felt the problem in my teeth.

What I want next: the measuring scene, obviously — I want it and I'm braced for it; it's the most charged thing the book has ever set up. Guesses from here: the measuring session becomes the first fully erotic Vee–Pace scene, consent-perfect on its surface; the bench-table sits in the room or nearby while it happens; and at some point Vee's eyes will pass over something of Randi's — the second towel, the cold-citrus scent, blue polish — and either catch or not. I dread the day the flannel, the dress, and the bench turn out to be exhibits in the same case.
