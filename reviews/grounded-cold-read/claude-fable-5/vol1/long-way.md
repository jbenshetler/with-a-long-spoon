# Cold read (grounded) — The Long Way

## Reader reaction

This chapter is short, and it did something to me that the long ones haven't: it made me ache on Vee's behalf while she was happy. Nothing bad happens in it. That's exactly the problem, and the book knows it.

Start with the swoon, because the swoon is real and I won't pretend otherwise. The froyo date works on me straight. Pace's little systems lecture — "Tart on the bottom holds up under the heavy ones. You put chocolate on tart and it's an argument the whole way down" — is charming in the precise register the book has taught me is his: unhurried, considered, faintly absurd, entirely sincere. "I've thought about most things," said "without apology," is a perfect line for him. And the closing beat is the best small thing in the chapter: she confesses — "This is the nicest a person has been to me in a really long time. And it's froyo." — and he doesn't pretend and doesn't make it large, he lets "the true thing have its space," and then: "Your tart's holding up." I melted at that. It's tenderness disguised as logistics, which is Pace's entire native language, and Vee hears it correctly. The held door with his back, the fistful of napkins, the walk "not toward anything" — I was warm the whole way through.

And underneath the warmth I could feel the floor. Because I know what Vee doesn't. I watched Pace point at her from a retaining wall. I watched Randi engineer the collision with a decade of dance training. I watched Randi sit at that coffee table lifting a cup to her mouth and not sipping while her boyfriend charmed the girl she picked. So when Randi grins and says "He wants to see you naked" — a line that plays as girl-talk — I hear a woman who knows firsthand exactly what he wants, saying a true thing inside a costume, again. And when she demolishes the wait-a-day rule — "The rule's for boys you're not sure about. You're not unsure." — she's right about Vee, and she's also greasing the track of a plan Vee has never been shown. The chapter never nudges me toward this reading. It doesn't have to. The dramatic irony is fully loaded from the previous chapters and this one just lets it sit under a sweet scene like a stone under a picnic blanket. Nothing on the page is unkind to Vee. Everything on the page is happening to a person who doesn't know the terms of her own evening. That's the ache.

The loudest moment in the chapter is a silence. Vee overfills her cup, "and it got away from her — too much, the cone of it leaning. Usually she heard her mother before she'd finished, that's plenty, that's enough now. Today there was nothing." Set that against everything I know about appetite in this book — Randi's thin apple slices, "I can't eat that" at the cheeseburger, "Let's go feed you," the mother who "lived on egg whites and tofu, a thing she said with a knife in it." Vee's mother's voice went quiet in Pace's company, and she "found she didn't care." One girl in this book is portioned to almost nothing and the man noticed and fed her; the other girl's inner ration-keeper simply switches off around him. I don't know yet whether that's liberation or the first sign of how completely he disarms people, and I'm holding it open — but as a felt beat it's gorgeous, and it's doing more work than any line of dialogue in the chapter.

Character movement. Vee deepened for me in two quick strokes: "It was easier to go looking for the flaw than to sit in the wanting" — that's the same girl who wore the cardigan over the better shirt, and I believe her completely — and Cassie's healer line, which lands a whole prior relationship in one sentence: "A whole semester of you up until two, keeping a business major alive in a video game." So Vee has done this before — organized her nights around servicing some unremarkable man's needs and called it devotion. Cassie remembers, and Cassie is warning her, in Cassie's four-words-a-day way, and Vee waves it off in one line. After "Goodbye, Vee," Cassie is becoming the person I trust most in this book and the one nobody listens to. Pace held steady — genuinely attentive on the page, and I want to be careful here: nothing in the froyo scene reads as technique. It reads as a man who actually listens. The trouble isn't in how he treats her; it's in what she hasn't been told. Randi I watch now the way Cassie watches her. This chapter gives me none of her interior — it's all Vee's chapter — and I notice the withholding: the last time I was inside Randi she was deciding, "quiet as a deadbolt going over," that Pace would never see her again. What she wants from handing him Vee, I genuinely can't fix, and the page doesn't fix it either.

Heat: low, deliberately. One frank line from Randi and then a clothed, blue-evening walk. After the bench and the bed, this cool chapter is the right temperature — it makes the tenderness legible instead of burying it.

Friction: almost none. If I reach: the chapter is slight, more a beat than a movement, but it's a beat the book needed — Vee's first hour alone with him had to be this ordinary and this kind for everything around it to hurt. I bought all of it.

The title. "The Long Way" — they walk it, literally, "not toward anything." But it's also Pace's signature phrase turned into a chapter name: "I'm not stopping. I'm taking my time." He took Randi the long way on the bench; Vee went "home the long way, in no hurry at all" after Chi Latte; now he's taking Vee the long way too — the courtship version of the same patience. The title didn't give anything away; it recolored on the last paragraph, which is the good kind. As for A Polite Invitation — "May I call you?" was one, the froyo text is another, and I'm increasingly sure the book means the politeness itself as the thing to watch: every door in this story is held open beautifully, and nobody mentions what's on the other side. With a Long Spoon keeps ringing its proverb at me — sup with the devil, bring a long spoon — and I notice, a little helplessly, that this book keeps handing people literal spoons: the ringing cups at Chi Latte, the fistful Vee grabs here. No tagline was in my packet, so I've only the titles to go on.

What I want next, and what I dread: I want more of Vee and Pace exactly like this, and I dread it, because I don't think this shape can hold. Guesses from here: Vee is falling for him as hers, not as a shared adventure — nothing in her chapters knows the word "threesome" — and the moment the geometry surfaces it will land on the girl with the least information. And Randi's not-sipping hands tell me the plan is already costing her something she hasn't priced. I turned the last page wanting the next one immediately.
