# Cold read (grounded) — All Told

## Reader reaction

Felt read

This one is small and I loved it, and the loving it has a wire running through it that I want to be honest about.

First the warmth, because the chapter is mostly warmth. "The one place she got both her girls in a single room" — that line did more to me than any of the plot in it. Vee walking in "fit to burst," underslept and overfull, dropping into the seat they left open *between* them: after twenty-one chapters of a girl who prices menus and reads rooms for exits, watching her be the person two people saved a seat for nearly undid me. And the chapter knows exactly which of the two flanking presences to spend its best money on. Cassie. Cassie who "had cracked it hours ago off the made bed, and let Randi have it anyway" — that's a whole ethics of friendship in one sentence. And then the quiet reveal that lands harder than anything loud could: Vee surfaces from forty honest seconds of note-taking and realizes Cassie never went down into the numbers at all, has been turned a few degrees in her seat the whole time, "because the friend was the only part of any of it Cassie had ever been able to see." Then the plainest exchange in the book — "Was it a good night?" "Yes. … Oh, yes." — and "something eased in Cassie's face, and she picked her highlighter back up." I trust Cassie without reservation and this chapter is why. She has been holding something (the "Goodbye, Vee," the folded-away looks), and here I finally saw what the holding is *for*: she was waiting to know Vee was okay. She got her answer. Highlighter up. I could cry about the highlighter.

Now the wire. The professor is doing it to me again. Latent variables were announced over Vee's head in ch4 the day Randi sat down beside her; now it's Simpson's paradox: "Each year on its own, the first man is the better hitter. Put the two years together and the second man is. Same numbers. Nobody touched one of them." Reader, I felt that in my spine. Because that is this book. Take Vee-and-Pace on its own: tender, consensual, the best thing that's ever happened to her. Take Vee-and-Randi on its own: the warmest friendship of her life. Put the two together — add the variable she can't see — and the verdict reverses without a single fact changing. *Same numbers. Nobody touched one of them.* Every kindness stays a kindness and the total still flips. I'll be honest that I could feel the author's hand on this one — it's the third time a lecture has doubled for the plot, and this is the most legible of the three — but I don't experience it as a thumb on the scale so much as the book saying the true thing out loud where only I can hear it, which has become one of my favorite cruelties in this novel. It gave me an actual chill, so I can't call it a miss.

And Randi. God, Randi in this chapter. "Vivienne Thorne. It is a Friday" — the lamp of her attention, the scandalized flat hand, "You lucky slut" low and delighted. She's a joy to be around and I never once forgot what I know. Two moments pricked me, both earned by the text and not by my guard. One: "slut" landing "lower than Randi meant it to" — down into the soreness Vee's body has been keeping account of — the mother's "floozie" register arriving by accident from the friend's mouth, and Vee suddenly sure the whole room can read the night off her. Randi didn't intend it; the word still found the old bruise. Two: "there was the one snag in the gold of the day, and Randi had reached in and found it." That is Randi's entire method in one sentence — locate the lack, arrive as the solution — and the chapter states it as plainly as the professor states his paradox. The shoe date is probably real generosity *and* it is Randi taking possession of the outfit, of Saturday, of a "heel height Vee would need and hadn't known to need." Both live at once; I can't apportion them, and I've stopped trying, which I think is the point. And I notice — the checkpoint has taught me to notice — that Vee's "don't you dare tell me no" warmth is exactly the yes-freely-given the jacket warned me about.

Heat: low simmer only, but real — the night's account written into her body ("a good soreness no gym had ever given her, down low, and up the inside of both thighs"), the fear-thrill of being legible. Nothing happens; everything is remembered in the flesh. It works.

The title. "All Told" is doing at least three things and I admire all of them. Randi's command — "Tell me everything. Leave nothing out" — which Vee, as ever, does not obey; the middle stays ladled out, the dress "still shown to not one living soul." The accountant's sense — *all told*, the sum — which is literally Simpson's paradox: the parts say one thing and the total says another. And the bitter reader-facing sense: nobody in this book has been told all. Cassie gets the clean version, Randi gets further, Pace withholds Randi, Randi withholds everything, and Vee — Vee has been told nothing at all. The title looked innocuous walking in and recolored the whole chapter walking out. That's the good kind. *A Polite Invitation* keeps paying out the same way — every invitation in this chapter is polite, warm, wanted, and I still counted them like a man counting knives. The jacket fragments I carry ("with a long spoon," every yes freely given "was the problem") sit over the shoe date like weather.

Friction: almost none. It's a breather chapter and it knows it; at this length nothing drags. If I squint, the closing sentence — "the dress three blocks back in a closet, zipped in its bag, still shown to not one living soul" — leans a touch on its own significance, but it's also just true and lovely, so I'll allow it.

What I want and dread next: the shoe date, obviously — Randi and Vee alone, all Saturday, money Vee doesn't have and Randi does, heels, and two curbside kisses already in the ledger. My guess from here: that outing moves the Vee–Randi line forward in a way Vee won't be able to explain away as French. And the induction is coming — the room turning, the mother in it, the dress finally shown — and I both want it badly and am braced for it, because this book has taught me that the gold days are the ones with a snag in them.
