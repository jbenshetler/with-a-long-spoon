## Reader reaction

This one got me the way the dress got her — the trouble hidden so all I could see was the glow.

The reveal is the heart of it, and it worked on me completely. Pace breaking the doorway kiss — a kiss that was "never going to stay gentle," and didn't — because he can't wait one more second to show her, "grinning, helpless with it, the way a kid can't keep a surprise in his mouth" — that detail is what makes him so hard to hold at arm's length. He built the pedestal, moved it, hung a full-length mirror with "the screw heads still bright," hung the dress where the sun would come through it. It's the heat-turned-up-hours-early care again, staged into a room. And then the line that did the most work on me, in both directions at once: "he'd taken all that trouble and then taken the further trouble to hide the trouble, so the only thing left for her to see was the part that looked like no trouble at all." Vee reads it as love and cries the dumb happy kind. I read it as love too — and then felt the floor tilt, because that sentence is also the exact architecture of her entire courtship. Everything in her life right now looks like no trouble at all because the trouble was hidden. I don't think the book winked at me; the line is honestly about the dress. But it doubled on its own, and it's the coldest warm sentence I've read in this book.

The mirror sequence is the erotic peak, and it's her alone — which feels like the point. The dress coming down "like water finding its level," the lining set only where "the dress had decided not to give away," and then the slit: "the silk parted and gave her whole leg to the room, calf to the jut of her hip." When the pale edge of her underwear cuts the line, her first thought is to keep her steps small — the old Vee, minimizing — and the second "came up hot behind the first and didn't care what showed." She strips them off herself, alone, before he's even seen, and "the shame of it went through her hot... and it crossed into wanting almost before it was shame at all, and she let it come." That's the ch18 engine — shame running the whole length and never closing her — now self-administered, no one in the room. *Floozy* flares and loses. Randi's ch1 mirror ended in a vow to seal herself off; Vee's mirror ends in a stride. I felt the rhyme in my chest.

Then she walks to him — "the length of her given and taken back and given" — hooks the bare leg around him, and the chapter fades. Honestly? A flicker of greed in me wanted it to stay on the page the way "Famished" did; the cut to "nothing left for either of them to decide" is graceful but I felt the door close. The afterglow buys it back, though: the two of them stopping mid-frenzy — "wait, the dress" — to hang it "with the two-handed care you'd give something alive" is the most married thing anyone has done in this book, and it melted me. So did her deducing, from how hard he sleeps, that he finished the dress at some ungodly hour "while she slept across town, not knowing."

And the induction daydream — standing in the middle of the light, letting the room turn, "the looking didn't cost her a thing. It just felt like what she was for." I want that for her so badly. Which is the trap, because the last line is "getting away with something," and she isn't the one getting away with anything. Also filed, uneasily: the other girls in "safe department-store navy," her mother presumably in that room, and the shoes she doesn't own — "the shoes this called for weren't anything she owned, and that woman could wait." My guess from here: someone provides the shoes, and I'd put money on Randi, which would fold her fingerprints onto Vee's body at the induction itself. Marked as a guess.

No new suspicion earned inside the scene — Randi never appears, isn't even mentioned, and this is, I think, the first chapter with literally no shadow cast over the two of them. That absence is its own weather. Four straight chapters of Vee's happiness now, each one escalating her authorship, and the altitude is starting to make my ears pop.

The title: "Peekaboo" is the slit — open, shut, open, "given and taken back and given" — and his kid-with-a-surprise grin, and the underwear's edge appearing and then removed. Innocuous going in, charged coming out. But peekaboo is also a game about a face that vanishes and returns, seen/not-seen, and this book has a woman whose face is a decision — I can't tell if the title means to reach that far, and I'm leaving it ajar. *A Polite Invitation* and the long-spoon warning sit quietly under "getting away with something": every yes in this chapter was freely given, and that is still, apparently, the problem.
