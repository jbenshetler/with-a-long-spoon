# Cold read (grounded) — Old Acquaintances

## Reader reaction

This one hurt, and it hurt in the exact way the book has been teaching me to be hurt: by letting me stand on both sides of a pane of glass at once.

Because I just came off "Fairytale," I read this whole chapter in stereo. Vee lies in her too-small childhood bed scrolling Randi's feed — the ski run, the golden chalet windows, "bare shoulders, white-gold bracelet, and the solitaire at her throat" — and aches for it, "and it did not matter that she knew what had bought the dream." And I know what that feed actually holds: a woman lying awake next to "the perfect nothing," warm only along one seam, putting her face back on at the mirror at two in the morning. The text exchange lands twice — I already read both messages from Randi's side, word for word, and getting Vee's side of the same 11:52 now, "She read it twice for anything that gave her away, found nothing, and sent it. Put the phone facedown. Turned it back over to be sure it had gone" — that little compulsive flip of the phone gutted me. Two women, each convinced the other is living the real life, each sending the other a carefully laundered version of her own loneliness. "Every word of it true. None of it the thing" was Randi's line last chapter; this chapter shows me Vee doing the identical laundering in the other direction, and neither knows. That mirroring isn't a trick I caught the author at — it's the chapter's whole engine, and it worked on me completely.

How I feel about Vee: this is one of her best chapters and there's no sex in it at all. The childhood room is rendered with a precision that made my chest tight — "The room did not know her. It had known Vivienne — knew her still, in high resolution, and loved her... The room kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed." The knees bent because her legs outgrew the bed at the end of middle school. The clarinet in the closet. The cropped induction photo downstairs on her mother's phone — "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it" — that's the Vivienne/Vee split made literal and it's the best single image in the chapter. And then the spite-blonde. Watching her build Pace's imaginary next woman "from spite: blonde, tan, a fashion model's body where Vee's was a stripper's" — handing him the exact opposite of herself — and then watching her one warm memory, the fra diavolo night in the falling sheet, get colonized by that invention while she's inside it trying to be warm: "She could not keep her own night." That's the cruelest interior turn the book has done to her, and it's entirely self-inflicted, which is what makes it true. Jealousy at midnight, alone, is exactly this shape.

Pace: my feeling about him is doing something complicated, and I want to be precise. Nothing here changes the facts — she walked out, he said her name once, and "he left you the room. He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now." That line is the chapter in miniature: his signature virtue, the courtesy that has been the erotic core of the whole book, reread as abandonment from the far side of a fight. And I can't tell — the page can't tell, Vee can't tell — whether the reread is jealous distortion or a real finding. "May I" and "the room to choose" were the swoon of the first thirty chapters; here is the bill for them. A man who never claims you also never has to name you. I don't think he's cooking for a blonde. I do think his silence across the whole break is data, and I notice the book pointedly not letting me see his week, same as Vee.

Randi: the dramatic irony around her has gone from suspense to grief. Vee calls her "the year's gift she had literally stumbled into" — and I flinched at "stumbled," because I watched the stumble get choreographed in chapter 3. And "the only girl she could talk to about real things" lands on top of Randi's own resolution last chapter — keep the brunches, keep the word under cover, "let Vee arrive at it on her own." Vee reaching for Randi as her one sure thing — "a day she could circle for the one and nothing at all for the other" — while Randi lies in Gstaad having just decided Vee is the realest thing in her life: I want them to have each other and I know the wanting is booby-trapped and I want it anyway. That's the book working exactly as its jacket promised.

Erotic charge: essentially none, and rightly. The one flicker is the memory of the sheet slipping "and him seeing and her not fixing it" — warm, and then stolen from her mid-memory. This is an ache chapter, not a heat chapter, and after "Under the Rug" and "Fairytale" the cold is the right note; three chapters of everyone lying awake unnamed and unheld, each colder than the last.

Friction: very little. If I push, the "stripper's body" self-description is harsher than Vee's usual self-talk — though at midnight, mid-spite-spiral, I buy it as the mother's old "floozie" voice wearing new clothes. And I registered how neatly Sheri's Thanksgiving gets repurposed as evidence ("He had cooked for Sheri... about Christmas he'd said nothing at all") — but that's Vee's mind doing the fitting, not the author's thumb; jealousy is exactly this good a prosecutor.

The title: "Old Acquaintances" is Auld Lang Syne, of course — the song of this exact hour — and it opens three ways at once. The room full of old acquaintances who no longer know her (the faces on the corkboard "who hadn't texted in a year"). Should auld acquaintance be forgot — Vivienne herself, the girl the room keeps dusted. And, bitterly, the two people she's reaching toward at midnight, both of whom are older acquaintances of each other than she will ever guess. It gave nothing away and paid out on every line. The book title keeps sharpening: the invitation's politeness now includes its silences — he leaves her the room, Randi leaves her the shape to arrive at on her own; every door held open, nobody ever pulled through. "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" — and this chapter shows the flip side: the things never asked can never be answered.

What I want next: term to start. I want the reunion with Randi that Vee has circled — and I dread it a little, because Randi comes back having decided to run a covert courtship inside the friendship. And I need to know what Pace does with the walked-out door. Guesses from here: he won't reach out first — leaving the room is constitutional in him — so Vee will have to drive back out Route 614 herself, and whatever she finds there won't be a blonde; the danger to her was never another woman. And the pile "she did not look at" — the laundry-room pile of small repairs — is going to matter before Randi's secret does.
