# Cold read (grounded) — Old Acquaintances

## Reader reaction

This one hurt, and it hurt in the best way this book has: by showing me the other side of a page I'd already read. "Fairytale" ended with Randi in a Gstaad hotel room, putting her face back on, sending Vee the fairytale — "every word of it true. None of it the thing." Now I'm in the twin bed those texts landed in, and the symmetry is quietly devastating. Two women, one midnight, both cold in rooms that aren't cold, both holding phones over empty fields, both turning them facedown, both composing messages engineered to conceal, and each meaning exactly one line — Randi's "miss your face most though, gorgeous," Vee's "thinking about you." They are lying to each other and loving each other in the same breath, and neither knows the other is doing the identical thing. I got to watch Vee read Randi's text and swallow it whole — "She was fine. Better than fine… a devastatingly handsome billionaire, a weekend so wild Vee wouldn't recognize half of what they did" — knowing that on the other end of it a woman was lying down against "the perfect nothing" in warmth "she could borrow and could not make." Vee envies the exact night I watched fail. That's not a trick; that's the book cashing a check it set up honestly, and it's the strongest structural move in a while.

The room broke my heart before any of that. "The room did not know her. It had known Vivienne — knew her still, in high resolution, and loved her." The little gold swimmers with their toes pointed, the clarinet in the closet, the corkboard girls "who hadn't texted in a year," the bed her legs outgrew in middle school — this is the whole arc of the book rendered as furniture. Her body outgrew the bed the way it outgrew the swimming; her self outgrew the room the way it outgrew "Vivienne." And the cropped photo lands its cruelest recurrence yet: the induction picture, whole on Pace's phone, "cut off at the waist" on her mother's — "Same picture, one house, and she was the one who had divided it." After "Cropped" (the chapter where she told Cassie she cropped it for her parents), this is the motif saying its quiet part out loud: she is living in halves, and she did the scissoring herself.

Where I am with each of them. Vee — I have never felt for her more, and the chapter earns it without once begging. The spite-built blonde is the truest ugly thing she's done on the page: "She handed Pace the exact opposite of herself and named it the thing he would want the moment he was free of her, and she believed it, lying there — what you believe at midnight." And then the fantasy corruption — she reaches for her best memory, the sheet and the fra diavolo and the wine light, to get warm, "and it turned while she was in it. … She could not keep her own night." That sequence is the chapter's peak and it's brutal: even her interior refuge has been colonized by the fear. The dramatic irony is a knife I have to hold carefully — she invents a blonde rival out of nothing while the actual other woman is the one she just texted goodnight, the one whose reply lit her ceiling and comforted her. The rival she fears is imaginary; the rival she loves is real.

Pace — my feeling moved, and I want to be careful about how. From inside Vee's skull, his silence across the whole break is damning: she walked out crying and there has been "not a word," and "he left you the room. He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now" is the best single line in the chapter about how a virtue curdles into an absence. But I know things she doesn't. I know he said "I want her to get there on her own"; I know he held a line "at cost" in "Believe Me" and reached for her as she left. So his silence reads two ways at once — a man respecting a door she closed, or the machinery running even now, letting the hunger build the way he builds hunger before every meal. Vee herself makes that connection and it chilled me: "the same building, then giving, that his hands worked on her in the dark. And now he was doing it for someone, and she didn't get to know who." The page leaves it open whether the someone exists at all, and I'm leaving it open too — but I notice the book has taught me to be unable to tell his patience from his technique, and that inability is now Vee's too. She's caught up to my dread without any of my facts.

Randi — retroactively enriched. Her text was already the one true line in a lie; watching Vee receive it as proof of a glamorous stranger's life, "another country," makes Randi lonelier in memory than she was even at that mirror. And the detail that Vee knows about the fraud money and envies the mountains anyway — "even a poisoned Gstaad was more life than she had" — is such honest, unflattering interiority. Vee keeping Randi's secret, tenderly, inside a message designed to demand nothing: "an open hand offered." These two are being so careful with each other, and every carefulness is also a concealment.

Swoon and heat, plainly: this chapter runs cold on purpose and I felt the cold. The only warmth is remembered or imagined — the fra diavolo night, the arms she lets herself have at midnight "and it came, and it would not hold." The eroticism is present only as loss, and that's right for where we are; I didn't want a sex scene here and the book didn't flinch into one.

Friction: almost none. If I reach — this is the fifth chapter in a row orbiting the unsaid word (Seconds, Under the Rug, Bare, Believe Me, now this), and I can feel the beat repeating; but the New Year frame, the Randi mirror, and the fantasy corruption each refresh it enough that I never sagged. The middle stretch re-summarizing the fight ("Maybe it was over. Maybe he thought it was…") is the one passage that told me things I already had, though it's brief and true to insomniac looping.

The title: "Old Acquaintances" is Auld Lang Syne — should old acquaintance be forgot — and it opens in every direction at once. The corkboard girls who stopped texting. Vivienne, the old acquaintance the room still loves and Vee no longer is. And the dread reading: that Pace, nameless and silent, might be becoming one — demoted from the unnameable thing to someone she used to know. It didn't give anything away; it deepened on finishing, which is what this book's titles do at their best. *A Polite Invitation* and *With a Long Spoon* keep their long-game menace: every door here was opened politely, and Vee is now starving in the gap between invitations. (I wasn't given a cover tagline in this packet, so I won't invent one.)

What I want next, and dread: term starts, and Vee has circled Randi's day on the calendar — "a face that would light up and call her gorgeous and mean it" — while Pace comes with "no hour she could name." The wound Pace's silence left is exactly the size and shape of Randi, and Randi has just resolved to "let Vee arrive at it on her own." My guess from here: the reunion brunch is where the two loneliest chapters in the book collide, and whatever Vee confides about the fight hands Randi — lover of both of them, liar to both of them — a live wire. I also haven't forgotten the sorority knows Randi's boyfriend is named Pace. I turned the last page wanting the next one immediately, which after five chapters of ache is its own verdict.
