# Cold read (grounded) — The New Ordinary

## Reader reaction

This chapter did the thing this book keeps doing to me: it made me completely happy for about four thousand words and then dropped one green cold stone into the middle of the warm, and I'm still holding it.

The warm first, because the warm is most of it and it worked. Five nights apart — her own call, for school, and I loved that it was hers; the girl who used to apologize for her own appetite told a careful man she needed her nights back, and he gave them without making her pay. And then the honest, slightly crooked interior underneath: "some unreasonable corner of her had wanted him to fight, to want the nights badly enough to say so out loud." That's exactly the grain of truth this book is best at — his grace costing her more than a fight would have. I believed every syllable of it, including the small worry it plants: he never says so. He never says anything. The chapter itself notices — "He was good at not asking things" — and after Daphne's door, after "I don't go back," that quality reads two ways at once, and I let it stay two ways.

The reunion is one of the sexiest sequences in the book precisely because it's the least staged. She's towing him down the hall by two fistfuls of shirt, the jeans become "the enemy," she's hopping one-legged with denim inside out at her ankle, laughing so hard she has to stop laughing to finish undressing — "there was no dignity anywhere in any of it and she'd have burned the house down for him." That line got me. And then the bed, and the first one breaking "shamefully fast," and this, which is my favorite sentence in the chapter: "her body having just published the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private." Five nights, counted by her body, read out by her own throat. The shame-and-heat fusion the book has been building since the fitting is all here, but notice what's changed: "she took him without question now, the old bracing habit long gone, nothing left in her that wanted to argue the point." The Victorian LARPing is gone. That's the title doing its work — this is what ordinary is now.

And then the stone. "The thong she took off herself, three steps into the room, and threw. It landed green on the low table of odd proportions that stood in the middle of his bedroom. She never looked to see where it went." I went cold. I know that table. Chapter one opened on it — the bench, the hand-built apparatus disguised as a low table that Randi used to lay her underwear on, the thing that broke her open and made her lock the bathroom door forever. It is in his bedroom, in the middle of the floor, and Vee's underwear just landed on it exactly the way Randi's used to, and Vee doesn't know what it is, and the prose gives it half a sentence — "odd proportions" — and moves on the way Vee moves on. Nobody telegraphed anything. It's the purest example yet of how this book plants dread: one object, correctly named, in a scene of total love. I sat with that green scrap of satin on that lid for the rest of the chapter.

The flannel scene at the end undid me, and I want to be plain that it did. She washed the shirt — the one from the rain on the hike, the one that "went stale, and then it went to nothing" after the Rusty Nail disaster when she washed it and it smelled of no one — and she hands it back not as a return but as a commission: "I need you to wear it for me. The whole weekend. And give it back to me Sunday so it smells like you again." His face doing puzzlement, then the start of hurt, then the open warm thing when he understands — and then he pulls it on right there, over the shirt he's already wearing, and stands in his own kitchen in two shirts because she told him to. "Smelling like you." "Smelling like me." That exchange is the romantic peak of the book so far for me. It's also — and I say this with the checkpoint's whole architecture in my head — the moment I most wanted the design not to exist. She has invented a ritual of him. She is going to carry his smell home in a shirt and sleep inside it. If the frame around this ever comes down on her, this is the scene that will hurt worst, and the chapter knows nothing about that, and that's why it lands.

The pizza interlude I'd normally flag as indulgence — this is the fourth or fifth sex-then-he-cooks movement in the recent run, and I felt the pattern this time — but it buys its length with two things: "He'd have made the pizza exactly like this for an empty house," which is the best single observation anyone has made about Pace's care (it isn't performance, it's just where his attention lands, and she "could not have said, watching him, which of them got the larger share, and found she didn't mind" — a sentence I turned over twice, because not minding is either peace or a warning); and the tasting paragraph — "the difference between cotton candy and a bowl of cut strawberries" — which continues the waking-up-of-her-mouth thread from brunch. Still: as a beat, the domestic-bliss chapter is starting to repeat. What saves this one from sameness is the bench and the shirt.

Who's not here matters too. No Randi. No Cassie. Not one other name in the whole chapter — after "The Outlier" cracked Randi's family open, the book hands me a chapter of pure two-person warmth with the bench sitting silently in the middle of it. That contrast is the pacing doing something deliberate-feeling, and it worked on me: the quieter this is, the louder the waiting things get.

Trust and suspicion, honestly stated: within this chapter I suspect no one — every gesture of his is generous, every yes of hers is hers, freely and joyfully given. And that's exactly the trouble the jacket named: "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." This chapter is wall-to-wall freely given yeses — the drawer, the shirt, her body's published number — and the only shadow is an object she threw her underwear onto without looking. *The New Ordinary* as a title is perfect and a little terrible: the extraordinary thing has become her ordinary life — her space, her drawer, her ritual — which means she now has an ordinary that can be taken from her. *A Polite Invitation* keeps feeling less like one invitation and more like a standing one she's moved into. *With a Long Spoon* I still hear as the proverb, and chapters like this are how you forget you're at that table.

What I want next: I want the shirt back on Sunday smelling like him and I want that to be all this ever is, and I don't believe it will be. Guesses from here: the bench gets introduced to Vee — the spanking thread has been walking toward it for chapters (the bet, Randi's story, her body's answer), and her underwear just landed on its lid like a promise the furniture made to itself. And the anthro class, "just us," is still sitting out there with Randi's roughened voice under it. I also haven't forgotten MIRH — Randi came back "brighter than she'd been all afternoon," and I don't trust brightness from Randi.
