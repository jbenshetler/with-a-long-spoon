## Reader reaction

This chapter wrecked me a little, and it did it with a hug.

I came into it raw from the run of chapters before — Vee saying her best sentence into the pre-dawn and getting "I love how good we are together" back, the Chili's disaster, "Believe Me" and her walking out into the cold, then the split-screen New Year's where I got to be inside Randi's Gstaad emptiness while Vee lay in her outgrown bed building a blonde out of spite. So when Vee cries most of the way in from Roanoke and lands at the sorority house because "she knew where she went when it got like this," I was already aching for her. And then the door shuts and Randi's arms come around her "before either of them had said a word," and the hug goes "past where a hug stops, the beat longer and the beat after that, and neither of them let go" — and I felt both things at once, fully: the pure relief of Vee finally being held by someone, and the enormous doubled weight of who is doing the holding. Vee breathing her in — "bitter orange, green at the edge, nothing sweet" — and not wanting to stop is the closest the book has come to letting Vee's body say out loud what she won't. She's doing to Randi's neck exactly what she does to Pace's flannel. I don't think she noticed. I noticed.

How I feel about each character right now. Vee — undiminished love, and a new wince: watching her hand Randi the whole fight, the whole wound, and take back a plan, is watching her do the thing she has done all book, only now I know the plan restores the arrangement that Randi and Pace built. Randi — this is the chapter where I genuinely cannot separate the strands anymore, and I think that's the point. Everything she does here is good counseling. It's also true. "The fear's a liar, and you haven't slept." "Don't you dare hand him to a woman who doesn't even have a face" — and I, holding the Sheri chapter fresh, know Randi is right in a way she doesn't even fully know she's right: the imagined blonde model is a five-foot bleached-out cyclist who calls Vee "the one with the big tits" and steals Pace's fries. Randi dismantles Vee's spite-phantom with surgical kindness. And every stroke of it also serves the project, and also serves Randi's own decision from the hotel mirror — "keep the brunches exactly as they were," let Vee "arrive at it on her own." I can't call the comfort false. I can't call it clean. The book has me exactly where it wants me.

Pace — my feeling didn't move much this chapter because he's offstage, but Randi's reading of him ("That's not a man who's done with you, honey. That's a boy holding himself still so it won't show how much you got him") lands with the authority of the one person alive who actually knows him from the inside, and Vee has no idea that's why the diagnosis is so good. That's the chapter's quiet horror: the counsel is expert because the counselor has been in his bed. Brooke — the warmth-as-weapon note again, "Oh, honey," and Vee holding everything behind her teeth. Nothing detonated. The fuse is still lit; I felt it when Vee walked in the front door crying about a man this whole house knows by name.

Swoon / pull. The hug, first and hardest. Then, differently, the coat. Randi leaving the sentence unfinished — "You've got that good long coat." A beat. — and waiting for Vee to find it herself is seduction technique, verbatim: leave the room, let her choose, let choosing become the erotic thing. Vee finishing it — "And nothing under it" — and then the mother's voice arriving, "slutty" said aloud at cost, and the shame converting to heat "hand in hand with the mortification and sharper for it": that is Vee's engine, the one the book has taught me note by note, and it fired here without a man in the room. Randi lit it. Randi knows how to light it. "That's not a slut, gorgeous. That's the bravest thing a woman can do" is the most Randi sentence in the book — absolution, aim, and appetite in one breath — and I believed her and wanted Vee out that door, and only afterward remembered whose door it is and who taught the man behind it to wait.

The Gstaad retelling gutted me in a slower way. I had the real version — the borrowed warmth, the face going back on, "the flaw was hers." Vee gets the fairytale, and I got to watch Randi stop at the bedroom door of the story: "And then it got very late," she said, "and we went up." The woman who "had told Vee across a dozen tables exactly what a man's mouth had done and where" goes silent exactly where the emptiness lives, and Vee reads it as discretion. Then the lie, delivered as a quip: "He was perfect... Everywhere but the bedroom, and the bedroom's the room I actually live in." It's the precise inversion of the truth — the bedroom was the one place he was fine and she was nowhere — and it's told so lightly that Vee laughs, "really laughed for the first time all day." I didn't laugh. That line is Randi handing Vee her loneliness dressed as a punchline, and it may be the saddest sentence in the chapter. Also the small knife of Vee's envy going in sideways: "it was the exact thing she didn't have... a man who'd build the whole future out loud in the first hour and not be afraid of one word in it." Vee envying the fictional John for the very fluency Pace withholds — words spent freely, futures named — while I know the fluency was worth nothing to the woman who received it.

Trust vs. suspicion. My suspicion isn't new but it sharpened: the plan Randi hands Vee doesn't just reconcile the couple, it re-arms the whole architecture — and the black heels pressed into Vee's hands ("Randi always knew the shoes") are the shoe motif doing what it has always done, Randi dressing Vee for Pace. Whether Randi is doing this for the game, for Pace, for Vee's happiness, or to keep her own Tuesday hour safe, the page does not say, and I'm not closing it. What I'll note honestly: nothing in her comfort read as cold to me in the moment. The extraction ("What did his face do — right then?") and the care are one gesture now. I've stopped being able to price them separately, and I think that's what this book is.

Erotic charge. No sex on the page, but the chapter hums — the too-long hug, the breath off her neck, the coat plan blooming under the shame, "You've got the goods, gorgeous girl." It's a 1 by the numbers and it does more to me than some of the 3s.

Friction. Very little. One small thing: Randi's counseling beats are so uniformly effective — every question landing, every reframe accepted — that once or twice it edged toward demonstration ("'…Cornered,' she said slowly"). But Vee is sleepless, wrecked, and starving for exactly this, so I buy the compliance; it's characterization, not thumb. If anything the chapter is admirably disciplined about what it withholds — it never once tells me why Randi does any of it.

The titles. "My Friend Randi" is a dagger in five syllables. It's Vee's phrase — the possessive, the warmth, the category — and the chapter fills the word "friend" until it splits: the friend who holds you, the friend who lies to you about her New Year, the friend who is your boyfriend's lover coaching you back into his bed, the friend whose neck you breathe in a beat too long. It didn't give anything away; it accreted. A Polite Invitation — the coat plan is literally an invitation being engineered again, politely, by the same hand as the first one; and the tagline "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem" has never fit better: Vee will walk to that door tonight wanting to, and every step is hers, and the route was drawn for her. With a Long Spoon still hums under it — supping with something, at arm's length, and the arms keep shortening.

What I want / expect / dread next. I want the door to open. I want the coat scene with everything in me and I'm a little ashamed of how much — which is, I suspect, the book working as designed. Guesses from here: the reconciliation happens and is scorching; Pace still doesn't say the word; and somewhere in it — the heels Randi chose on Vee's feet in his bedroom, the low table standing there — the two halves of Vee's life stand in the same room again with only me knowing it. Longer-range dread: Brooke saw Vee cry in that front hall over a boyfriend. That house knows a name. One careless sentence.
