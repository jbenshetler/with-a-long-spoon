I was swept up by the ski day at first: Randi laughing despite herself, the easy invention of three children in the lift line, the champagne, the pale-blue dress, his face when he sees her on the stairs. I wanted that night to work for her because she so clearly wants it to be a story she can live inside. “The year I met your father, the ball, the snow.” That longing is tender to me, not foolish.

John is charming, capable, and attentive in the particular ways Randi has been taught to value. I believe he means the evening sincerely. But I felt the difference as soon as they danced: his certainty makes things easy for her, while Pace’s lighter lead leaves her room. The chapter makes that contrast land without making John cruel or ridiculous. He is not a villain; he is simply unable to find the part of Randi she keeps buried, perhaps because she has made sure he cannot.

The sex went emotionally slack for me on purpose. It is apparently good, and Randi is physically responsive, but she is far away from herself. The image of her lipstick left on him while her mouth in the mirror is bare is bleak. “Her body had spent itself on him and she remained unmoved.” That hit harder than any heat. Her return to the polished face is even sadder because it works perfectly: “The smile still worked, she checked, and meant nothing.”

My feeling toward Randi deepened here. I ache for her, and I am wary of her. Her recognition that Pace and Vee reach her where this immaculate life cannot is painfully lucid. But her decision about Vee—“Let Vee arrive at it on her own”—makes my stomach tighten. She knows she is preserving the friendship partly because she cannot bear the risk of a direct answer, yet she also chooses to keep a cover over what she means. The text has earned my suspicion of that choice, even as I understand the fear underneath it. “Vee was her gorgeous” is devastating; it feels like love and possession pressed together.

Pace appears only through Randi’s mind, but he pulls hard on the chapter. On his “worst night,” he has reached farther into her than John can at his best; that makes the current silence and secrecy around all three of them feel more fraught, not less. Vee’s text is lovely and unsuspecting—“i'm here if you want to talk. or not.”—and Randi answering with the glittering surface of the trip while adding “miss your face most though, gorgeous 💋” made me ache. It is a real tenderness, but it is also not the whole truth.

“Fairytale” gave something away emotionally before I started: I expected a beautiful, manufactured dream, and that is exactly what Randi gets. But the title turns bitter by the end. It is not a lie, exactly—her reply to Vee is “Every word of it true. None of it the thing.” The fairytale is real in all its visible parts and empty at its center. *A Polite Invitation* now feels like it promises a welcome that is exquisitely mannered, generous, and possibly devastating in what it does not ask aloud. *With a Long Spoon* still feels oblique to me, though the distance between safe appearances and dangerous intimacy is beginning to give it a faint menace. The packet’s closing beat, “Every yes was freely given. That was the problem,” feels sharper here: Randi’s yes to John is free, and it shows her what freedom without being known can feel like.

I want to keep reading because Randi has now named too much to herself to feel entirely settled. My guess from here is that she will keep trying to preserve Vee’s friendship by letting desire remain deniable—and that the deniability will get harder to live inside.
