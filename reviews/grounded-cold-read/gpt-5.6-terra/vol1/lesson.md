## Reader reaction

I had such a fizzy, hopeful feeling at the start that made the crash hurt. Vee repeating “If you want to, I’ll teach you” is exactly the kind of care she has been learning to recognize from Pace: an invitation, not an assumption. Meg’s brisk practical help is lovely too. The black shirt, the borrowed boots, the fact that Vee changes and changes back by five-thirty—she is already trying to let herself arrive as the woman who gets to be wanted.

And the lesson itself swept me up. Pace’s instruction is physical without turning slippery: “Give me something to push back against,” “Being jelly doesn’t work,” “Close it—come all the way in.” Vee’s nervousness and the bodily precision of learning a partnered dance make the closeness hot, but what lands more strongly is her discovery that she does not have to anticipate every move alone. Once she can feel the lead, she can stop scrambling. “There it is,” he says, and I felt that little triumph with her. It is a genuinely sexy scene, especially once the dance is happening in the crowded room and she lets his hand at her back give her the next step before she has thought it through.

I also felt warm toward Pace seeing him in a room where he belongs. The battered boom box, pressed jeans, familiar greetings, the fact that he teaches beginners and gets women safely home: it all broadens him. It does make Vee’s first shock understandable. She has been receiving his attention as extraordinary, and suddenly she sees evidence that he makes many women feel welcomed, graceful, and safe. But nothing on the page before the blonde speaks makes him seem false to me. His hugs are even; he introduces Vee; he brings her onto the floor. The blonde’s line—“Knows how to get a girl to do just what he wants her to”—is designed to sting, and Vee is already primed to hear the worst version of the night.

Then Vee turns cruelly inward and outward at once. I hurt for her, and I was frustrated with her. “Three dates, and she’d spread her legs like a slut”: that is her old shame seizing the first available weapon. She knows the venom in her question, and still uses it. Pace’s “Yes… Sometimes. When it’s required” is such a bare, honest answer that her walking away felt awful. I believe her jealousy; I do not blame her for having it. But I do feel she punishes him for a quality she has loved in him—his steadiness, his attention, his care for women—because it suddenly threatens the specialness she needs from him.

The bartender scene was a relief, though not an easy one. The page earns the correction: he gets intoxicated women home, leaves them at the door, and has a reputation precisely because he does not exploit that position. More affecting still is that he does not generally bring dates there, while Vee was the person he brought. “Wasn’t him they were jealous of, honey.” That landed hard. The women’s attention may still be complicated, and I can understand why it rattles Vee, but the chapter does not make Pace into a man collecting girls. It makes him a familiar, trusted fixture in a place where Vee feels newly exposed.

The ending is bleak in the right way. Vee cannot sleep in his washed shirt, trying to hold both the fact that she hurt “a good man” and the bodily memory of being led in the dance. I am glad she decides to go to Randi’s—not because Randi is uncomplicatedly safe, but because Vee has reached the clear fact: she was wrong and wants to repair it. How she will do that feels frighteningly open. I want her to apologize without making herself small, and I dread the additional mess that Randi’s knowledge and desire might bring to that conversation.

“Lesson” first means the literal dance lesson, but by the end it has become Vee’s lesson in trust, jealousy, and the difference between being guided and being controlled. The dance frame is particularly resonant: she mistakes closeness for danger, holds herself away to avoid being stepped on, and that very distance makes her stumble. I do not want to flatten that into a neat answer for her whole life, but the page clearly lets it touch more than dancing. The title did not give away the rupture; it made the opening feel sweetly practical, then deepened after the fact.
