## Reader reaction

Felt read

This one is a gift to Vee, and reading it, I felt like a co-conspirator in giving it to her — which is exactly the discomfort the book has trained me to notice by now.

On Pace: my trust in his craft and tenderness is, if anything, higher after this chapter, purely on its own terms. The "helpless grin," the boyish inability to wait one more second to show her the dress, the pedestal moved and the mirror newly hung with "the screw heads still bright" — that's such a specific, unglamorous detail of effort, and it's the detail that gets me, more than the finished dress itself. "he'd taken all that trouble and then taken the further trouble to hide the trouble, so the only thing left for her to see was the part that looked like no trouble at all" is a genuinely lovely sentence about what devotion looks like when it doesn't want credit. And then the small aside in bed afterward — that he'd been up "at some hour with no business being awake in it" finishing the dress while she slept, unknowing — deepens him further, makes his care feel bottomless rather than performed. And yet: I can't fully unhook this from what I already know. This is the same exquisite, anticipatory attentiveness that engineered a "chance" meeting at a coffee shop. Nothing in this chapter itself is sinister — it's the opposite, it's disarmingly sweet — but the accumulated dramatic irony means even his purest gestures now arrive to me with a second reading underneath the first. That's not the chapter's fault; it's what the earlier chapters bought me, and I notice it rather than manufacture it.

On Vee: I'm entirely on her side, more than ever. The dress-in-the-window moment made me tear up a little right alongside her — "she hadn't decided to cry and she was crying" is such an accurate description of that kind of undefended happiness. And the moment on the pedestal where the shame-word flares ("Floozy") and she stays anyway, makes herself look, and "the looking stopped being shame" — that's a real, earned turn for her, not asserted but shown, built out of every earlier scene where that word ambushed her. I liked her for choosing to strip the underwear off rather than mince her steps to hide it; it read as a genuine, hot, self-authored decision rather than something done to her. The line I keep turning over, though, is the very last beat of her daydream: "the looking didn't cost her a thing. It just felt like what she was for." That's swoon and quiet worry at once for me — it's presented as liberation, and on the page it plainly is one, a girl finally allowed to be looked at without flinching. But "what she was for" is a determinist, purpose-giving phrase, and given everything the checkpoint has told me about her mother's voice and about the actual, literal purpose she was selected for by two people who haven't told her, that phrase landed with a little chill under the warmth. I don't think the text is condemning her for it — it reads like real, hard-won pleasure — but I noticed the shape of the sentence more than I think a first-time reader without my memory would.

Swoon / pull: this whole chapter is built to sweep me, and it worked. The dress catching the fall light "the way light only comes through silk," the slit opening on her bare leg and snapping shut when her feet come together, her walking to him rather than the careful walk — I felt the heat rise with her, not just watched it. And the hung-dress detail mid-lovemaking — "wait, the dress" — undercut nothing; it made the whole scene warmer, funnier, more human. That's the moment I liked best in the chapter: two people who want each other urgently, stopping anyway, out of care for a third thing between them (literally the dress, but also standing in for the care itself). It's tender in a way that felt completely trustworthy on its own terms.

Trust vs. suspicion: nothing new is earned on the page here — this chapter, read alone, would give me zero reason to distrust either of them; it's unclouded, un-ironic tenderness from Vee's side, and Pace is nothing but generous and delighted in it. My unease is entirely carried in from memory, not textual — I want to be honest that the chapter itself doesn't plant anything new, it's my accumulated knowledge doing the work. Which is its own kind of unsettling: the better and sweeter these two are to each other, the more the dramatic irony does its job on me without the prose lifting a finger.

Erotic charge: it peaks at the mirror and the walk to him — the slow build of her deciding to remove the underwear, the strip of bare leg through the slit, is more charged to me than the sex itself, which is handled almost entirely through gesture and cuts away before anything explicit ("there was nothing left for either of them to decide" — and then straight to "After"). I don't mind the fade; the anticipation carried the heat better than description would have.

Friction: very little. If I'm picking at anything, it's that the chapter is entirely one register — pure indulgence, no friction, no interruption from the outside world (no Cassie, no Randi, no economic anxiety surfacing in the moment, though it flickers in as she imagines "department-store navy" versus Milan). That's probably intentional — a held breath of happiness — but stacked against "Famished," the morning-after chapter, and "Fed," it's now four chapters in a row of Vee glowing, and I did notice the accumulation. It works because each one adds a new texture (first sex, morning shame with Cassie, extraction-lunch with Randi, now the dress) rather than repeating a beat, but I'm aware the story hasn't let anything genuinely complicate Vee's happiness yet, and I'm waiting for that.

Titles: "Peekaboo" is doing real work for me once I've read the chapter — the slit that opens and closes on her bare leg with every step is almost literally a game of peekaboo, and so is the whole structure of hide-then-reveal that defines this entire book (the hidden effort behind the "no trouble at all" dress, the closed bedroom door versus the open one, the pointing game itself). It didn't spoil anything for me going in — it read as merely playful — but coming out the other side it resonates uncomfortably with everything I already know is being hidden from Vee. It's a title that means something different to the reader who knows about the game than it would to Vee, which is exactly the position the book keeps putting me in. The book's title, *A Polite Invitation*, keeps chiming against "May I choose?" and the invitation Pace extends to her ("left the rest of it to her") — every act of this relationship is framed as an invitation Vee is free to accept or decline, and I keep hearing an echo of the other invitation she never got to accept or decline, because she doesn't know it exists. And *With a Long Spoon* — the proverb about supping with the devil — sits under this entire chapter like a held breath; nothing devilish happens on the page, everything is tender, and that's precisely why the title keeps nagging at me even here. No tagline was included in this packet, so I'm not asserting one.

What I want next: I want to keep believing in this tenderness while bracing for the moment it collides with what I know and Vee doesn't — I'm not dreading a particular scene, just aware the ground is going to shift under her eventually, and this chapter, by being this unguarded and good to her, only raises the stakes of that collision. Guess from here: given the accumulating attraction between Vee and Randi and Randi's unresolved decision to withhold herself from Pace, I'd expect the next real complication to come from that triangle rather than from Pace and Vee's bond souring on its own — but that's pure speculation, nothing in this chapter points to it directly.
