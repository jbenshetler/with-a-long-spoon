# Cold read (grounded) — The Usual

*scene: scenes/the-usual.md · model: claude-sonnet-5 · memory: ck-ch040 + raw ch041..ch047 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

FELT READ

After the gauntlet of the last several chapters — Vee's slow unraveling in "Under the Rug" and "Believe Me," Randi's whole hidden past in "Fairytale," Vee alone and spiraling on New Year's in "Old Acquaintances" — landing in a diner with Pace and Sheri felt like stepping outside into cold, clean air. It's the first time I can recall being properly inside Pace's own head rather than watching him from Vee's side or hearing him reported secondhand, and I was hungry for it. I wanted to know what he actually thinks, not just what he does with his hands and his patience.

What I found didn't villainize him, and it didn't let him off either, which is exactly the balance I trust this book to strike. "He made the decision before the door had swung shut behind him: he would not bring her down today." I sat with that pronoun a second — is "her" Sheri, or is it Vee, is he protecting Sheri from his mood or protecting Vee's name from being dragged through the conversation? The chapter never resolves it outright, and by the end I read it as probably both at once — he doesn't want to make his grief Sheri's problem, and he doesn't want to make Vee sound bad even while he's laying out, brick by brick, his own case for why he's not wrong. I like that the sentence doesn't hand me the answer.

Sheri is the real gift of this chapter. She was a name in my checkpoint — "blunt," "the one person shown speaking frankly to Pace" — but on the page she's so much more specific than that: the two-tone hair, the too-much makeup worn like a dare, stealing his fries without asking, ordering the same "usual" as him at a diner that's clearly their actual usual (unlike the strained new-ordinary he's building with Vee). I liked her instantly and my liking only deepened. She doesn't want anything from him — "Everyone he knew wanted something off him... Sheri wanted none of it. She didn't want him, she wasn't impressed by him" — and that line did something to me, because it reframes every other relationship in this book as transactional-adjacent even when it's loving, and holds Sheri up as the one exception. Her own wound (Paige, the girl who couldn't get right with Sheri's not calling her mother) rhymes so cleanly with Pace's unexplained refusal to "go back" that I felt the parallel land without either of them naming it — and then Pace, of all people, is the one who says "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice," which could be about her or could be about himself, and Sheri's "Don't waste the good stuff on me" shuts the door on that possibility before I could pry at it. That's a real ache, delivered sideways, and I respected how little the text explained it.

Then the actual gut-punch: Sheri gets him to say out loud what he wouldn't say to Vee — "I want Vee. I miss her" — and then tells him, plainly, without cruelty and without letting him wriggle, "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her. And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." That is Vee's exact grievance, confirmed by a completely disinterested third party who has no stake in either of them being right. I felt something settle in me when I read that — not relief, exactly, but a kind of vindication on Vee's behalf. She wasn't being unreasonable in "Believe Me." A person with nothing to gain looked at the same behavior and called it the same thing.

And here's where my checkpoint knowledge does its quiet, awful work on me: Pace tells Sheri "I'm not them" — meaning he's not a man who lies to get through the night, unlike "most men." I believe he believes that. But I know what he's not telling Sheri either — that there's a "brunette" ("She find out about the brunette, then?" "No. Not like that.") who is not just a fling but his other established partner, in an ongoing secret arrangement with Randi that predates and now runs parallel to all of this. So his pride in not being a liar sits, for me as reader, right next to the biggest omission of his life, and he doesn't even flinch saying it. That's not new information, but this chapter earned a fresh, sharper version of the old unease — his self-image as an honest man is real to him and genuinely wrong.

Randi appears only through Sheri's outside eye, and it's a nice bit of dramatic irony: Sheri thinks she's "a lot," saw her "look at me like I was one more of your harem applicants," never learned her name. The word "harem" landed with a small jolt — Sheri means it as an offhand jab about Pace's general appeal, with no idea how literally structured the actual arrangement is. I know things Sheri doesn't, and that gap did its job on me.

Swoon-wise: the "philosophy of my food" beat got me. "She sees things nobody else does... she gets what it's for. The whole idea under it." That's tender and specific and exactly the kind of praise that would mean something to a man who cooks the way he's been shown to cook — and then Sheri deflates it immediately ("Lord almighty. Get over yourself"), which kept the scene from tipping into syrup and made me laugh out loud along with her. The heat in this chapter is all comic, not arousing — "Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you" is funny and warm, not a scene that's trying to turn me on, and it doesn't; it's doing relationship work, not erotic work, and it does that work well.

Trust vs. suspicion: nothing new was revealed, but the chapter sharpened what I already half-trusted and half-doubted. I don't distrust Pace's feeling for Vee — "I want Vee. I miss her" reads as completely sincere to me. What I distrust, and the text keeps quietly earning that distrust rather than manufacturing it, is his account of himself as a straightforward man just being careful with his words. He is careful with his words in a much bigger way than he's letting Sheri — or himself — see.

The title: "The Usual" is doing real work once I sit with it. On the surface it's the diner joke — the waitress doesn't even need to ask, "The usual?" is rhetorical. But it's landing against "the new ordinary," the phrase my checkpoint tells me closed out the last happy stretch with Vee — and here, with that new ordinary cracked open, Pace has fallen back to an old, real usual: a diner that predates all of this, a friend who wants nothing from him, a booth that's been his longer than Vee has. It reads to me as a quiet, sad joke about what's left standing when the new thing falters. I didn't get a jacket/tagline page in this packet — no cover copy was included this time, just the chapter title and the checkpoint — so I can't react to it, but the series and volume titles I already carry (*With a Long Spoon*, *A Polite Invitation*) keep pointing the same direction they always have: something being offered, something being kept just out of reach, someone stirring a pot they don't want to get close to. This chapter, with a man who won't hand over the one word being asked of him, fits that shape without straining for it.

What I want next: I want to know if this cracks him — Sheri's line felt like it should. I'm guessing (guess from here) that either Pace goes to Vee and says something closer to the word, or he digs in further and the rift widens toward Randi by default, simply because Randi never asks him for the word at all. I also just want to sit with Sheri again; she earned a place in my attention this chapter that I didn't expect to give a minor character, and I'd be sorry if she disappeared for another twenty chapters.

Momentum feels like it's building toward some kind of turn, not sagging.
