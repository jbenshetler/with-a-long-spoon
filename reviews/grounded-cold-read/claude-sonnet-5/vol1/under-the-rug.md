# Cold read (grounded) — Under the Rug

## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

I came into this chapter already braced, because the chapter right before it ("Seconds") left that unbearable asymmetry hanging — her cracker-and-meal confession, his "I love how good we are together," her deciding not to say anything else about it. So when this chapter opens with the word "right there, simple, wanting out" and she doesn't say it, I recognized exactly what was happening before she even spoke: this is the same withheld word, still unspoken, now pressurized. And then she blurts something else instead — "Is this what we are now?" — too loud, wrong register, and I felt the wince of it in my own chest. That's such a true piece of writing about displaced feeling: you can't say the real thing, so you say an adjacent, uglier thing, and it comes out at the wrong volume because it's carrying more freight than it was built for. I was fully with her here, not judging the sharpness — I understood exactly where it came from.

Pace, through most of this chapter, still reads as the same deliberate, generous, controlled man — "Okay. Where do you want to go tonight?" is such a perfect Pace non-reaction, and I actually ached a little watching her aggression have nothing to catch on, the way she wanted a fight and he just... solved the problem she handed him. But then the restaurant scene gave me something genuinely new, and it landed as new — not manufactured suspicion on my part but the text itself flagging it: his eyes sliding to the screen, the fork-squaring, and then "a flicker of plain irritation, there and gone" — the narrator is explicit that this is something Vee has never seen on his face "not in all these months, not once." That's a real crack, the first one I've actually watched happen on the page rather than one I know about only because the checkpoint told me about the game and Randi. I believed his explanation afterward — the sensory overload, "I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you" — it's consistent with everything I already know about him (the concentration he brings to a knot in wood or a step in a proof), and it's tender and true enough that it dissolved her fight and mine along with it. But the text itself refused to let that resolution be clean: "It didn't sit right even as she thought it, some small wrong note she'd have had to stop and turn over to hear properly, and she didn't stop." I want to honor that exactly as written — the book is telling me there's something here worth a second look, without telling me what it is. I'm not going to decide it's about Randi, or about anything specific; I'm just going to sit with the fact that both Vee and I let it go without turning it over, and that not-turning-it-over is doing work.

The nachos moment is where the chapter's food motif got its best new phrasing yet — "It was to food what a billboard is to a painting" — and I loved it as writing while feeling genuinely sad reading it, because it's not really about nachos. It's Vee discovering that Pace has ruined an entire register of her former life for her — a plate that used to be fine, has now gone dead in her mouth, and she can't get it back. That's such a specific, unglamorous way to show how thoroughly she's been remade by him, and it doesn't feel like the author's thumb on the scale; it feels like something she'd actually notice.

Then the ending sex scene is where the chapter really got me — heat and heartbreak fused so tightly I couldn't separate them. The reversal, where his control finally breaks and he takes her "like a man who'd run clean out of the strength to wait," worked on me exactly the way I want erotica to work: fully physical, fully consensual (she wanted this, wanted it "with a sharpness that startled her"), and yet freighted with everything unsaid between them. And then the gut-punch: "she came with his name in her mouth and not the other thing." That line devastated me a little. The orgasm becomes the place where the unsayable word almost surfaces and gets drowned again, "as it always did, as she let it" — that's such a precise, unflinching piece of interiority, exactly the kind of "want she will not look at squarely" I read for.

The aftermath is where the title clicked for me. "Under the Rug" — she'd told herself, crossing the room to him, that she'd forgiven him, and it felt like plenty in the moment. By the end of the chapter it very obviously isn't plenty; the fight didn't get resolved, it got absorbed into more intimacy, and the real question — what he is to her, what word covers it, whether she'll ever get the word she actually wants — is still sitting there, unnamed, "ground in her and would not quiet." Nothing got fixed. It just got covered by something warm and physical enough that she could tell herself it had. That's a good title: it doesn't spoil anything, but once I finished the chapter it named exactly what had happened, cleanly and a little cruelly.

I also caught the echo back to the Cassie dinner scene in "recent chapters" — Vee unable to find a word for Pace in Cassie's frame ("she had nothing that fit... not the right word for what he was"), and now at the end of this chapter, still hunting for a word she could say to her mother or lay out for her father. That's a real, earned recurrence, not a coincidence — the naming problem is becoming the load-bearing motif of this whole stretch of the book.

Trust-wise: I don't feel any *new* structural suspicion of Pace planted here beyond what the checkpoint already gave me (the game, Randi, the concealment). What's new is smaller and more human — a real, visible flicker of irritation and a narrator-flagged wrong note that neither Vee nor I chased down. I'm holding that open, not resolving it toward anything sinister; it's just the first time I've watched him be less than perfectly patient with her, and it unsettled me exactly as much as the text let it.

I want to keep reading to see whether that wrong note ever gets picked back up, and whether Vee's need for the actual word from him — not a sentence *near* it, the word itself — keeps deepening into something that has to break open, or whether she just keeps folding it back under the rug indefinitely. Guess from here, held loosely: given how explicitly the chapter names the pattern of drowning the word at climax and sweeping the fight under warmth, I'd expect this can't stay buried forever — but I have no idea what form that eventually takes, and I'm not going to pretend I do.
