# Cold read (grounded) — Old Acquaintances

## Reader reaction

FELT READ

I came into this chapter still bruised from "Believe Me" — that scene where Vee finally says out loud "Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me" is one of the hardest lines in the whole run so far, and it colored everything I read here. And then "Fairytale" landed right before this chapter, and it changed the register of the whole thing: I now know, and Vee doesn't, that the "you ANGEL 😇 ... miss your face most though, gorgeous 💋" text she's about to receive was typed by a woman who just had cold, faultless, feeling-nothing sex with a "devastatingly handsome" man specifically because he wasn't Pace and wasn't Vee — and who has just, for the first time on the page, said the actual word to herself about what Vee is to her ("she knew exactly what it was"). So I walked into Vee's dark, cold childhood bedroom already carrying a weight she doesn't have, and the chapter used that mercilessly and beautifully.

**Vee** — this is the chapter that made me feel closest to her yet, and it's the least "eventful" one I've read. Nothing happens. She lies in a bed her legs have outgrown, checks her phone, doesn't send things, builds a woman out of spite to torture herself with, and falls asleep still reaching. That's the whole chapter, and it worked on me completely. The line that got me was "The room kept all of it dusted and had no idea who was in the bed" — and then, at the very end, "the cold room kept watch over the girl who was gone." That's a genuinely devastating way to render someone who has outgrown her own childhood without anywhere yet to put the person she's become. I ached for her the way I'd ache for a friend on the phone at 1am. And the moment where her own good memory — wrapped in the sheet at the table, watching him cook the fra diavolo — gets hijacked mid-recall by the invented rival, the hair going gold and the leg going tan right there inside her own remembered night — that's one of the more quietly brutal things I've read in this book. It's not a villain doing that to her. It's her own mind doing it to her, and I recognized the mechanism completely: she's using the fantasy to protect herself from the real question (does he still want me) and it turns around and eats the one thing she actually has.

**Pace** — my feeling here didn't move so much as sharpen. His silence over break is not written as cruelty, and I don't think the text wants me to read it that way outright — but "He left you the room. He always left you the room — a thing she'd appreciated once, and hated now" lands as a real indictment of the exact thing that used to charm me about him (the consent-forward, unhurried, "May I" quality). Given no word across the whole break, that virtue reads, for the first time, like withholding with teeth. I don't distrust him exactly, but my sympathy for the "words are cheap, believe what I do" line from "Believe Me" has curdled a little under the silence — believe what he does is a fine policy right up until "what he does" is nothing at all.

**Randi** — this is where the chapter did its most painful work on me, entirely through dramatic irony, because Vee is envying a total fiction. "off somewhere in all that snow with a man she'd just met, a devastatingly handsome billionaire, a weekend so wild Vee wouldn't recognize half of what they did, and it sounded wonderful to her" — I know, because I was just inside Randi's head, that the actual truth is the opposite: Randi felt nothing, was cold "along every seam" except where his body touched her, and thought of Vee, specifically, by name, as the one thing in the room she couldn't counterfeit. Watching Vee build a rich, gorgeous rival for herself out of a night that was, in the room I'd just left, hollow and sad for Randi — that's the sharpest irony this book has handed me yet. It doesn't make me trust Randi more or less than I already did; it makes the whole friendship read as tragic in a very specific, structural way — two women lying awake on the same night, each one certain the other has the full life, neither one telling the other the truth.

**Swoon / pull** — very little swoon in this particular chapter (it's an ache chapter, not a heat chapter), but the pull to keep reading is enormous. I want someone — anyone — to say the true thing out loud. The gap between what Vee knows and what I know has never felt this wide, and it's excruciating in the good way, the way dramatic irony is supposed to hurt.

**Trust vs. suspicion** — I don't distrust Vee at all; her spiral is completely earned and completely human. I trust Randi's feelings are now fully real (not manufactured, not "the game" anymore, per "Fairytale") but I distrust her choices — she has decided, in that same recent chapter, to keep this from Vee on purpose ("Let Vee arrive at it on her own... as long as the word kept its cover"), and this chapter shows me the cost of that decision landing on someone who has no idea it's being made. That's the text earning my unease, not me manufacturing it.

**Erotic charge** — essentially none in this chapter, by design. The one charged beat — the remembered sheet slipping at the table — is brief and gets corrupted almost immediately by the jealousy fantasy, which is the point; this is a chapter about appetite denied and turned against itself, not appetite fed.

**Friction** — very little. The repeated checking-the-phone-and-setting-it-down rhythm risks feeling like padding, but it reads to me as accurate insomnia rather than authorial stalling — I've lived that loop. The one small thing that made me flinch (not at the craft, at the content) was Vee's self-description of the imagined rival's body as the opposite of "a stripper's" for her own — it's harsh, but it's exactly consistent with the mother's "floozie" vocabulary the checkpoint told me runs under Vee's arousal and shame; it's the book being cruel to her in a way that's already been established as hers, not new authorial cruelty.

**Titles** — "Old Acquaintances" does real double work for me: it's the most literal possible New Year's title (Auld Lang Syne, should old acquaintances be forgot), and it's also exactly what this chapter is about — the old acquaintance of her own outgrown room and childhood self, the friends on the corkboard who don't text anymore, and, underneath, the old acquaintance she and Randi are both performing being for each other over text while each hides the real thing. It didn't give anything away going in — it read as pure holiday scene-setting until the chapter closed and I felt the second meaning land. As for the book — *A Polite Invitation* and *With a Long Spoon* — I don't have a tagline in this packet to react to, but the long-spoon idiom (you need a long spoon to sup with the devil) keeps nagging at me every time someone in this book is shown being generous, patient, or "polite" while concealing something structural underneath — and this chapter, with its two women each performing warm generosity toward the other over text while withholding the truth, feels like it's aimed straight at that title.

**What I want next** — I want the two of them in a room together, off their phones, where one of the two truths has to come out. I have no idea if that's coming soon or if this ache is going to be allowed to keep stretching (guess from here, nothing more). I'm also newly worried about Pace's silence in a way I wasn't before — I want to know if it's grief, pride, distance, or something colder, and the chapter gave me no way to tell which.

**Characterization:** Vee deepens substantially — this is the most interior, least plotted chapter I've read for her, and it holds together completely: her class-consciousness, body-shame vocabulary, and anxious attachment all surface consistently under the stress of the unresolved fight and the silence. Pace doesn't appear on the page but is characterized entirely by absence — his established "leaves you the room" quality reads, for the first time, as costly rather than charming. Randi doesn't appear in person either, but the prior chapter's revelation recolors her completely for me going into this one — verified real feeling underneath continued, deliberate concealment.

**Pace — within the chapter:** slow by design and it earns the length; the repetitive phone-checking risks padding but reads to me as accurate insomnia rhythm rather than stalling.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** a deliberate deceleration after the confrontation in "Believe Me" and the revelation in "Fairytale" — not a drop in tension, though, because the irony payload (what Vee doesn't know she's envying) keeps it taut. Feels like a held breath, not a lull.
