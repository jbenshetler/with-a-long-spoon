# Cold read (grounded) — hills-and-valleys

*scene: scenes/hills-and-valleys.md · model: claude-sonnet-5 · memory: ck-ch020 + raw ch021..ch027 · reader-protocol: v3-grounded-checkpoint*

## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

This one's short and it does something none of the previous chapters did: it steps all the way outside the triangle for the first time and gives me a total stranger's-eye view of Pace — through a woman I've never met, on a bike, in the cold, talking about Vee and Randi like they're items on a list. That alone recalibrated me before I'd finished the first page.

**Sheri** lands oddly vivid for how little space she gets — the small frame "not much bigger than a child's," the bleached ponytail, the fact that she's beaten Pace on climbs for two years and never let him forget it. I liked her immediately, the way you like a blunt friend in any book: "Hell of a backside on her, though. I'll give her that" — said about Vee, a woman she's never met, purely from having watched her sit alone and sulk at a bar. That's a hard, almost unkind way to be introduced to a stranger, and it unsettled me a little on Vee's behalf even though Vee isn't in the room to hear it. Sheri isn't cruel exactly — she's the kind of friend who "remarked on women the way other people remarked on the weather" — but it's a cooler, more transactional register than anything we've had access to before, and it made me feel, for a second, what it might be like to be discussed by men (or people) who like you fine but are keeping score.

**Pace** is the real subject of my unease here. Watching him from outside — "He heard himself going and didn't stop" as he talks about Vee running her thumb down the chair joint — is the first time I've seen him lose a little control of himself in front of someone else, and it's oddly touching: proof that what's happening with Vee isn't just his usual careful architecture, it's actually gotten into him. But then Sheri says the sentence that does the real work of the chapter: "Two of them at once… If that's not already complicated, it will be." And he doesn't answer. That non-answer is the loudest thing in the chapter. He confirms, flatly, "Randi. That's not finished" — present tense, ongoing, no hesitation — while in the very same breath he's rhapsodizing about Vee's thumb on the wood grain. I already knew this from the checkpoint, of course; the reader has known since chapter 1. But hearing Pace say it out loud, casually, to a friend who isn't shocked, made the double life feel suddenly *administrative* rather than tragic — like he's just managing two open items, and the person managing them is the same man who spent three chapters building a dress by hand and crying with Vee over a scar he wouldn't name. That gap between the tenderness we've watched him render on the page and the flat "that's not finished" here is the most unsettling thing this book has shown me about him yet.

**Trust vs. suspicion** — this is the first chapter that gave me actual, textual cause for unease about Pace rather than structural/dramatic-irony unease inherited from the premise. Everything before this was "the reader knows what Vee doesn't," which is dread by design. This is different: it's Pace's own carelessness, in an unguarded moment with a friend, treating "two of them at once" as a logistics question Sheri gets to weigh in on like it's mildly funny. It doesn't erase what I feel about him from the fireside chapter — the Daphne story, the scar, "not tonight" — that was real vulnerability and real trust-building. But this chapter sits right next to it in my head now and won't quite dissolve.

**Erotic charge** — none, and I don't think it's meant to have any; it's a cold, physical, non-sexual scene (breath, cranks, cold fingers) that functions as a palate-cleanser/reveal rather than a heat scene, and it works fine at that register.

**Friction** — very minor, but I wanted more of Sheri than I got; she arrives fully formed and then the chapter just... ends, "and that was the whole of it," almost throwaway. It reads intentional (a glimpse, not a scene) but I noticed the appetite for more.

**Titles** — "hills-and-valleys" is plainly literal on the surface (the ride itself, climbs and descents) but it's clearly also doing the double-duty the other titles have done — the *up* of Vee (talked about with real warmth, unguarded) and the *down* of the flat, unbothered "that's not finished" about Randi; also maybe the larger shape of the whole arrangement, the highs of being adored and the valleys neither woman knows she's standing in. It didn't give anything away going in — lowercase, unassuming, almost hidden compared to the sharper single-word titles ("Famished," "Fed," "Gone") — which in hindsight fits a chapter that's deliberately smaller and more oblique than the ones around it. The series title *With a Long Spoon* keeps nagging at me more than ever after this chapter — you sup with the devil with a long spoon, keep your distance from what you're feeding on — and this chapter, with Pace's casual "that's not finished," is the first time I felt the *spoon* metaphor land on him specifically rather than just on the situation.

**What I want next** — I want to know if Vee's planned reconciliation ("tomorrow night... sure, not sorry") plays out, and whether anything from this cycling-ride scene (Sheri's knowledge, the flat admission) ever crosses back into Vee's world. Guess from here: probably not directly — Sheri feels like a closed loop, a device to let the reader hear Pace say the quiet part out loud — but I'll be watching for whether Pace's ease here curdles into something that costs him, or whether the book keeps letting him have both without consequence for a while longer.

**Cast present (in person):** Pace, Sheri. (Mentioned only: Vee, Randi.)

**Heat:** 0 — no sexual content; a cold-weather bike ride and dialogue.

**Romance:** 1 — faint warmth, but directed oddly: Pace's unguarded enthusiasm about Vee reads tender, but it's undercut immediately by the flat acknowledgment of Randi, so the chapter doesn't build romantic feeling so much as complicate it.

**Motifs & images:** the chair joint / wood grain (first sighting for Sheri, but continues the furniture-as-Pace's-language motif from the checkpoint — the bench, the dress pedestal, now a specific dowelled arm); the math book Vee "didn't put back like it had burned her" (small first-sighting image, resonant with her hunger/appetite motif); cold and breath as a structuring device (climbs take "the breath for themselves," mirroring earlier fire/cold-glass imagery from "Rock").

**Symbolism:** the climbs vs. descents structure of the ride doubles the chapter's content (effortful truth-telling on the flats, silence on the hard parts) — feels like the title's literal engine, not over-read.

**Characterization:** Pace deepens in an uncomfortable direction — first time his warmth and his double-dealing are shown side by side without any narrative cushioning. Sheri is a strong, economical first appearance — distinct voice, doesn't feel like a plot device even though her function here is largely expository (confirming the "two at once" fact to the reader through Pace's own mouth).

**Pace — within chapter:** brisk, almost brusque; very short chapter, all dialogue-and-motion, no lingering. It earns its length by being exactly as long as the reveal needs and no longer.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** a real gear-change after a long run of Vee-POV chapters (the dress, the shoe shopping, the fireside confession, the dance-and-fight). Coming right after Vee's resolve to "fix it" with Pace, cutting to Pace breezily riding with Sheri and confirming Randi's ongoing status feels like a deliberate cold shower — it holds the dramatic irony taut rather than releasing it, and resets tension right before (presumably) the reconciliation scene.
