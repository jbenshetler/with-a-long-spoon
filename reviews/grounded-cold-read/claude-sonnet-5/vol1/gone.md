## Reader reaction

FELT READ

This one knocked the wind out of the sweetness a little, on purpose I think, and I'm grateful for it — the last three chapters (the dress, the stats lecture, the shoe-shopping) were so much pure glow that I'd almost stopped feeling the thing I'm supposed to keep feeling underneath it. "Gone" put me right back inside the dramatic irony, and did it by finally giving me the scene I didn't know I'd been waiting for: Pace and Randi, alone, after, talking about Vee to each other.

The afterglow itself is lovely and completely disarms me for the first page or so. "Whatever she ran on out in the world, she set it down at his door" — that's such a tender, exact line, and it made me soften toward both of them at once: toward Pace for being the place she doesn't have to perform, and toward Randi for actually, finally, being unguarded with someone on the page, in real time, not just in the private collapses the checkpoint told me about. When she says "I keep waiting to get bored of her — you always get bored — and it doesn't come… She got past me before I could help it," that landed as genuinely moving. It's the closest thing to a confession Randi's given us. I believed it completely, and it made me like her more than I have at any point so far — not despite the manipulation I know about, but almost in spite of my own memory of it.

Then two things happened that pulled the rug a little, and I think the chapter earned both.

First: "She's not ready, though. She'll get there. You'll want to take your time." / "I want her to get there on her own." That's the line that put the chill back in. It's said so gently, so lovingly even, that it took me a second to register what it actually is — two people discussing a third person's arc, her *readiness*, as if she's a project with a timetable, while she's off across town thinking she's just fallen for a wonderful, generous man and made a marvelous new best friend. I don't know exactly what "there" means — the text doesn't tell me, and I'm not going to guess at it — but the fact that they're managing her toward *anywhere* without her knowledge is the coldest true note in an otherwise warm scene, and it's exactly the kind of quiet, un-shouted detail that earns suspicion rather than manufactures it.

Second: Randi's stillness. Pace, unprompted, genuinely moved, describes Vee lighting up a room without knowing it — "she's the most alive person standing there, and it never once occurs to her" — and Randi goes still in a way the prose flags explicitly as different from her usual after-stillness: "the kind that takes holding." I don't know what that stillness is — jealousy, a flicker of her own guardedness recognizing itself in Vee's obliviousness, the first cold brush of realizing Pace might be falling further than the game accounted for, some blend of all three — and I like that the chapter doesn't tell me either. It just lets me sit in it with her for a beat before she covers with "Yeah. She is," and reaches for sex instead. That felt true to everything I know about her: when something gets too exposed, she reaches for a body instead of a word.

Erotic charge: it works on me steadily rather than in one big peak. The hands-in-hair, the "loose the way she only went afterward," the low Sade under the door — all of that is warm, sensory, unhurried, and it primes me well before anything explicit happens. The one moment that actually got under my skin was Randi drawing her body down the length of his to *illustrate* what's under Vee's cardigan — using her own body as a stand-in for Vee's to arouse Pace. That's erotic and faintly vertiginous at once: Vee's body, present only by proxy, being handled between two people who are keeping her in the dark. I felt the heat of it and the strangeness of it in the same breath, and I don't think that's an accident. The coda — "She rode him slow in the low lamplight" — is tender and unhurried rather than graphic; it reads more like a settling-back-into-each-other than a scene built to peak on its own, which is right, since the real charge of the chapter was in the talk, not the act.

I trust Pace more after this chapter, oddly — his line "He didn't take up the rest of what she'd set down; that was hers to hold" is exactly the door-respecting instinct the checkpoint told me to watch for, applied here to Randi rather than Vee, and it reads as consistent rather than convenient. My unease about him is structural, not personal: he is plainly capable of genuine, unprompted tenderness for Vee ("it never once occurs to her") that reads as more than a plan executing itself — which almost makes the "she's not ready… you'll want to take your time" exchange worse, because it means the plan and the real feeling are now running in the same channel, and I can't tell where one stops and the other starts. I don't think the text wants me to be able to tell yet, so I'm leaving that ajar rather than deciding it for myself.

My feeling about Randi has moved the most this chapter — toward more sympathy, not less. Her vow at the end of chapter one ("never let herself be that exposed again") is visibly not holding here; she is, in fact, exposed, just to him and about someone else. That's a good, painful contradiction, and it's the kind of thing that makes me trust the book to keep looking at her honestly rather than flattening her into either villain or ingenue.

The title: "Gone" does at least two things for me. On its face it's Randi's own line about Vee — "She's got it bad… I love it" — Vee is gone over the both of them. But the chapter is really about Randi being the one who's gone, past the point she meant to let herself go, over a person she was supposed to be using as a piece in a game. And there's a colder, more literal edge sitting under it too, given the "she's not ready… she'll get there" exchange — gone as in, whatever "there" is, something is being decided about Vee's trajectory without her in the room, and it made me wonder — a genuine guess, nothing more — whether the title is quietly pointing forward at Vee herself being carried somewhere she doesn't know she's headed. I don't know which reading the book wants me to land on, and I like that it hasn't told me.

Against the book's own titles — A Polite Invitation, With a Long Spoon, and whatever tagline sits on the jacket — this chapter feels like the one that most literally shows me the "invitation" being planned between the two people extending it, in bed, in the dark, fondly, while the invitee is the only person not in the room. It's the clearest single scene yet of what it must mean to eat with a long spoon: warmth this real, offered by hands this careful, still measured at a distance from the person receiving it.

What I want next: I want to know what "there" is. I want to see whether Pace's unprompted tenderness for Vee starts to outrun whatever the plan originally was, and whether that troubles Randi more than this chapter let her show. And I'm bracing — not dreading exactly, since the book has earned my trust that it will handle this honestly rather than punitively — for the moment Vee is in a room with both of them at once and some fraction of what I just read leaks through the face Randi and Pace are keeping up. Pure guess from here, nothing the text has promised.
