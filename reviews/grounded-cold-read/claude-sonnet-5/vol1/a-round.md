## Reader reaction

FELT READ

I came into this stretch still carrying the shadow from the checkpoint — the pointing game, the engineered "collision," the fact that Pace's whole courtship of Vee began as something he and Randi cooked up together — and for four chapters running, Randi doesn't appear on the page at all. That absence changed the temperature of my reading. Without her there to remind me of the plan, I could feel myself sliding, chapter by chapter, into just wanting these two to have each other, and then catching myself doing it.

Pace keeps earning trust in specific, small ways that matter more to me than any grand gesture would. The boot fix on the trail — kneeling, the moleskin, the relacing, "Should've done that at the lot. I'm sorry" — is such an unshowy piece of competence and care that it disarmed me completely. And then the thing that actually got me: he steps back out into the freezing rain so she can change, not because it's dramatic, but because it's the obvious kind thing to do, and he does it without narrating his own goodness. When she calls him back in and undresses in front of him, he doesn't move, doesn't grab — "he sat and looked at all of her and didn't move, and the gladness in him was the whole of the answer, and it was right." That "and it was right" is doing a lot of work on me. I believed it.

But the fitting scene is where this chapter really lives, and it's where my feelings about Pace got most complicated in the best way. He is, by the checkpoint's own account, a man who builds BDSM furniture and orchestrates elaborate exposures of women's "real" selves for his own — and his girlfriend's — purposes. Reading "A Round" with that in my pocket, I couldn't not notice that what he does to Vee here — the sustained, controlled, permission-bounded exposure, the "keep still," the deliberate withdrawal after each touch, the riser she's put up on like a display — is structurally the same shape as what the checkpoint told me he does with Randi on the bench. He never crosses a line Vee doesn't open herself, and every one of his choices (stopping the kiss himself, choosing the hem over the "easy yes" she's handing him) reads as genuine restraint, not performance. And yet I noticed the parallel and couldn't unnotice it. That's the first real, textually-earned unease I've felt about him since the checkpoint's warier read of "the plan" — not that he's lying to her, but that this, apparently, really is just how he loves: through total, patient control of exposure. Whether that's frightening or just who he is, the chapter doesn't tell me, and I don't want it to tell me yet.

Vee is where the chapter hit me hardest, though. The whole extended dread of the wet spot on the satin — the bargaining ("Maybe kindness could be only kindness. Maybe."), the counting of maybes she doesn't believe, the shut eyes because she can't stand to see disgust or its opposite, the polite pretense that would tell her he'd noticed everything — that's the most interior, unguarded writing about shame I've gotten from her yet, and it landed exactly where the book has been aiming since the pool chapter and her mother's voice. And then the reversal: he's not looking at her ruined satin, he's craned his neck all the way up waiting for her eyes, smiling like a man handed a gift. "She had spent the entire afternoon being seen at her most ashamed and had not, once, been found wanting." That sentence undid me a little. It's the whole book's appetite thesis delivered in one image — the thing she was sure would get her mocked instead gets her chosen, again.

The swoon points, plainly: the shirt reveal (he made it — no tag, because there was never a brand, because there's only him), which recontextualizes something she'd been wearing into the ground as an act of quiet devotion she didn't know she'd already received; and the dress itself — not the color she asked for, the color that's actually right for her, because "he hadn't matched the dress she'd described. He'd matched her." That's a genuinely moving piece of courtship writing, and I felt the swoon fully, eyes open to it.

Where it went dark for me, briefly, was the interlude with the mug of tea and the growing stain — that's where the erotic charge and the dread braided together most uncomfortably and effectively; not slack at all, just uncomfortable in the way the chapter wants. The scene never really goes clinical. If anything it risks the opposite problem: the density of Pace's perfections (patent income, handmade furniture, handmade shirts, courtly restraint, the six-year-old delight over MRI signal processing) stacks up fast enough that I felt my own eyebrow twitch — is he allowed to be this flawless? — except the checkpoint's dramatic irony is exactly what keeps that from curdling into fairy tale for me; I know there's a frame around this man that Vee doesn't have.

Cassie's scene ("Rye") was a small warm rest stop I was grateful for — dry, funny, safe, no ulterior motive, "Vee... Borrow real boots next time. / I'm keeping the shirt. / Keep the shirt." I trust Cassie completely and I like that the book keeps giving me somewhere to stand that isn't shadowed.

On titles: I wasn't handed a fresh tagline in this packet, only the chapter title and my memory of the jacket phrasing from the checkpoint (the appetite that "can only give her the best of herself by taking it," the game between the two people closest to her). Against that memory, "A Round" reads, after finishing the chapter, as almost purely literal and rather satisfying in retrospect — the tailor's circuit of pins and measurements literally going around her body, his knee-walk around the base of her — but it also carries a faint undertone of a boxing round, a contest of who breaks first, which fits the push-and-pull of her testing him and him nearly falling. It didn't give anything away walking in; it clicked into place afterward. A Polite Invitation feels dead-on for this chapter specifically — the "least information a human being has ever sent me" text, the "May I," the "If you'll let me," the whole courtship conducted as a series of scrupulously offered, never-forced invitations. With a Long Spoon I can only read through the checkpoint's lens right now — nothing in this chapter itself gestures at danger, it's all tenderness — but I know what the spoon is for, even if Vee doesn't, and that's exactly the discomfort the series title wants me sitting in.

I want to keep reading with a mix of hope and dread I didn't expect to feel this strongly. I want the dress finished, the induction dinner, Vee in silk that was mixed for exactly her. I dread Randi's return to the page — not because I distrust the dress, but because the moment Randi is back in the room, I'll be forced to hold "this is real" and "this began as a game" in the same hand again, and this chapter let me set that down for a while. Guess from here, clearly marked as a guess: the induction dinner is where the two threads — Vee's real, unshadowed happiness and the reader's knowledge of the plan — are going to collide.
