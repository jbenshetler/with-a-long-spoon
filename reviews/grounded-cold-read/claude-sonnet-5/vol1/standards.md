## Reader reaction

FELT READ

I came into this chapter still inside The Bench — that scene worked on me completely, the long unhurried build, the "there you are, Randi," the crying she didn't seem to know she was doing. So Standards landed as a gut-punch of a different kind: no heat at all, Pace not even in the room, and somehow more devastating than the scene before it, because it shows me exactly what she does with what he gave her.

Randi is the whole chapter, and my feeling for her has deepened hard. In The Bench I already ached for her — the apple sliced thin, the calculation behind every bite, the "portioning herself" Pace notices with such tenderness. But watching her walk into her own house and perform normalcy for Brooke, Sloane, and Kinsey made that portioning visible from the outside, and it's worse than I expected. The line that got me most: "Randi did not close doors quietly. She closed them with the small unhurried click of a woman who knew the room was hers." That's establishing, in one sentence, how completely she has lost her footing this morning — she is not herself in her own house. And then the ending undid me: "It did not feel like a decision... It felt like there being no door." That is exactly the kind of interior collapse I read for — not melodrama, just the quiet mechanics of a person choosing numbness so smoothly she can't even call it a choice anymore.

Brooke is new to me this chapter (nothing about her in The Bench) and she is beautifully, quietly awful — or maybe not awful at all, just doing what a sorority president does, which might be worse. "Brooke had a voice the way other people had a perfume. It preceded her into rooms and stayed in them after she left." The whole interrogation — engineering vs. math, "he doesn't teach, does he," the relief when she confirms he's not TAing her section, the truck, "he's a short-cute" — is class and status policing wearing the mask of warm curiosity, and I felt the specific, low-grade violence of it because the text never once tells me to feel it. Brooke never raises her voice, never says anything unkind, and it's brutal anyway. I don't distrust Brooke exactly — I have no evidence she's being deliberately cruel — but I clocked the machinery of the scene the way Randi clocks it: "she knew exactly how much authority it took to stay seated while another person stopped walking." That's a very precise, very cold piece of social observation and I trusted it completely.

Pace isn't on the page, but he's all over the chapter by absence — the warm house versus this cold one ("The room was cold. The house was lovely and old and did not hold its heat"), the door she locked first thing at his place versus the door here that's always unlocked and that she wants shut fast. That contrast did real work on me: two different definitions of safety, and she's retreating from the one that actually held her.

Swoon/pull: none, and rightly — this is the cooldown chapter, and it earns its total lack of warmth by contrast with what came before. If anything the "pull" here is dread, not swoon: I want her to go back to him and I can feel, with real unease, that she isn't going to, or not as herself.

Trust/suspicion: none of Pace — nothing in this chapter touches him, and what I remember of him (the water bottle, the door he wouldn't open, Daphne) still reads as trustworthy, maybe more trustworthy in retrospect now that I see what he's up against. My only "suspicion," if I can call it that, is toward the sorority house itself as an environment — not any one girl maliciously, just the atmosphere, the sense that Randi has to perform there constantly and that performance has a cost the others aren't paying. That reading feels earned by the page, not manufactured — the text gives me Randi's felt lag ("The fraction-of-a-second was wider here") several times, timing her own delay in answering, and I believed every one of those beats.

Erotic charge: zero, deliberately. It doesn't go slack because it was never trying to be erotic — it's the exhale after the held breath of The Bench, and I didn't miss the heat because the chapter is doing something else entirely and doing it well.

Friction: very little. The one thing I noticed as a device rather than just prose is the repeated "fraction of a second" measuring — used three or four times in slightly different phrasing to track Randi's social lag. It works, but by the third instance I felt the mechanism a little, the way you notice a metronome once you've heard it enough times. Not a complaint, just a texture I registered.

Titles: "Standards" is doing real double work and I like it — Randi sits on the sorority's Standards board (an actual disciplinary seat, we're told plainly: "she sat on the board herself, Standards"), so the word is literal on the page. But it's also, obviously, about the standard she's failing to meet for herself right now — the composure, the performance, the "standard" version of Randi that Brooke is quietly testing and that Randi barely passes. It didn't give anything away going in; it recolored nicely once I'd read the chapter, especially against Brooke's easy authority to enforce standards of her own without even standing up. I have no tagline in this packet to react to, and no book/series title info was supplied here either — I'm reading blind on that front this time, so I'll just say the two titles I do have (A Polite Invitation, With a Long Spoon) aren't active for me yet from what's actually in front of me; if anything "polite invitation" fits Brooke's interrogation disturbingly well — every question phrased as an invitation to explain, none of them optional.

What I want next: I want to know whether Randi tells Pace anything, or whether she simply goes quiet on him the way she went quiet on herself in this chapter — and I'm dreading the second option more than hoping for the first, because the chapter has made her capacity for smooth self-erasure so convincing. Pure guess from here: I expect a scene where Pace notices the withdrawal before she can explain it, because everything about him so far (the water bottle, the "I've learned not to interpret things for her") suggests he reads her better than she wants to be read, and that's going to collide with exactly the retreat she's just decided on.
