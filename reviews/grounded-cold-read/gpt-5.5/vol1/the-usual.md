## Reader reaction

I felt the relief of this chapter before I understood it: the quiet diner, the griddle, the jukebox dark, the sense of Pace coming somewhere he can actually breathe. After the last run of chapters with Vee cold and sleepless and Randi cold inside the fairytale, this one has warmth, but it is not the lush private warmth of Pace’s house. It is plainer, greasier, more honest. A burger, a shake, Sheri giving him hell. I liked being there.

Pace is both more lovable and more frustrating here. I believe him when he thinks, “When I’ve got her, there’s nothing else in the room.” I believe that the air changes for him when Vee enters. His wanting is not casual, and this chapter makes that even clearer because he is not performing tenderness for Vee; he is away from her, talking to someone who will not let him get away with sounding noble. But he is also doing the exact thing Vee accused him of: substituting action for the word, building a wall out of true things. “Every weekend, I’m there” is true. “I told her she was the most important thing in my life” is true. And still, Sheri cuts right through it: “She didn’t ask you for reasons.” That landed hard on me because it is exactly the missing piece. He keeps answering the adjacent question.

Sheri, right now, I trust almost completely. She is crude, funny, bruised, and clear-eyed. I liked her already as the blunt cycling friend, but here she gets texture fast: the makeup “worn like she’d dare you to mention it,” the roots grown out, the design-school Paige with bird prints, the family she cannot or will not go back to. She feels like someone Pace cannot manage by being patient at. She does not want his rescuing, his sex, his glamour, or his food-philosophy self-importance. She wants the shake that belongs to her, his fries, and the truth. That makes her a kind of relief on the page.

Vee is not present, but she is everywhere. The most aching line for me was Pace thinking “that Vee would either love this place or never see it at all, and he wanted very badly to know which.” That is the whole chapter’s tenderness and fear in one sentence. He wants to bring her into a different public place, one that would actually fit him, where he could hear her whisper. It retroactively softens the Chili’s disaster without erasing the hurt of it. He was not hiding her because she was only sex. But he also has not given her the one thing she has begged for, and the longer he waits, the more his silence becomes an answer of its own.

Randi is only mentioned as “the brunette,” but even that little turn made me tense. Sheri knows enough to say “She find out about the brunette, then?” so the secret is sitting right there at the table with the ketchup and fries. Pace saying “No. Not like that” is accurate and awful. Vee has not found out, but the hurt is already happening anyway. Also, Sheri having clocked Randi as looking at her like “one more of your harem applicants” made me laugh, and it sharpened Randi’s possessive edge from outside her own beautiful self-story.

The erotic charge here is not explicit, but it is alive in absence. Sheri’s dirty jokes about Vee’s body are funny because they are too much and because Pace blushes instead of playing cool. “Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you” is vulgar, but it also makes Vee’s physical power over him visible in a way he cannot quite say cleanly. The peak heat for me is actually his line: “The air’s different when she’s in the room. It — crackles.” That is desire, but not just desire. Then Sheri punctures it, and the scene lets both things be true.

The chapter title, “The Usual,” works on several levels for me. It is literally the diner order, the ritual meal, the place where Pace and Sheri know how to be themselves. But it also points at the usual evasion: Pace doing what he always does, choosing care, presence, patience, and not the word. Sheri’s grief has its “what always happens,” too. People want her to call home; Vee wants Pace to declare himself; everyone keeps being asked to fit into somebody else’s usual shape.

The book title, *A Polite Invitation*, still feels like it promises a doorway into desire that looks gracious from the outside and becomes much more dangerous once accepted. The series title, *With a Long Spoon*, feels sharper now: closeness with something you know may burn or bite, intimacy at a careful distance. I do not have a cover tagline in the material here, so I can’t react to one exactly.

What I want next is painful and simple: I want Pace to go to Vee and say the thing, but I do not trust that he can. My guess from here is that his “I can’t” has history under it, maybe tied to whatever he has kept locked away with his parents or Daphne or the scar. But from Vee’s side, none of that changes the fact that she is alone with his silence. I dread the secret about Randi landing while Vee is already this raw. I also want more Sheri, because she tells the truth in a room where everyone else keeps making beautiful shapes around it.
