# Cold read (grounded) — hills-and-valleys

## Reader reaction

FELT READ

This one caught me off guard by being small. After the whole white-knuckle arc of the dance — Vee spoiling her own night, torching a good man over "the girls," lying awake in his washed-out shirt, then marching to Randi's to be dressed for the apology — I braced for the confrontation, or for Vee's Saturday-night door. Instead the chapter steps clean out of her fever and into a cold bright morning on a bike, next to Pace, and it's a *breather*. And what the breather does is quietly devastating in a way the big scenes weren't: it lets me watch Pace be gone over her without her in the room to perform for.

Because that's the whole thing here. Sheri says "After you left Thursday, I kept an eye on her," and I brace again — is this the reckoning, is he going to be told how badly Vee behaved? And he *is* told, flatly ("all she did was sit by herself, glaring at the floor... left alone"), and he just keeps his eyes on the road. And then he starts talking about her, and can't stop — "the joint he'd spent a weekend on," her thumb down the grain of the walnut chair, the math book she pulled off the shelf and "not put it back like it had burned her." He "heard himself going and didn't stop." For a man the whole book has drawn as exact, withholding, always leaving the choosing to someone else — running his mouth about a girl to a friend on a hill is the most undefended I've seen him. It moved me. It rhymes with "Gone," where Randi lay on his chest saying *she got past me before I could help it* — now it's his turn, and neither of them saw it coming. The reciprocity is the swoon: Vee catalogues him (the lift-face, the scar, "Peter"), and here he's cataloguing *her*, the chair joint, the burned-hand book, and he doesn't even know he's doing it. "Listen to you," Sheri says. Exactly. Listen to him.

But the same short chapter is where the cold gets in, and it earned it on the page, not by my being on guard. Two lines. "What happened to the pale sorority chick?" — "Randi. That's not finished." *Not finished.* Like a job with an end date. And then Sheri, running the arithmetic anybody could run: "Two of them at once. If that's not already complicated, it will be." He doesn't answer. That's the chill under all the goneness — I've now watched him fall, genuinely, for Vee, in the same forty lines that name her, out loud, as one of two, a thing that isn't finished. The tenderness didn't cancel the machine; it's riding right on top of it. That's the book's whole trick and this chapter does it in miniature and cold.

Sheri herself I liked immediately, and she's built fast and clean — the small frame "not much bigger than a child's," the bleached ponytail swinging as she drops him on every climb and never lets him forget it, the two years of remarking on women "the way other people remarked on the weather." "Hell of a backside on her, though. I'll give her that" — she clocks Vee the way the bar women clocked Pace, and it's the display motif turned a new direction, a woman appraising Vee frankly and without heat. What she is, structurally, is the first outsider we've met who knows the *whole board* — not Cassie half-sensing something wrong, not the sorority thinking "Pace" is Randi's boyfriend, but someone who knows there's a Vee *and* a Randi and says so plainly. "You want me to not like her?" — she'd turn on Vee for him in a breath and keep it burning for years, and he tells her not to: "She couldn't take your heat." That line I turned over. It's fond, but it's also him managing Sheri away from Vee, keeping his people in their lanes.

TRUST / SUSPICION: The chapter genuinely earned unease — not from a shadow I imported but from two flat sentences: "That's not finished" and "Two of them at once... it will be complicated." Sheri's neutral arithmetic is more unsettling than any villainy would be, precisely because she's not judging him, just naming the geometry. Against that: Pace's goneness reads real to me, as real as Randi's did in "Gone." I hold both, the way I've held both all book.

EROTIC CHARGE: essentially none, and rightly. It's cold fingers, breath going back over shoulders, a hill. The one bodily note is Sheri's offhand appreciation of Vee, and it's weather, not heat. This is a cooldown chapter and doesn't pretend otherwise.

FRICTION: Almost none, but I'll be honest that it's *slight* — it's an interstitial, a repositioning beat, and if it had come between two quieter chapters I might have felt it as a lull. Coming right after "broken-in" it works as a deliberate exhale and a stakes-reset from the outside. The only thing I wanted a hair more of was a beat of Pace *feeling* the two-women problem land — he "didn't answer," which is in character, but I could've used one flicker of what not-answering cost him.

TITLES: "hills-and-valleys" reads mostly literal — the rolling road, the climbs that "took the breath for themselves," Sheri cresting ahead. But it sits as a soft emotional map too: the ups and downs coming, the terrain of two women at once, the parts of a thing that leave you silent. It doesn't give anything away; it colors gently. The book's title *A Polite Invitation* keeps pointing at the courtly seduction, the "May I," the invitation extended to Vee — and this chapter reminds me the inviter is running two invitations. The series title *With a Long Spoon* is the one that hums under "That's not finished" — the old proverb about supping with the devil and needing distance; two-of-them-at-once "will be complicated" is a long-spoon line if I ever heard one. (I wasn't given a cover tagline in this packet, so I've got none to lean on — only the two titles.)

WHAT I WANT / DREAD NEXT: I want Vee's Saturday-night door — the apology she was dressed and drilled for. I dread it a little, because I now know he's not sulking, he's gone over her, so she'll walk in braced for cold and find warmth, and the reconciliation will land as sweet exactly when I most know the ground under it is two-women-deep and "not finished." *Guess from here:* the apology goes well, tenderly, and the tenderness is the trap tightening, not springing. And Sheri's "it will be complicated" is a bell that's going to get rung.
