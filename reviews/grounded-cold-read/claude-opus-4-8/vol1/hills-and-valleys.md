## Reader reaction

FELT READ

After the long, wrenching night of "broken-in" and the whole giddy engine of the shoe-shopping and the sorority-room fitting, this chapter lands like a cold-air interlude — short, plain, a man on a bike with a friend, and I mean that as praise. It breathes. It's the first time I've been let into Pace's head *outside* of Randi's bed, and what I find there is not a mastermind gloating over a plan. It's a man who can't shut up about a girl and doesn't know he's doing it.

That's the whole chapter's small, precise blow. "He heard himself going and didn't stop." He tells Sheri about the walnut chair and the thumb Vee ran down the joint he spent a weekend on, about her pulling a math book off the shelf and not putting it back "like it had burned her" — and it's the joint he was proud of, and the math that got his desk moved into the hall, so of course *that's* the detail that undid him: she reached for the two things nobody's ever reached for. "Listen to you," Sheri says, and "He didn't know what there was to listen to." That's the tell. The man who asks before he takes, who leaves every choice to the woman — he can't see his own face. It moved my feeling about him a notch back toward tenderness, right when the recent chapters had me most uneasy about what he and Randi are running.

Sheri is a gift. A brand-new voice, and immediately the cleanest sightline in the book after Cassie — cleaner, maybe, because she's *inside* Pace's world and owes the game nothing. Two years across a handlebar, faster than him on the climbs and never lets him forget it, remarks on women "the way other people remarked on the weather" ("Hell of a backside on her, though. I'll give her that."). She reads him in one sideways look. And she's the first person who's said the thing out loud to his face: "What happened to the pale sorority chick?" — "Randi. That's not finished." — "Two of them at once... If that's not already complicated, it will be." An outsider running "the arithmetic anybody could run," and Pace doesn't answer, because there's no answer that survives being said. After all the interior justification (Randi's "she's not ready," Pace's "I want her to get there on her own"), it's bracing to hear a plain third party call it what it plainly is: complicated, and about to get worse.

The swoon here is quiet but real — it's the swoon of watching someone be caught out in love without his consent. "She couldn't take your heat," he says of Vee versus Sheri, which is both a deflection and, I think, true, and Sheri laughs and lets him have it. I liked him in this chapter more than I've liked him since the fitting, precisely because he's *undefended* and doesn't know it. It cuts against the dread I've been carrying — or rather it sharpens the dread, because the more real his feeling reads, the worse the hidden foundation gets. The jacket keeps promising both, and this chapter is a clean shot of the "how real it got" side.

Trust vs. suspicion: no new suspicion of anyone here — if anything the chapter *lowers* my guard on Pace's feeling and raises the stakes on the structure. The one thing I sat with: "That's not finished," about Randi, said flatly, and then "Two of them at once." Pace holding both women, one who knows and one who doesn't, and an outside voice underlining that the collision is coming. Nothing here is a manufactured shadow; the shadow's just the shape of what I already know, spoken by someone who doesn't know the half of it.

Erotic charge: near zero, and correctly so — this is the morning after, cold fingers, breath going back over shoulders. The only carnal note is Sheri's, and it's deliberately offhand ("Hell of a backside on her"), the same appraising eye the whole book runs on turned into a throwaway between friends. It doesn't try to be hot and shouldn't. It's a palate-cleanser and a wide shot.

Friction: almost none. It's a lean chapter and it earns its brevity. The one line I turned over — "She could turn it on a person inside a breath and keep it burning for years; he'd seen her do it, and seen what it was worth on the other side, which was everything." That's a lot of backstory-weight to hang on a character we just met in a single paragraph; it reads like the book telling me Sheri matters more than this chapter alone shows. Not a thumb on the scale exactly — more a promise I'm being asked to bank. I'll take it, but I noticed the hand.

Titles: "hills-and-valleys" sits mostly literal on first read — the rolling road, talk on the flats, silence on the climbs. But it recolors nicely against the stats lecture two chapters back, Simpson's paradox: "A thing can run one way across every short stretch you measure and the opposite way across the whole of it." The ride is short stretches — flats, descents, one climb — and the chapter is *about* Pace not being able to see the whole of himself from inside the stretch he's on. And it's the up-and-down of the thing generally: last night the valley of the Rusty Nail, this morning the man cresting a hill talking helplessly about her. As for the book — *A Polite Invitation*, *With a Long Spoon* ("he who sups with the devil should have a long spoon"), and the tagline "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — Sheri's "Two of them at once... it will be" is the long-spoon warning spoken plainly for once: the person supping thinks the spoon is long enough, and the outsider can see it isn't. That's exactly where the chapter points me — at the complication arriving, at nobody's spoon being as long as they think.

What I want next: I want the two of them in a room — Vee at Pace's door "tomorrow night," sure instead of sorry, per Randi's marching orders. I dread it a little, because the last time Vee walked in with a plan she'd been handed by Randi (the shoes, the scarf, the whole engineered afternoon), and here's Sheri telling me from the outside that the arithmetic is bad. Guess from here (marked as a guess): Sheri's the one person orbiting Pace who could, eventually, say the true thing to Vee — or be the crack the truth comes through. Two years, no stake in the game, a plain mouth. I'd watch her.
