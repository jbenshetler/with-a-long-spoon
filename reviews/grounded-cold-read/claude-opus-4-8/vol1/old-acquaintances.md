# Cold read (grounded) — Old Acquaintances

## Reader reaction

FELT READ

This one landed on me as a slow, cold ache, and I want to say up front that it worked — quietly, without a single sexual gesture, it may be the chapter that has moved me most in a while, precisely because it's the loneliest.

The trick of it — which I only fully clocked at the last exchange — is that I've just come out of Randi's chapter, watching her lie awake in a faultless hotel room next to a faultless man, cold everywhere she wasn't touching him, envying nothing and no one except Vee across a nothing-Tuesday table. And now here's Vee in her twin bed in Ohio, under the shelf of little gold synchronized-swimming trophies, envying Randi's whole lit spectacle — "even a poisoned Gstaad was more life than she had." I got to watch the two texts I already knew the insides of pass each other in the dark. Vee's *thinking about you 💛 ... i'm here if you want to talk. or not.* and Randi's *you ANGEL 😇 it's honestly a fairytale out here.* I already knew Randi typed her fairytale over a man who meant nothing and a cold she carried in with her. Vee reads it as the life she doesn't get to have. Two women, each certain the other one is warm. That's the whole engine and it's devastating, and the book earned it by giving me both interiors back to back. I didn't feel manipulated by the irony; I felt let in on it.

What I love about Vee here is the same thing I've loved all along, turned mournful: her honesty with herself even when the honesty has nowhere to go. "The room did not know her. It had known Vivienne — knew her still, in high resolution, and loved her." That line got me. The corkboard ribbons, the clarinet in the closet, the girls who "hadn't texted in a year" — a whole earlier self the room keeps dusted for, a girl who "no longer lived inside her." She's been "breathing bigger air for months" and can't fit back in the small room. That's the cost of the whole fall semester rendered in furniture, and I felt it in my chest. She's not sentimental about home and she's not cruel about it either — the good meat her mother made "because it was Christmas and it was love and had never once been the thing Vee was hungry for, not then and not now; she just hadn't had the word for it then." That's the appetite motif turned into grief. Being woken up has a price: you can't go back to sleep.

And then the part that genuinely frightened me for her — the spite-fantasy. After the fight with Pace and a whole week of silence across the break, with no name for what she is to him, she can't hold her own best memory. She reaches for the fra diavolo night, the sheet slipping at the table, the garlic blooming, her body quiet and used-well — reaches for it "to be warm" — and it *turns* on her while she's inside it: "The hair went gold and the leg went tan and the blonde sat where Vee had sat in the wine light." She built a rival "from spite," handed Pace "the exact opposite of herself and named it the thing he would want the moment he was free of her." I found that unbearable in the good way. It's exactly the interior life under the wanting the book keeps promising — the shame doing its work in the dark, converting love into dread. And the cruelest structural beat: all the reassurances she'd banked — the bartender, "he doesn't take advantage"; Randi, "he leaves you the room" — "had only ever held her up while she was the one," and "now she did not know she was anything, and they went straight through her hands. The floor was gone." The comfort was collateralized against a certainty she's lost.

Where's my swoon here? It's not heat — there's zero heat, correctly. It's the ache-swoon, the rooting. I want her to have him and I no longer trust that she does, and this chapter made me feel her not-knowing as my own. The image at the very end — letting herself have "the thing everyone else was having somewhere tonight — arms around her, his arms" — and it won't hold "because in the same breath she could not keep from it: whose he had them around instead." That's the fear and the wanting fused in one picture, and the line "no way on earth to know which was the true one" is the book's whole method said plainly.

My feelings moved this chapter. On Pace: my unease deepened, but honestly through Vee's spiral more than any new fact. The one concrete thing she reaches for is real and it stung — he cooked for Sheri at Thanksgiving ("she's alone"), and "about Christmas he'd said nothing at all — a whole week she couldn't see into." I don't actually know he did anything. Neither does she. But I know from the checkpoint that this man and Randi built her a plan, so when Vee invents a blonde at his stove I can't fully tell her she's wrong, and that split is where I live now. The page didn't hand me evidence against him; it handed me Vee's dread, and I'm holding it as dread, not as fact — the picture is ajar and I'm leaving it ajar.

On Randi: coming straight off "Fairytale," I feel more tenderness toward her than ever and it curdles here into something sadder, because Vee is lying under those trophies wishing she had Randi's night, and I know Randi's night was a bright empty room she'd have traded in a heartbeat for the Tuesday table with Vee. The dramatic irony makes both of them ache, and I ache for both. Vee even notes she "wished she could talk to Randi now" — and the one thing she can't talk to Randi about is the thing she's carrying (the fraud she saw and covered), and the one thing Randi can't say to her is the two-way meaning of "gorgeous." They're each holding a secret kindness back from the other. That's almost unbearably poignant and, given what I know, also a little sick — the closeness is real and it's also the plan working.

Trust vs. suspicion: no new machinery surfaced on the page. My suspicion sits exactly where the checkpoint left it — on the plan, on what Vee can't see. What's new is that the chapter let me feel how defenseless Vee is inside her own not-knowing, and that raised the dread without adding a fact. I want to be careful: nothing here *proved* Pace is unfaithful or cooling. The cooling is entirely Vee's midnight construction, and the text is honest that it's "what you believe at midnight." I'm taking it as her fear, earned by the fight, not as a reveal.

Erotic charge: deliberately none, and right. The nearest thing is the remembered fra diavolo/sheet tableau, and its whole point is that it *fails* to warm her — it gets colonized by the rival. So the absence of heat is doing emotional work, not leaving a gap. I didn't want heat here and would have flinched if the book had reached for it.

Friction: very little. The prose is doing the sustained-interior thing it does, and a couple of readers might find the loop of set-it-down-pick-it-up-set-it-down slightly long, but the repetition *is* the insomnia and I bought it. If I reach for a thumb-on-scale moment, it's "what you believe at midnight. Cold. Alone. On the one night you're not supposed to be." — that's the narrator underlining a feeling the scene had already delivered. It's a small over-say in a chapter that otherwise trusts me. I forgave it.

The titles. "Old Acquaintances" — I read it first as the Auld Lang Syne pun (New Year's, "should old acquaintance be forgot"), and the chapter is exactly about acquaintances being forgot: the girls on the corkboard who don't text, the earlier self the room still loves, the whole beige former life she's outgrown. But it recolored on me by the end — the two "acquaintances" who matter now, Pace and Randi, are each an unknown quantity tonight, a person she can't place, can't name, can't be sure of. Old acquaintance / new uncertainty. It's a gentle, apt title and I don't think it gave anything away; it pointed at the auld-lang-syne setting and let the ache accrue. Paired against Randi's chapter titled "Fairytale," the two titles rhyme cruelly — Randi's glittering "fairytale" is the thing Vee is envying by name in the text ("it's honestly a fairytale out here"), and both are hollow. The book title *A Polite Invitation* and series title *With a Long Spoon* still hum in the background: "he who sups with the devil should have a long spoon," and tonight I feel which of these women is supping with which devil, and how little spoon either has. The book title's "polite invitation" reads, from here, like the engineered courtesy of the whole thing — the warmth extended so exactly that you can't refuse it and can't name it either. (I was given no cover tagline in this packet, so I'll leave that.)

What I want / dread next (guess from here): I want the term to start, because Vee told me it's "the one sure thing the new year held" — Randi, a class, a face that lights up. I dread that reunion precisely because I know what Randi is holding and what the plan is, and Vee is walking into January starved for exactly the person most dangerous to her. And I dread the next Pace scene: the break has ended on silence after "what am I to you," and neither of them broke it. I don't know if he'll come warm or come changed, and — this is the point — neither does she. Guess: the term restarts and Randi is the one who "fixes" the Pace rupture for Vee, deepening Vee's dependence on exactly the two people running the game. I'm bracing for that.
