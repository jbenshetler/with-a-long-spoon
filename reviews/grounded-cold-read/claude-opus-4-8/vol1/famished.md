## Reader reaction

**The felt read.**

This is the one the whole slow burn has been leaning toward, and — I'll say it plainly — it landed on me. It swept me up. After ten-plus chapters of "May I" and waiting and the hem and the tape measure, Vee finally walks through the closed door at the end of the hall she leaned toward and wasn't given, and she takes it herself, and the taking is the thing. That's what made it work for me instead of just being the payoff on a countdown: it's *her* verb the whole way. "I never thanked you for the shirt," and she kisses him before he can answer. She pulls the apron strap. She asks to see the bedroom. She pulls him up, gets his jeans open, draws his arms around her neck and "she went." He keeps offering the beginning of care, the step-back, and she keeps refusing that particular tenderness — "she did not want his care, not that kind" — because she has finally decided what she wants and doesn't want to be slowed into being a nice girl about it. I found that genuinely moving. The book has been about a woman starved down to nothing by her mother's voice, and here she eats.

The heat is real and it is not clinical. The peak for me is standing up, his mouth on her, "the shame came up with the heat and she could not have said which was which... She waited for the shame to crest and close her, the way it always had, and it didn't." That's the whole interior arc rendered *as sensation* — I've been reading for exactly the thing under the wanting, the shame she won't look at, and this chapter finally opens it all the way and then burns it clean through. "The shame was still running and she was burning through it." And later, the plainest, best line in the chapter: after the actual sex, "the shame was still running and she could not find the top of it and she did not try. It was the first time that had been true." I felt that land in my chest. Not shame *cured* — shame outrun, which is truer and better.

The title pays off with no coyness and no giveaway. "Famished" is what she says about dinner, a beat behind him, and hears herself — but it's the whole starved appetite of her finally admitted out loud, food and being-wanted on the same wire. This book's hunger motif (the thin apple slices, the froyo cup getting away from her, eating unwatched at Christine's) has been building to a woman saying *I am starving* and being fed. She eats twice here — his mouth, and then the fra diavolo at the table, "she ate until she was full," her body quiet, "the evening had used her well." I loved that the sex and the supper rhyme.

Now — where I can't fully melt, and it isn't the chapter's fault, it's what I carry. I know what Vee doesn't. I know this man is Randi's lover, that this whole courtship was pointed at Vee as a game two people are running. So the entire tender consummation reads double for me. His question — "Would you be bothered if I was too hard?" — is disarming, it's the "May I" family scaled up to the bedroom, and on the page it's beautiful and it works. But I've watched him do exactly this attentiveness as an instrument. The page gives me nothing false in the room; the tenderness reads real. That's the ache the whole book runs on — I can't tell where the care stops and the operation starts, and neither can she, except she has no reason to look. This chapter didn't add new suspicion so much as let me feel the full weight of what I already know pressing on a scene that's playing entirely sincere. That's the good kind of unease, earned by the architecture, not manufactured here.

Two things the page *did* newly hold open, and I'm going to leave them open. First, the scar: "a scar, large, high on the inside of his thigh," and she stops on it, and then he draws her arms up and she goes. Unexplained. My checkpoint has a mention of Daphne, a woman from his past, a door he didn't respect that "cost him" — and a large scar high on the inner thigh is the kind of thing that *could* be a cost. But the page doesn't say, so I won't say. It's a flag, not a fact. Second, the very end: she folds her soaked panties, "the spot exactly where she'd put it," and holds them out — "Trade." He takes them, and "something in his shoulders was private and she let it be private." That gesture is the chapter's strangest, richest beat. On one reading it's the completion of her whole afternoon on the platform: the evidence of her wanting, which she used to die over, deliberately folded spot-centered and *given*, shame turned all the way over into gift, into a claim. She's not hiding the wet anymore; she's presenting it. It swooned me and it chilled me a half-degree in the same breath, because a woman handing a man the proof of her desire, wet and folded like an offering, is also a trophy passing into the hands of a man who collects people — and his "private" shoulders are a closed door I'm not allowed through. The word "Trade" implies she got something for it. I don't think she knows what she traded.

The domestic middle — sheet wound around her, sitting with the wine she never drank, watching him build the sauce from where he'd stopped, neither of them wanting to talk — is quietly perfect and I didn't want it shorter. "He'd done all the preparation and stopped" — the food held at the edge, waiting, is the same as everything he does, the withheld thing set out ready, and I clocked it without the book nudging me.

Friction: almost none. The one line I side-eyed is "Like a short story, she told the mirror, long enough to cover the subject and short enough to keep you reading" — it's a shade too writerly-cute for the moment — but she catches herself ("a nervous thing to say to an empty room, kidding herself"), so it stays inside character instead of being the author winking. I'll allow it.

On the book and series titles: no jacket or tagline came in this packet, so I'm reading only the names. *A Polite Invitation* keeps ringing truer the deeper in I get — the whole seduction is invitations, "Dinner Friday?", "May I," courtesy as the delivery system, and the politeness is exactly what disarms the guard. *With a Long Spoon* — the proverb about supping with the devil — sits over this chapter like a cold hand. Vee is at the table with her whole appetite finally open, no spoon at all, reaching in bare-handed. She's famished and she's feeding at exactly the table where you're supposed to keep your distance. The titles promise that the tenderness is the trap, and this chapter is the tenderness at its most convincing — which is to say, from where the titles sit, its most dangerous.

What I want next: I want to stay with Vee in this glow before it turns, because it's real and she earned it. And I dread — genuinely, from here, a guess — that the "trade," or the scar, or Randi's soft new mouth from last chapter, is a thread that gets pulled. I want to know what's private in his shoulders. I don't need this to end happily; I'd rather it broke honestly.
