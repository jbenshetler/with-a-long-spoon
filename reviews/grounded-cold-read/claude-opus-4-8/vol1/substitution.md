## Reader reaction

## The felt read

I read this whole chapter with my stomach dropping, slowly, the way it drops on stairs — which is exactly the image Vee gets handed at the end, and I think that's the point. Because I know what Vee doesn't. I know Randi and Pace. I know what the two of them did on the bench, what Randi decided in his bathroom mirror, and I know that four weeks ago Pace walked Randi across the quad pointing at women until she stopped cold, helpless, at a red-haired girl coming out of the dining hall — *this* girl, Vee, whose name Randi came back glowing with. I know Randi engineered a collision, a shared stats class, a coffee date, "love you, girl," the whole warm siege. So when Pace crosses the floor of the place on Wilson with his eyes on Vee "the whole way, not on the table, not on Randi, on her," I am not watching a meet-cute. I am watching a trap close, in daylight, with the girl inside it beaming.

And it worked on me anyway. That's the thing I have to be honest about. The seduction is *good*. The rolled cuffs, the forearms that "were not a math tutor's forearms," the way he reads the problem like he's meeting it fresh, asks what her professor said instead of showing off, writes the ugly formula only to wave it away, gives her back the pencil at the exact line where she can finish it herself — I felt the pull of it in real time, the same swoon Vee feels, right alongside the dread. "May I call you." I heard the *may* the way she heard it. It landed. The scene made me want it for her even as every fact I hold says *don't*. That doubleness is the whole charge of the chapter, and it's the darkest, cleanest thing the book has done to me yet.

Because here is what recontextualizes Pace, hard: this is the *same seduction he ran on Randi.* The ordinary-but-magnetic face, the total attention, the courtly formality, the reading-of-a-person as a structure — I'd taken all of that, on the bench, as devotion to Randi specifically. Watching him deploy the identical instrument on an unsuspecting undergrad who thinks he's a kind stranger, I understand it's a *method.* The tenderness is a technique, and he can point it. My trust in him didn't survive this chapter. He stayed warm and gentle on every surface — which is worse, not better. The gentleness is the mechanism.

And the cruelest, most brilliant stroke: Vee, with her econ eye, *analyzes the technique and is charmed by the analysis.* She clocks that he "kept himself out of it so the solving stayed hers," admires it as "the path of a person who wasn't performing," and — this is the line that put ice down my back — she told him earlier she likes econ because it's "the gap between what they say they're doing and what they're doing." She is describing precisely what is being done to her, at the table, by both people at it, and she cannot see it. She even *borrows Randi's name-bit* — "V.T., like the university, no my parents didn't plan it" — trying on the poise of the very person deceiving her. I ached. She is so smart and so hungry to be chosen that the hunger blinds the smarts.

Then there's Randi, and this is where I have to leave things ajar, because I'm in Vee's head and I only get Randi from the outside: the cup. Her hand goes to the coffee cup the instant Pace's attention lands on Vee, and it doesn't leave — "twice the cup came up to her mouth and went back down, and no sip happened." Her other hand in her lap. The bare "Randi," one word, no bit, none of the warmth she's poured on Vee for two chapters. Something is happening in her and I can't fully name it, and I don't want to name it more definitely than the page does. Is it jealousy — watching the man who put her on that bench look at another girl "like he'd forgotten there was anyone else in the room"? Is it the sick cost of a plan she set in motion and is now watching arrive? Guilt? All three? She *engineered* this. She picked Vee off a lawn as a "gift." And yet it's plainly costing her something, and Cassie — watching the cup, twice — sees it. I moved on Randi this chapter: on the bench I pitied her, here I distrust and pity her at once, and I can't resolve which is louder. The girl who lives to be looked at is being looked *past,* by the one person who looked all the way through her, and Cassie says it out loud with the knife I'd been waiting for: he looked at Randi "the way you'd look at somebody's great-aunt." That's the substitution. That word is doing a lot.

Cassie I love without complication. She's the one honest instrument in the book. Her flat "Goodbye, Vee" in the last chapter had already made the back of my neck cold — the whole word, the word for a longer parting than a Wednesday — and here she's the truth-teller who names the looks, watches the cup, sees the geometry everyone else is pretending isn't there. She reads like the reader's proxy, the person who brought a long spoon to the table. I want her to protect Vee. I'm afraid she can't.

**Trust vs. suspicion:** no ambient dread here that the text didn't earn — the dread is entirely built from what I *know* and Vee doesn't. That's clean dramatic irony, not a manufactured shadow. The one thing I'm holding genuinely open is Randi's interior: the page gives me the cup and the bare name and lets me infer the rest, and I'm going to leave it inferred, not decided.

**Erotic charge:** low and sublimated, and that's right for the scene — the heat is entirely in the tutoring, the forearms, the "may I," the being-chosen. It's a seduction, not sex, and it simmers exactly as much as it should. Where it "peaks" is the pencil handed back and the "may I call you," both of which are erotic precisely because nothing overt happens.

**Friction:** very little. "The thought did not finish" runs three times — Vee's body outpacing her mind — and by the third it's a hair mechanical, but it's also *the* accurate rendering of a person half-catching a thing she can't afford to catch, so I forgave it. The cup business is maybe one Cassie-glance past necessary, but it stayed mostly restrained. I bought the chapter.

**The titles:** "Substitution" sat innocuous when I opened it — u-substitution, the math trick, "turn it into something you already know how to do," Euler's formula slotted in at right angles until the hard thing goes plain. Then it curdles on the second thought: *Vee is the substitution.* A new girl substituted into Pace's method; "theirs" substituted for "hers"; Randi substituted *out* of the gaze she's built her life on. It didn't give anything away going in; it recolored everything coming out. That's the mechanism working on me exactly once. The book title, *A Polite Invitation* — I feel it land here in the "May I call you," the courtesy as the wrapper the trap comes in; the invitation is genuinely polite and that's what makes it work. And *With a Long Spoon* — I finally feel the proverb bite: he who sups with the devil needs a long spoon. Someone at that table is the devil, and Vee came to supper without one, and I'm no longer sure Randi has one either — she may have thought she was holding the spoon and is finding out at this table that she's at the wrong end of it. (No jacket copy or tagline came in my packet, so I'm going only on the two titles.)

**What I want / dread next (guesses from here):** I dread this landing on Vee — the phone she won't turn over, wanting it to light up, is going to light up. I want to know Randi's real feeling and I expect the book to make me wait for it and to keep it complicated. I half-expect this to be less a threesome procurement than something worse: Pace running his whole tender apparatus on Vee as a *second* courtship, with Randi watching herself be replaced by the thing she went and fetched. And I want Cassie in the room when it goes wrong. Guess, from here: the "latent variable" the last chapter named — the factor you can't measure, only infer — is Randi's actual heart, and this book is going to make me infer it a long time before it says it.
