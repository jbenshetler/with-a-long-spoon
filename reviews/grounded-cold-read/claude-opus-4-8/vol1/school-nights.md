# Cold read (grounded) — School Nights

## Reader reaction

FELT READ

After the escalating fever of the last few chapters — the induction walk, the bare-under-the-dress costume game, the bathroom in the bistro where she couldn't finish because Randi's face arrived, the drawer he cleared for her — this chapter lands soft and quiet, and I was grateful for the exhale. It's a Cassie chapter, and it works on me the way Cassie always does: as the one clean surface in a book full of hidden joints.

Vee comes in "past eleven with the night still on her," lit up, and Cassie doesn't even look up: "You're doing the streetlight thing again. You're a public utility." I love that line so much. It's affectionate and it's a diagnosis — Vee is glowing outward, broadcasting, spending light on everyone. And the whole chapter is Cassie watching that glow and gently, precisely, taking its measure. She "listened with no face on at all," she "let it sit," she lets the "maybe" stand. That restraint is its own kind of love and I trust it completely.

What moved in me here: nothing about my feeling toward anyone shifted so much as it settled and ached. Vee telling the tomato-burn story with her hand going to the side of her breast "without her knowing it moved" — that's the whole book in one gesture. Her body narrates before she does; she doesn't even govern the telling. And Cassie catches it dry: "There's only one way that burn happens. Not enough on." That's Cassie reading, in a single wry beat, that Vee was topless at his stove — and choosing not to make her say it. It's the tenderness of a friend who sees the omission and doesn't force it open. Which is exactly the opposite of what Randi does at brunch, and the contrast is quietly devastating. Randi mines; Cassie declines to mine. One draws Vee open on purpose; one lets her keep what she keeps.

The line that got me was "You don't come home school nights." Not a question — Cassie's whole register is statements Vee has to walk back into and check. And Vee "went back over the week she hadn't counted and found her own bed had sat empty more nights than it hadn't." That's the consequence-stacking I've been watching — skipped classes, the maxed card — surfacing here not as alarm but as a fact Vee genuinely hadn't tallied. Cassie's "You've had boyfriends. You never went like this over one" is the reader's own unease, spoken kindly. She's not warning; she's noticing out loud, and letting Vee hear it.

And then the "fine" — this is the jacket's whole thesis handed to Cassie's floor. "The ones before him had been fine." Vee reaches for the word after "fine" and doesn't have one and laughs at herself reaching, puts her hands up at all of it. I felt that reach. The inarticulateness is the truest thing about her happiness right now — she can't name it, so she can only gesture, and the gesture is that "the whole of the room went dark around her." That's swoon and it's also a small chill: everything that isn't him is going dark.

"Do you love him." Flat, the same way she'd said the rest. And Vee "heard herself go small. Maybe. I think so." And then "It's all so fast." That's the closest Vee's come to naming her own vertigo, and she names it to Cassie, not to Randi and not to Pace — which tells me where her real honesty still lives. In "Above Him" she let "she loved him" be true in her own chest; here, out loud, to the one person who won't feed it back to anyone, it shrinks to "maybe." I don't read that as backpedaling — I read it as the difference between what she can hold privately and what she can bear to hand over. And Cassie, beautifully, "let the maybe stand. She didn't say a thing against it, and she didn't say I know, only went back to her book." She holds Vee's door open without pushing through it. God, I trust her.

Trust vs. suspicion: no new suspicion earned here, and that's part of the point — this is the chapter without Randi and Pace in the room, and the air is different. Cleaner. What unsettles me isn't anything Cassie does; it's what her steadiness measures. "Go to sleep, streetlight" bookends "public utility," and between those two the whole chapter has quietly established that Vee is disappearing into someone else's warmth and can't count the cost of it herself. Cassie can. Cassie isn't sounding an alarm — she's just the instrument that reads true, and the reading is: this is more than a boyfriend, and it's fast, and Vee's own bed is empty. I sat with Cassie's "Goodbye, Vee" from way back and this felt like the same worry, warmer, unspoken.

Erotic charge: low and secondhand, and rightly so — the heat here is in the retelling and the involuntary hand-to-breast, not on the page as sex. This chapter isn't trying to work me that way, and I didn't want it to. It's the cool-down, the honest room.

Friction: none, really. It's short and it earns its shortness. If anything I wanted one more beat from Cassie — she reads Vee so completely and says so little that I found myself hungry for what she's not saying, which I think is deliberate but did leave me slightly wanting.

The title: "School Nights" — on the surface, innocuous, a scheduling fact. Cassie's line "You don't come home school nights" is its literal home. But read against everything, it colors darker: these are the nights being quietly consumed, the classes and the routine and the ordinary life dissolving into his warm house. "School" is the whole architecture of Vee's actual life — her major, her honors, her future — and the nights of it are going empty. The title points at cost without naming it, which is the book's whole method with me.

The book's title A Polite Invitation and the series With a Long Spoon: the spoon keeps meaning what it means — you sup with the devil with a long spoon, and this chapter is Cassie holding the long spoon while Vee has forgotten she owns one. The cover line I was given, "Every yes was freely given. That was the problem," sits right under this whole scene: Vee's empty bed, her uncounted nights, her can't-find-the-word happiness are all freely chosen, gladly, and Cassie is the one person watching the freely-chosen accrue.

What I want next: I want Cassie to stay the compass and I dread the moment she can't reach Vee anymore. I want Vee to keep one true room — and this chapter reassured me she still has one, in Cassie. Guess from here, marked as a guess: the book is lining Cassie up as the person who'll see the plan, or its shape, before Vee does, and "School Nights" is planting how far outside Vee's life has already drifted for that to land. But nothing forced it; I just felt the ground being kept honest.
