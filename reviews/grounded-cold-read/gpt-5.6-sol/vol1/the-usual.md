## Reader reaction

My first feeling is relief: Pace misses her. He has not replaced her, has not decided the rupture means nothing, and has not been sleeping easily through a silence only Vee feels. The chapter lets me into the ache on his side almost immediately—“Vee would either love this place or never see it at all, and he wanted very badly to know which”—and that lands hard. The possibility in that “or never see it at all” hurts because he is already imagining his future divided by whether she comes back.

I am also more frustrated with him than I have ever been. His care is real; his love seems almost embarrassingly visible; and he still cannot say the thing. When he explains, “I’m there. Every weekend, I’m there,” I understand him. This is how Pace speaks: heat turned up before she arrives, food built around her hunger, room made in a drawer, attention held wholly on her. But Sheri cuts straight through the defense: “She didn’t ask you for reasons.” Exactly. Vee did not fail to notice his actions. She named them all to his face. She asked for the meaning he gives those actions, and he answered as though more evidence could settle a question about commitment.

The hardest line is Sheri’s: “Then not saying it matters more to you than having her.” My body went still there, because Pace looks down instead of arguing. Whatever he “carried in from the truck,” whatever he has long practice deciding “could keep,” is costing him Vee now. I do not know what it is, and the page does not let me decide whether he literally cannot say he loves her, cannot promise a conventional relationship, or cannot make some other declaration she would accept. But “I can’t” is no longer attractive reserve. It is a wall, and right now he is choosing to hold it.

Sheri rises enormously for me. She is coarse, funny, loyal, and exactly the friend he needs. I like that her crudeness about Vee’s body does not become rivalry or threat; she can appreciate “that backside” and still be entirely uninterested in taking anything from Pace. Her friendship with him feels wonderfully clean: “she just liked him and gave him hell.” She refuses both to flatter him and to let his genuine goodness excuse the harm he is doing. “From this seat, that feels pretty shitty” is blunt without being cruel.

Her own story moved me, too. Paige’s large laugh and crowded family table appear only briefly, but they make the loss feel specific. Sheri did not merely lose a girlfriend; she lost a noisy household she liked entering, because Paige treated Sheri’s estrangement as a repairable stubbornness instead of a wound. Pace’s response—“People who’ve never lost anything think grief is a choice”—is one of those sentences that makes me ache for both of them. It deepens my suspicion that his refusal and his family estrangement touch the same buried place, but that is only a guess from here. The page gives me the shared grief, not its history.

The chapter has little conventional swoon, but there is strong emotional pull in Pace’s helplessly particular love for Vee. “The air’s different when she’s in the room. It—crackles.” Then, even more tellingly, he says she understands “the whole idea under” his food. That is so precisely them: he expresses himself through making and feeding, and she experiences the thought inside the meal. I believe completely that he loves her. That belief makes his refusal worse, not better. Sheri’s “Lord almighty. Get over yourself” punctures his solemnity at exactly the right moment, and his first full laugh gives me warmth without falsely resolving anything.

I trust Sheri. I still trust Pace’s tenderness and consent, and this chapter strengthens my trust that Vee is not interchangeable to him. But I am suspicious of the sealed place inside him that turns “I want Vee. I miss her” into “I can’t” when the words must be given to Vee herself. The opening says he has “long practice” putting painful things away; that feels earned as a warning because the chapter shows the habit operating in real time. He chooses not to tell Sheri what he brought in, just as he has kept his family, scar, and now this refusal behind closed doors.

There is also the continuing dishonesty around Randi. Sheri asks whether Vee found out about “the brunette,” and Pace says, “No. Not like that.” That answer leaves the larger fact untouched: Vee still does not know. Then Sheri casually reveals that she has met Randi, though apparently Pace did not remember it, and recalls Randi looking at her like “one more of your harem applicants.” It is funny, but it also makes the hidden overlap feel crowded and dangerously ordinary. Pace can speak of Vee as though nothing else is in the room while maintaining a secret relationship that is absolutely relevant to what Vee believes they are.

There is no explicit sex here, but the erotic charge still flickers through Sheri’s teasing and Pace’s blush. “Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you” is vivid, funny, and bodily; his embarrassed agreement that the sex is good confirms desire without turning Vee into only a body. The charge is a simmer rather than the chapter’s center.

The title did not give away the chapter; it became sadder as I read.
