The first thing this chapter does to me is make me want Randi and Vee together—not abstractly, not merely because I know the kisses happened, but physically, in the room. Randi looking at Vee in the dress, going still before she speaks, asking her to “Turn for me — slow,” then touching the silk up Vee’s body is intensely charged. “He made it *to* you” is a beautiful piece of recognition: Randi understands exactly why the dress moved Vee to tears, and she gives Vee language that enlarges the feeling instead of taming it. I melted for that. Randi can see Vee with unnerving precision.

That precision is also why I cannot entirely relax. Randi’s delight feels real here—the ruined-shoe laughter especially, “the real laugh, the one that costs her something to give,” makes her feel less polished and more open with Vee. Their silliness is easy, mutual, and lovely. Vee gets to be funny, not merely dazzled, instructed, or desired; feeling “like the funniest woman in Virginia” may be the warmest moment in the chapter for me. I believe their friendship as Vee experiences it. That makes the concealed beginning hurt more, not less.

The boutique scene turns the heat up beautifully. Randi kneeling, taking Vee’s foot in both hands, noticing the plum polish, and moving her thumb once over the big toenail absolutely works on me. Vee feels it “go all the way up the length of her,” grabs Randi’s shoulder, and Randi looks up and smiles without explaining anything. That silence is hotter than an explanation would be. It is intimate, deliberately prolonged, and neither woman seems confused in her body, even if Vee still will not consciously name what is happening. I wanted Randi to keep touching her. I wanted Vee to stop calling this only friendship.

But the chapter also gave me my first strong discomfort with Randi’s influence over Vee that is not principally about the hidden plan. Randi does not force Vee to buy the shoes, does not offer to pay, and may not even understand what Vee’s silence means. Still, she takes Vee from an affordable compromise into a boutique where Vee cannot afford anything, then gives extravagance a moral and emotional logic: the dress “goes on these”; cheaper shoes would be “an apology”; the dress is “owed” the expensive pair. By the register, Vee has absorbed that logic so completely that “The shoes were not a want she had to own as a want.” That line chilled me. It is exactly the kind of self-deception Vee is studying elsewhere: she does want them, badly, but converts desire into obligation so she does not have to claim either the want or the reckless choice.

Watching her put an unaffordable purchase on the emergency credit card, knowing “she did not have the next part, the paying-it-back part,” took the shine off the fantasy for me. Not because she should never have beautiful things, and not because desire must always be sensible. I understand why the shoes feel transformative. But this is debt, not merely indulgence, and Randi’s effortless purchase beside her makes the class gap suddenly brutal. Randi spends several times as much without looking while Vee hides a financial wound because she cannot bear to “flinch” in that room. Randi notices the comic way Vee clutches the box but apparently does not notice—or does not address—the fear underneath it. I do not yet know which.

Vee has my tenderness throughout. She is radiant in the dress and adorable during the shoe-store play, and I love that she is learning to inhabit beauty instead of apologizing for it. Yet her emerging freedom is becoming entangled with performance: arriving late so the room will turn, buying the shoes the dress is supposedly owed, refusing to let cost reach her face. Some of that is thrilling ownership. Some of it looks painfully like a new version of managing how she appears. The page leaves those together, and I feel both.
