## Reader reaction

This chapter made me feel relieved in my body before it made me angry. Vee reaching Randi’s room, breathing in “bitter orange, green at the edge, nothing sweet,” and finally collapsing into a hug she does not have to earn landed hard. I believe that embrace. Randi sweeping the expensive new clothes onto the floor because Vee matters more made me ache for both of them, and the fact that neither woman lets go when the hug should have ended has real romantic pull. Vee has missed Randi bodily, not just conversationally, and Randi is holding the woman she loves without daring to name what that holding means. I wanted them to stay there.

But once Randi begins interpreting Pace for Vee, my relief curdles. She does help Vee separate the imagined Sheri from the real evidence: “I’ve seen her… In my mind” is a painful, honest recognition, and “fear needs a face to point at” is genuinely perceptive. Randi gives Vee back some ground beneath her feet. Yet she also knows far more than she admits—she knows Pace’s attachment, knows Sheri is not the hidden woman, and knows that if there is “another woman,” it is Randi herself. Her certainty—“I know so”—therefore feels deeply unfair. She is not merely comforting a friend from good intuition; she is using concealed knowledge to make her guidance seem almost magically trustworthy.

Worse, she recasts Vee’s legitimate question as an attack on Pace: “You backed him into a corner in his own house. Name it or lose me—the one thing he can’t do.” That moved me sharply against Randi. Vee did not invent Pace’s refusal. She asked what their relationship was after months of intimacy, and he would not answer. Randi makes his silence romantic—“a boy holding himself still so it won’t show how much you got him”—and turns Vee into the person who “blindsided him with a fight.” Some of that may be true from Pace’s side, but Randi fixes his motive more confidently than the page permits and uses that version to take the weight off him. I felt Vee’s real hurt being tidied away because Randi wants the relationship restored.

The coat plan is intensely hot and intensely troubling for exactly the same reason: it finds the seam between Vee’s shame and her desire and pulls. Bare beneath a long coat, heels on, crossing the cold to Pace’s warm door is charged as hell. Vee’s wanting rises through the mortification, and I believe she chooses it. Yet Randi has again converted Vee’s need for words into a sexual answer: “You don’t do it with words. Words are the whole war.” Vee came asking whether she is loved and leaves planning to present her naked body. That does not make her desire false, but it does not answer her question either. She risks offering Pace exactly the language in which he already excels, while retiring the language she needs from him.

Randi’s speech—“You’d be walking through the cold to hand him everything, no words, no fight, nothing kept back”—swept me up despite my resistance. It is a gorgeous erotic proposition, and “there’s not a man alive who survives it” has the confident, wicked warmth that makes Randi so magnetic. I understand why Vee feels remade by her. But “nothing kept back” is frightening here because Vee has already given Pace nearly everything and is suffering over what he keeps back. The imbalance is not solved by her giving more.

Vee herself has all my tenderness. Her exhausted certainty that the silence means she was only sex feels believable after a month without contact, though I do not believe Pace used her. Her jealousy of an invented Sheri hurts more because the actual Sheri is nothing like the woman Vee constructed. I was glad Randi punctured that fantasy. I was less glad that Vee’s despair became proof that she must make the next—and most exposed—gesture. I want her to go to Pace because I want them back in the same room, but I dread the possibility that spectacular sex will drown the unanswered question again.

Brooke warmed me unexpectedly. “Oh, honey” could have been social performance, but nothing on the page earns suspicion of it here; she sees distress and offers uncomplicated help. The unknown blonde is cooler but not cruel—she correctly recognizes that Vee’s crisis belongs with Randi. Pace is absent, but his absence dominates every inch of the chapter. My feelings toward him remain divided: I still believe his care was real, and Sheri’s recent conversation confirms that he misses Vee and cannot say what she asked him to say. But a month of mutual silence is not just Vee’s doing. He chose not to contact her, too. Randi’s defense does not absolve him.

The New Year story creates a second, quieter ache. Vee hears precisely what Randi once wanted to hear: a man inventing marriage and children aloud without fear, a future instantly given shape. Her repeated responses—“The kind you tell your daughter about,” “And you just let him lead”—are not merely delight in Randi’s adventure; they press directly on everything Pace will not name. She hears John’s easy declarations as paradise while Randi knows that paradise was emotionally empty. The two women are sitting side by side envying opposite halves of each other’s lives, neither able to tell the other the truth. That hurt beautifully.

Randi’s “Everywhere but the bedroom, and the bedroom’s the room I actually live in” is funny enough to make Vee laugh, but it is also a brilliant concealment. The sex with John was good; what failed was that he never found her. Randi turns emotional emptiness into a bedroom-performance joke because that is the version Vee can receive without asking more. I felt sympathy rather than suspicion there. Randi cannot tell Vee that Pace reaches her, or that Vee does too, so she leaves the real room locked.

Erotically, this is simmer rather than consummation. The extended hugs, Vee breathing Randi in, their knees touching, Randi brushing back her hair, the coat-and-heels fantasy, and the now-routine mouth kiss all carry charge. The peak for me is not the imagined doorstep reveal but the first hug held “past where a hug stops.” That is the place where the desire feels least performed and most dangerous. The coat plan is hotter in a conventional sense, but the hug has the deeper current.

The chapter title, “My Friend Randi,” lands with painful irony. Randi truly is Vee’s friend—perhaps the person Vee most instinctively seeks—and this chapter proves the friendship’s tenderness. But “friend” is also the cover under which Randi can love, desire, steer, and deceive her. Vee leaves believing her friend has helped her see clearly, while I know Randi has used truths unavailable to Vee and withheld the central one. The title does not give the chapter away; it makes the simple word “friend” feel full of incompatible truths.
