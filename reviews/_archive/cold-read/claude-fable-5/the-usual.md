# Cold read — The Usual

*scene: scenes/the-usual.md · model: claude-fable-5 · read after: old-acquaintances*

## Reader reaction

**Felt read**

Oh, this was a relief and a gut-punch at once. After two chapters of midnight loneliness on either side of the country — Randi in Gstaad, Vee in her outgrown bed — the book finally lets me at Pace, and it does it sideways: not alone at the house, but across a booth from Sheri, who I've known mostly as a name ("Pace cooked for her Thanksgiving — 'She's alone'") and who turns out to be the best thing that could have happened to this stretch of the book.

Sheri is wonderful. Five feet of scowl and stolen fries, the two-color hair, "worn like she'd dare you to mention it." She's the first person in the entire novel who wants *nothing* off Pace — "She didn't want him, she wasn't impressed by him... she just liked him and gave him hell" — and the moment I read that line I understood why he drove out to the county road. Everyone in this book wants something. Randi wants Vee. Vee wants the words. Sheri wants his fries. And so she's the only one who can put the knife in cleanly: "Then not saying it matters more to you than having her... And I'll tell you — from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." That exchange is the spine of the chapter and it landed hard, because she says exactly what I've been muttering at Pace for three chapters, and he doesn't argue. "There was nothing in it to argue with." He *knows*. That's worse and better than not knowing.

And her own grief-shape — Paige, the girl who "couldn't get right with me not talking to my people," the estrangement that isn't a choice, "call your mama, Sheri, it's Christmas" done in sing-song and killed flat — quietly rhymed with everything I know about Pace's cut-off parents, and the book didn't press it. "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice" is the closest Pace comes to speaking about himself all chapter, and Sheri catches it: "Don't waste the good stuff on me. Save it for your redhead." The chapter trusts me. The jukebox playing "I'll Be Home for Christmas" over two estranged people at Christmas, and neither of them saying anything because "there was nothing to say over it that wouldn't press the bruise" — that's the book at its most restrained and it worked on me physically. My chest did the thing.

The first line, though — "he would not bring her down today." Bring her *down*. So the silence toward Vee isn't neglect; it's a decision, renewed each day, dressed as consideration — "he had long practice at deciding a thing could keep." That's the most damning sentence in the chapter and it's delivered as calm. He misses her ("I want Vee. I miss her" — the plainest want he's voiced in the whole book, and it moved me), he's cataloguing the diner through her eyes ("Vee would either love this place or never see it at all, and he wanted very badly to know which"), and he still won't cross. Bricks: "every brick was true and the true things were supposed to come to something." That's ch.44's fight restated from inside his skull, and it's exactly Vee's diagnosis — half of something.

Two little bombs Sheri drops in passing: "She find out about the brunette, then" — so Sheri knows about Randi, knows there's a concealment, and treats it as ordinary Pace-business, which chills me a bit — and the account of Randi at the dance before Thanksgiving looking at Sheri "like I was one more of your harem applicants," never learning her name. That's the first outside view of Randi I've had, and it's not flattering: possessive, dismissive of a woman with no money and off makeup. It fits Standards. It also answers an open question — the Thanksgiving dancing brunette was Randi.

Erotic charge: none, and correctly none — but the blush is here. "Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you" / "His face was hot and he knew it, and he went on anyway." Pace embarrassed is new and endearing. And "she gets what it's for. The whole idea under it. The philosophy of it" — that's the fra diavolo motif spoken aloud from his side, the very memory Vee's spite-blonde stole in the last chapter. He treasures the thing she thinks is colonized. That irony ached.

Friction: almost none. The one beat I'd flag is the kid delivering the double to Pace "with the grave confidence of arithmetic" — charming, but the little comedy of plate-swapping runs a touch long for what it buys. Tiny.

The title: "The Usual" — already written, "because there was no question and never had been." It's the diner order and it's Sheri: the one relationship in Pace's life with no performance, no ledger, no wanting. Also, darker: his silence toward Vee is *his* usual — the long practice of deciding things can keep. The book title's long spoon — I keep supping with these people and every one of them is more sympathetic than the last, which is presumably how the devil works.

What I want next: the term to start, the collision. Guess from here: Vee comes back and Randi gets to her first — the fairytale friend, sure and scheduled — while Pace goes on deciding it can keep, and Sheri's verdict ("pretty shitty") hangs over him until something forces the word out of him or costs him her. I also now expect Sheri's Brooks and Dunn night, first Saturday in February, to be a scene — "I don't care what the redhead's got to say about it" feels like a planted collision.

**Structured block**

**Cast present (in person):** Pace, Sheri, the waitress, the delivery kid. Mentioned-only: Vee ("the redhead"), Randi ("the brunette — the sorority one"), Paige, Sheri's estranged people, Elvis on the jukebox.

**Heat:** 1 — clothed, all banter; Sheri's raunch ("that backside," "long legs like Christmas") and Pace's blush simmer without igniting.

**Romance:** 2 — Pace's plainest devotion yet ("I want Vee. I miss her"; the philosophy-of-food tribute), spoken to a third party, which is both tender and the whole problem.

**Motifs & images:** *Food-as-eros/food-as-meaning* — repeat (fra diavolo, Famished): "she gets what it's for... the philosophy of it." *Loud room vs. quiet* — repeat (Chili's vs. diner explicitly recalled; "Here I could have heard her whisper"). *Words vs. acts* — repeat, now weaponized by Sheri: "So say it." / "I can't." *Cold outside / warm inside, fogged glass* — repeat of cold=truth, inverted: fog "kept the cold where it belonged." *Christmas song about home* — first, rhyming with both estrangements. *Amber glass tabletop / preserved past* — first. *"He had long practice at deciding a thing could keep"* — first, and I expect it back.

**Symbolism:** The single/double burgers mis-delivered by "the grave confidence of arithmetic" — appearances mis-assigning appetite; tiny Sheri gets the double. The dark jukebox waking on someone else's coin: grief you didn't choose, played into the room anyway.

**Characterization:** Sheri arrives fully three-dimensional in one chapter — best single-scene character debut in the book. Pace deepened materially: the *can't* (not won't) around the declaration, the blush, the missing. Randi thinned slightly via outside view — usefully.

**Pace — within the chapter:** Beautifully unhurried; the food/talk rhythm earns its length. Only the burger-swap gag slightly overlong.

**Pace — chapter to chapter:** Third consecutive holiday-limbo chapter, but the triptych (Randi/Vee/Pace, each alone-ish at year's turn) now feels complete rather than repetitive. Ready — needing — the term to start.
