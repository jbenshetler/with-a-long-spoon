## Reader reaction

Oh, this is the chapter I didn't know I was starving for. After two cold beds and Vee inventing a blonde rival to torture herself with, the book cuts to *Pace* — and he's at a double-wide diner on a county road, unable to eat his cheeseburger because he's too busy going on about Vee to a friend who steals his fries and tells him to get over himself. The gap between Vee's midnight fear and this is devastating in exactly the right way. She pictured him cooking for someone else; he's here missing her so plainly it hurts: "I want Vee. I miss her." No rival. A friend, a chocolate shake, and a man stuck.

And Sheri is a gift. Small, five feet, two-tone permed hair, too much makeup worn "like she'd dare you to mention it" — a country girl making it work on her own dime, part-time in school now "that there was nobody behind her writing checks," said plainly, moving on before he can feel anything on her behalf. She wants nothing off him — "She didn't want him, she wasn't impressed by him, and she'd say so to his face" — and that's why he came in needing her. She's queer, too: Paige, the design-school junior who "couldn't get right with me not talking to my people." Sheri's estrangement from her family rhymes with Pace's closed room — "People who've never lost anything think grief is a choice." Two people who don't go back, sharing a booth.

The core of it: **Sheri puts the impasse cleaner than anyone has.** Pace lays up his bricks — every weekend, weeknights he gave her, "when I've got her there's nothing else in the room," *I told her she was the most important thing in my life* — "and she looked at me like I'd handed her half of something." And Sheri, not softening a thing: "not saying it matters more to you than having her... from this seat, that feels pretty shitty." He looks down. No answer. That's the wall Vee hit, seen from his side — and the book still won't tell me *why* he can't. It leaves it honestly ajar. He *wants* to, he misses her, he's not lying, he's not with someone else — he simply cannot say the word, and something in that is load-bearing and unshown.

Two things kept me a little on edge inside all this warmth. The opening: "he would not bring her down today... whatever he'd carried in from the truck could keep; he had long practice at deciding a thing could keep." He came in with *something* that would hurt Sheri, and shelved it. I don't know what. And "long practice at deciding a thing could keep" is the same controlled deferral I've watched him run on everyone. It doesn't earn a specific dread here — he's being kind to a friend at Christmas — but it's a note I'm holding.

The other: Sheri confirms she knows he runs two — "She find out about the brunette, then?" — no alarm, just fact, the two-at-once she flagged before. And she saw Vee "at the bar before she started throwing things" (Vee, *throwing things* at a bar — new, and I want that story). Whether Sheri is the older bartender who reassured Vee I still can't settle — she's a young design student, which cuts against "older" — so I'll hold it open, but she was *there.*

Heat is banter-level — "Bet those long legs feel like Christmas wrapped around you," his face going hot as he insists it's that she "gets what it's for, the whole idea under it," and Sheri deadpanning "The philosophy of your food. Lord almighty." That made me laugh out loud with them. The chapter's real charge is romantic and blocked: "The air's different when she's in the room. It — crackles."

**The title, "The Usual"** — the waitress writing the order before they speak, "like people saying yes to their own names." The unforced, un-engineered thing: no scene, no consent architecture, no one being run. Just the usual, with the one person he doesn't have to manage. It's the warmest room in the book so far — and the cruelty is that the woman he's gone on is the one he *can't* be usual with, because she wants the one word.

**How I feel — overall**
Melted, and thrown.
