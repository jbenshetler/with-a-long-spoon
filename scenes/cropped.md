# Cropped

The athletic cafeteria had the better food and, more to the point, somewhere to actually sit. The two of them at a table near the windows, neither an athlete. Sunday dinner, the night everyone came back. A low murmur under the high ceiling, punctuated by the clink of steel on hard plastic.

"How was it?" Vee asked. "Home."

Cassie thought about it. "Good to see my parents. My sister has the new baby, so it was all baby, the whole time. Her husband's alright. A little bit of a dork." She speared something on her plate. "It was good to leave, too. They still treat me like I'm in high school. Not overtly. But I can tell they don't quite see me as an adult yet." She looked up. "What about you?"

"Good," Vee said, and meant it. "They were so proud. The honor society thing. Mom cried on the phone when I told her, and then she cried again at the table, just remembering it." She smiled down at her tray. "Dad burned the rolls. On purpose, I'm pretty sure."

"He does that?"

"He does." Vee turned her fork over. "Mom had the picture. From the induction. She'd printed it out, she had it in her purse, she kept taking it out to show people who'd already seen it. I cropped it before I sent it. The version they have stops at the waist."

Cassie waited.

"And Dad kept looking at it. Not at me, at the dress." She set the fork down. "And Mom had thoughts about the top of it. The shoulders. She didn't say anything, just touched her own collarbone while she looked. You know how she does."

"I know how she does," Cassie said.

"She touched mine, too. At the door, before she'd even said hello. She said I'd gotten thin." Vee picked the fork back up. "She had her nurse face on all week. Every time I went back for seconds she watched me eat them, like they might be a trick. One night she came and sat on the end of my bed and asked if I was sleeping, if something was going on at school. If I was making myself sick." She turned the fork over. "She fusses over me like I'm a child. I told her it was the dancing, that we go dancing all the time. And then I finally just told her I'm happier than I've ever been."

Cassie looked at Vee's tray, most of the way gone already.

"How many times have you actually gone dancing?"

"Once."

"You lied to your mother."

"How am I supposed to tell her it's the sex?" Vee said. "The amount of sex?"

"Good point," Cassie said.

"Then of course it turned into, well, who took you to this thing. Who gives a girl a dress like that. Who is he." Vee heard the next part before she said it. "I told them he was my boyfriend. I think I put a question mark on it. Out loud. Like I was asking them."

Cassie didn't laugh.

"I told Dad the parts that fit." Vee looked at the window, the dark already down over the parking lot. "He's a math PhD candidate. We went hiking. He taught me to dance on a square of concrete with a boom box before he ever took me to a hall. Dad was happy. More than happy. A boy doing a doctorate who does the right things with his daughter, that's something a father gets to be proud of."

"Then he looked at the dress again and went quiet. He was pricing it. An expensive gift from a man means his daughter is in deeper than he'd pictured. And he pictured someone older than Pace is. To Dad, a math PhD is a man with grey coming in, not someone barely older than me."

"I didn't tell him Pace cooks for me. Or that he owns his house. My father would start looking for the catch. Too good is its own kind of suspicious."

"And what I couldn't tell him at all is that Pace made the dress. That he sewed it himself, on a machine on his sun porch, because I mentioned once I'd seen one in a magazine I couldn't afford." She said it slowly, like she was finding the size of it. "Honestly, my dad would take it better if I told him Pace takes that dress off me than that he made it. Sex he understands. The sewing he never would."

That pulled a short laugh out of Cassie.

"And it wouldn't even be that he'd think it was wrong. He'd think it was weird. In some ways weird is worse than wrong. Wrong is something he understands and rejects. Weird is something that doesn't fit anywhere in how he sees the world." She stopped. "I'm not ashamed of it. The opposite, if anything."

Cassie had stopped eating. "So what is he," she said. Careful, exact, not prying. "Not to your dad. To you."

Vee looked at her tray. She had nothing that fit in Cassie's frame, not the right word for what he was, not the right story for how it had gone, not the version of herself that stood up cleanly in the telling.

"Is it that you can't say," Cassie asked, "or that you don't want to?"

"Both," Vee said. "Probably both."

Cassie nodded and went back to her plate. She didn't push at it. Vee was grateful. She was also, a little, not.

---

He kept the porch heater turned down overnight, and what warmth there was out here came mostly from the den — the fireplace shedding its last heat through the archway — so the porch was cool at this hour, cool enough to make the quilt the right place to be. Outside the glass the yard was still dark. A pale moon was coming up over the black line of the mountains — worth the chill, she had decided, worth being awake for — and the porch glass held all of it: the dark yard, the moon, her own face faintly doubled in it where the angle caught.

She was on the couch with him. He had a quilt pulled across the two of them, his own quilt, the one that lived on the back of the couch and smelled like him. Her legs were across his lap. His hand rested on her shin under the quilt, warm and rough, the steady weight of a man who was not in a hurry to be anywhere else.

She had been awake for an hour. He had been awake longer — she could tell from the second cup of coffee positioned on the low table for her, already cool enough to drink. She had reached for it without looking. He had not moved. The reaching and the not-moving were the small choreography of a morning they had done several times now.

She had been thinking about a sentence.

She had been thinking about it for weeks. It had assembled itself in her piece by piece — first as a feeling she could not say, then as the shape of an image, then as the actual words. She had run the words in her head several times, in different orders. She had imagined saying them out loud and felt the heat come up the back of her neck at the imagining. She had not said them. *I love you* was the thing she actually meant, and *I love you* was the thing she could not say, because he had not said it, and the rule was that he said it first, and she did not know the rule was a rule until she had bumped against it from the inside, and now she knew. She would not say *I love you* into the open air ahead of him. She could not. The not-saying had its own gravity and the gravity was, by now, part of her shape.

But the sentence was a different thing. The sentence was something she had built up *around* the words she could not say, a structure she could push into the air that would carry what the words meant without being the words. She had checked it carefully. It was warm and true and it was not *I love you* — not in any shape he could take it for. It was just a thing she had thought of. It was just a way of saying *I am grateful,* which she had said before, which the rules permitted. It was just — slightly larger.

She had decided, sometime in the last week, that she was going to say it.

The moon had cleared the mountains by another fraction. His hand was on her shin under the quilt; the cup was warm against her palm. She set the cup down on the low table and she did not pick it up again.

"I want to tell you something," she said.

He turned his head a little. He did not move otherwise. "Okay."

She had thought she would have to find the sentence in the moment, and she found instead that the words were already there in her mouth where she had been keeping them. She had been carrying the sentence assembled for long enough that it came out the way a poem comes out when you have memorized it — whole, with the rhythm she had given it, finished before she had fully decided to start.

"I had been eating crackers my whole life," she said. "And you fed me a meal. I didn't know what a meal was until you fed me one. And now I know."

She had not breathed since she had started. She breathed now, once, and she did not look at him while she did it. She looked at the moon.

There was a silence. Not long. She felt it through the quilt — his stillness for one beat that was a beat longer than his stillness had been the moment before. His hand on her shin had stopped, and then it pressed once, a small held press, and then it stopped again, as if he had begun two different responses with his hand and put both down before either had finished. She heard him take a breath. He took a second breath. She had not heard him do that before.

Then he made the small sound that he made, the small *mm* that he used when he was registering something, and his hand on her shin began to move again, the intentional press of his palm against her leg, and he said:

"I love how good we are together."

A beat. He was finding the next sentence; she could feel him finding it, the small specific quality of him assembling something he meant rather than producing something easy.

"I never had this either," he said. "What you do — what your body does — I've never. I'm glad you feel it too."

He pulled her a little closer under the quilt — not casually, but the way a man rearranges himself when he wants you against him for a particular reason, the adjustment of his arm around her shoulder, the deliberateness of being held. He kissed the top of her head. He held the kiss there for a beat that was longer than his usual kisses, his mouth not moving, his breath warm against her hair, and then he settled his cheek against her crown and stayed there. The warmth of his mouth and then of his cheek against her hair was what she had come to expect from him in these mornings, and she received it, and her body knew what to do with being held this way, and she leaned her cheek against his shoulder and closed her eyes for a moment and let the warmth do what warmth does.

And underneath the warmth — underneath what her body was doing with the warmth — something else was beginning to move.

She did not know what it was. She did not know yet. She would have to think about it. She felt it as an adjustment somewhere behind her breastbone, a small *wait*, the kind her body produced before her mind had caught up with what her body was already registering. She kept her eyes closed. She kept her cheek against his shoulder. She thought, in the way you think when you are not yet thinking but only listening to your body, *what?* And then she thought, *what did he—* and then she stopped thinking because his hand on her shin had begun to move in the slow way it moved when he was settling further into a long quiet morning with her, and his other arm came around her, and she let herself be drawn into him, and the small *wait* behind her breastbone went into the body where small *waits* go when the quilt is warm and the man is holding you and the sun has begun to come up.

She did not say anything else.

He did not say anything else, either. He held her. The quilt was warm. The sky over the mountains had begun to go pale at its lowest edge, and the moon was thinning into it. They sat there until the pale became pink and the pink became gold on the floor of the porch.

---

Later — after he had moved them inside, after the coffee had been refreshed, after the morning had become a morning — she would try to think about what had happened, and she would not quite be able to. She had told him something. He had told her something back. He had pulled her close and kissed her hair. The morning had continued. She had been received. *She had been received warmly,* she would remind herself, in the small private way you remind yourself of evidence whose weight you are not sure of.

And yet.

Two days later, in the dorm laundry room with her clothes going around in the dryer, his sentence came back to her. *I love how good we are together.* Her body did a small thing it did not have a name for, before she had decided to think about anything at all.

She sat on the folding table and ran it again. Her sentence, then his. Hers had been about — she had been trying to say. And his had been about. The two things had not been the same shape. She let the *not the same shape* form in her and then she did not finish the thought, because either end of it hurt. *Maybe he understood and chose to say it like that. Maybe it had gone past him entirely.* She could not test it. Testing it meant saying the words, and the words went through him first.

What she landed on, somewhere in the last minutes of the cycle, was not a conclusion. It was: *I do not know what just happened.* She turned that over too. It did not resolve. It was not going to resolve, because the one person who could resolve it was the one person she could not ask.

She was not going to bring it up. She knew that about herself before the dryer stopped, and she did not argue with it. She loved him. She was not going to say *I love you*, and he was not going to say *I love you*, and the not-saying had a particular shape pushed into it now that the rest of the not-saying did not have. The shape did not have a name. *She had said her best sentence. He had not said the sentence back.*

She put it where she put things she did not know what to do with. The place was not empty. There were other things in it already — small flickers, moments across the fall when what she knew about him had had to do small repair work to keep functioning. This joined them. She did not look at the pile. The dryer stopped. She folded what was hers and carried it back down the hall.

She would not bring the sentence back. She had said it once, in the pre-dawn chill of his porch, under his quilt, with her cheek against his shoulder and the sun coming up, and the saying had been her one attempt. He had not given the answer. The sentence retired into her body where she had been carrying it before. The cracker-and-meal would not return to her speech.
