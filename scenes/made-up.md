# Made-Up

*Vee's POV; an evening at Pace's house, late October — the second night of the weekend.*

---

No one was coming to the door. The road had no children on it, no second porch in walking distance, no bowl of anything waiting by the knob; the thirty-first was going to pass the house by. In town it would be wall to wall by now — girls in cat ears, boys in whatever had been within reach at nine o'clock, everyone out being somebody else on the one night a year nobody had to explain it.

Vee stood at the kitchen window drying the big pan while the last light went out of the field across the road, and the dress was in her car.

Wednesday night she'd worn it home with the ceremony still on her skin, and zipped it into its bag in her closet before bed. Friday, on her way out to his house, she'd lifted the bag off the rail and laid it across the back seat. She'd brought no overnight bag, nothing for a night she wasn't letting herself plan on — and she'd brought the dress. It was out there now, in the cold and the dark, thirty feet from the man who'd made it, and he had never once seen it on her.

The heat came up in her chest before she'd finished the thought, the same low certainty that always got there ahead of her, and by the time her head caught up her mouth had already decided.

She hung the towel on its bar and walked into the den, where he was long on the couch with a book and something low turning on the record player, guitars under a woman's voice, the lamp throwing its circle and leaving the book wall dark.

"I've got two costumes to show you," she said.

He looked up. "Costumes."

"It's Halloween. Tonight they're costumes."

He put the book face-down on his chest and gave her his whole attention, which with him was a physical event, like a door coming open. "Do I get to guess?"

"No."

"Do I get to help?"

"No." She was already at the front door, stepping into his boots. "You get to sit there."

The cold outside was clean and enormous, the sky rinsed black over the field, and she clomped to the car in his boots, loose as rowboats, loud in all that quiet. She brought the bag in against her body like something sleeping. He hadn't moved from the couch, and he didn't ask, and she loved that he didn't ask — she carried it past him down the hall to the bathroom, and behind her he reached over and turned the lamp by its little switch, one click brighter.

"Take your time," he called, once, through the house. Then nothing. No footsteps in the hall, no voice at the door. The house went back to the record and let her alone with the mirror and the hook on the back of the door and the zipper's long tearing whisper.

Her heels were already here. They'd been here since Friday night, sitting together by the bedroom wall where she'd finally been let out of them, and she'd fetched them down the hall before her nerve could hear about it. She undressed to nothing, because she had to. The dress had settled that question a long time ago, in the mirror on his sun porch: the slit gave up the edge of anything, so it was silk or it was nothing, and she had wanted to wear the dress. She brought it down over her head and it came down her like it remembered her, cool for half a second and then hers, and she worked it settled with both hands and bent to the small buckles at her ankles and stood up four inches taller into the long line the two of them made.

The woman in the mirror over the sink was cut off at the collarbone. Fine. A roomful of strangers had already been given the rest to guess at. This was the one viewing that mattered, and it was ten steps down a hall.

She opened the door.

He sat up at the sound of it, and she came down the hall into the lamplight the careful way first — the small steps, the slit lying shut, the dress just a dress, deep red going bottomless where the light moved on it. It landed on him: he came up off the couch without seeming to decide to, slow, the book sliding somewhere and staying there.

"I know this dress," he said. "We've never met."

"You missed the debut. This is the walk the room got." And she crossed the den to the dark end and turned, and came back toward him the other way — the long stride, the one she'd given the induction on purpose, the slit falling open off her bare leg to the hip with every step, given and taken back and given. Three steps of it, four. His face did what the room's murmur had done, except silently, and to her it was louder.

He didn't touch her. His hand came partway up and he put it away again, and the putting-away cost him something she could see, and standing there with her heart going she understood that she had gotten this exactly right.

"That's costume one," she said, while she still could.

"There's a better one?"

"Different." She was gone back down the hall before he could raise an objection.

Behind the door she stepped out of the dress and hung it — properly, seams squared on the hanger, a thing somebody's hands were in — and did not let herself stand there breathing. The heels stayed on. That was all of it: the heels stayed on. Her face in the little mirror had gone pink down to where the mirror gave out, and the pink was not stopping there, and knowing that, and going anyway, was the entire costume.

She opened the door.

The hall took longer this time. Ten steps in four-inch heels and nothing else, lamplight standing at the end of it, the house's warmth moving over every part of her at once so that there was no forgetting, anywhere, what she wasn't wearing. He'd stood to meet the dress. He was still standing.

She stopped inside the lamp's circle and stood as the dress had taught her, weight settled forward, the long line finishing itself.

"And this is what I had on under it," she said.

For a moment the record was the only voice in the house. His eyes went down her slow and came back up slower, no part of it hidden from her, his breath moved once where she could see it — and he stayed where he was and let the looking be the touch, a man being handed something and taking his time about the taking.

"There she is," he said.

She stood in his light and let him look, and the blush went down and down, and she did not cover one inch of it.
