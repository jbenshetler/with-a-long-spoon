*Vee POV — Sunday through Tuesday, the days after the mixer; Pace's house, the dorm, the anthropology building. Present: Pace (Sunday), Cassie (asleep, Monday night), Randi (Tuesday).*

# Coming Due

Sunday she didn't leave until the light was going. It should have worn her out and it didn't. There was always a lot of it with Pace — she'd stopped keeping the count that used to scandalize her back in the fall — but Sunday ran past even that, and twice, which she almost never did, she was the one who started it: reaching for him in the gray before he was properly awake, and again in the afternoon, taking a book out of his hands. Each time something in her let go for as long as it lasted, and then, after, took itself back up. She put it down to the weekend, to greed, to the good long unhurried plenty of him. She drove home in the early dark full of him and not, somehow, filled.

Monday was worse for having nothing in it. No class with Randi, a problem set, the dining hall with Cassie — and under all of it, all day, the wanting the weekend had set loose and not put away, low and constant, and a worry wound so tightly through it the two came as one thing. It was still there at lights-out. She lay in the top bunk with the window's thin cold coming in and Cassie's breath going slow beneath her, wide awake and wired, her body asking plainly for the one thing that would quiet it for a time.

She kept her hand on her stomach. Flat, still, an easy reach from where it wanted to be, and she would not let it go lower — not for any rule she could have named, there was no rule, it was hers and nobody's business but her own — but because tonight she wasn't sure what she'd be doing it to, or whose it would be by the end. She remembered the night of the mixer, her own hand, what her mind had reached for the second she stopped watching it. So she didn't stop watching it. She lay with her hand flat on her own stomach like a lid held down on a pot, and looked at the rough ceiling a couple of feet above her face, and waited to be tired enough that the wanting would let her go — and it took a long time, and it hadn't quite let go when she finally did.

By Tuesday she wanted her head cleared, and at four the pool gave it to her — for an hour, anyway: the black line and her breath and the water over the back of her neck, her head going quiet, the whole tangle of the weekend left up on the deck with her towel. She climbed out clear. It wouldn't keep.

The clear lasted as far as the closet. Dressing for a night with Randi at the end of it was usually the best of the day — its own keyed-up private gladness, the good sweater, the skirt that ran the arithmetic in her favor, deciding what Randi would get to see across a table and then getting to watch her see it. Tonight the gladness wouldn't come. What came instead was Tuesday itself — seven o'clock, the saved seat, an hour beside Randi — arriving all at once while she stood at the rail with a hanger in each hand, and her hands stopped being any use. She got the plum sweater halfway on and stood there with her arms caught in it, unable to say whether she was dressing to be looked at or dressing to be covered up, and the not-knowing was worse than either answer, so she pulled it on and left the question hanging and went.

She was late. A little late, which she never was — most nights she got there early on purpose, for the few minutes with Randi before class, though early never once got her there first: Randi was always ahead of her, however early she came. Tonight she'd cut it close, on purpose, and Randi was already in the seat she always took, back third, on the aisle, her coat folded over the chair beside her, holding it. She found Vee at once — she always found her at once — and the smile came up before it had anything arranged on it, and she lifted the coat off the saved chair and patted the seat.

"Hey, gorgeous."

"Hey, you." Vee dropped into it and got her own coat open, and Randi's hand came over and squeezed hers, once, and let go, and her knee came to rest against Vee's and stayed. The ordinary nothing of a shared armrest — except that nothing about it stayed nothing tonight. She was aware of Randi's knee against her own, acutely, the whole hour, and of holding her own leg still against it, and of what the holding-still was costing her. Randi smelled the same. That was somehow the worst of it: that Randi was in every particular exactly herself, right down to the uncapped pen at the ready, and nothing about her had changed and all of it landed different.

Marsh came in with the canvas bag and set something loose in the room, the same as every night — reciprocity, tonight, the three obligations, to give and to take and to pay back, some cold island where a man could beggar his whole house handing things away because a gift was a debt you laid on someone whether they wanted it or not. Other nights Vee would have gone right down into it and lost the hour. Tonight it slid off her. She caught a phrase and lost the next one, like a station going out of range on a county road, and gave it up.

Somewhere in there Randi leaned an inch and murmured, did she have a Lifesaver — and Vee came back to the room to find she'd never gotten them out. She always got them out; that was hers to do now, had been since a sidewalk a week or two back, the roll wintergreen and gone soft in her coat pocket, one lecture one flavor. Tonight she'd forgotten it clean. She dug it out and thumbed one loose.

Randi's fingers found the inside of Vee's wrist on the way to taking it — a graze, nothing, the same as any of the few classes they'd been doing this — and it went through Vee low and complete. The cool of the wintergreen, and under the cool the warm drag of a fingertip crossing her pulse, and her pulse leaping up under it to be found. She nearly dropped the roll. She shut her hand around it and faced the front and did not take in one more word Marsh said. *Static,* she told herself. The skin was thin there, everyone's was, it meant nothing. She held very still, and it went on not being static.

At 8:20 Marsh turned them loose, and in the stand-and-gather of the whole room Randi wound her scarf around her throat and said it, glad, easy, her eyes on the knot.

"Having you at the dance was so great. I've wanted to do that for the longest time."

It reached Vee a half-beat late, the same lag it had had in his bed — the word arriving after the woman who'd said it had already moved on to her coat. *Having.* And behind the word, riding in on it, the thing it carried: that she was something Randi got to have. Had wanted to have, and for a long time. In his bed there had been him for it — the whole solid fact of him under her to pour the heat into, to set it against and call it his. Here there was a scarf being knotted and a bright emptying hall and a woman smiling at her over nothing she couldn't have defended out loud to a priest. Her body knew exactly where the heat wanted to go. That was the whole of it — that it knew, and that she would not have it. The heat came up anyway; she had no say in it.

The heat she could wait out; heat passed. But the heat was only the near edge of the thing. It had cracked open a door, and behind the door stood something larger than heat and more durable, that half of her strained toward, wanting to see it, while the other half would not look. What she felt of it was only apprehension: of something that had already happened and hadn't yet shown its size, a thing that reached, in that one word, everything she had. She was on her feet before she'd decided to be, because she wasn't going to be able to sit across a table from this tonight and hold still under it. She knew it the way you feel a fever coming on — the body certain of it before the head got the news.

They came out into the corridor with everyone else, floor wax and radiator heat, and this was the part where they walked the three blocks to the place with the loft and the green water climbing the jukebox and the bottle of red, and gave the little room two hours and shut it down at ten. Randi was already angling them toward the doors. Vee heard her own voice begin taking it apart — she was behind on the Marsh reading, her head was doing something, she thought she might be coming down with — and none of it came out the size of a reason, because there wasn't one she could hand across. She braced for Randi to push.

Randi didn't push. "God, no, don't — I'm wrecked anyway, honestly." She had Thursday hanging over her, she said, and made a martyr's face about it: the standards board, three hours in a folding chair while they came in one at a time to answer for what they'd gotten up to and the board decided what it came to. "Pray for me." Vee said something back, the right something, she was fairly sure.

And then, coats on, the doors right there — her mouth got there ahead of her.

"Brunch?"

Out before she'd cleared it with anyone. Before the part of her that had spent the entire day backing away from this woman could reach the front and stop it, the want had leaned past it and reached — toward, this time, not away — and set the question in the air between them.

"Absolutely." Randi lit right up, no daylight at all between the ask and the yes. "Saturday. God, yes."

So it was set. She'd come to dodge the thing and had stood in a doorway and made herself an appointment to have it instead — four days out, and nothing bought with the four days, no relief in them anywhere she could find.

Out through the doors, and then the goodbye — which had never once needed getting through, not until tonight, because it had never once had any friction in it. The hug, and then the mouth, soft, the small second press: the thing two friends did at the end of a good night, friends who'd miss each other and were already glad of the next. For Randi it was still only that — Vee could see it, no hitch in her anywhere, natural as the light going at the end of a day, already reaching. Dozens of those kisses by now, and Vee had spent all the months of them not counting, calling them French when she thought of them at all. She was counting now. Saturday had set her counting — Saturday, and then the small hours of Sunday spent explaining Saturday to Pace from up on top of him — and somewhere in there every easy goodbye she'd ever taken from that mouth had been called up for review, and she could no longer say what they had been. What she had let them be. What, if she was honest, some part of her had wanted them to be all along.

Randi hugged her first, the usual, wool and cold and bitter-orange, and the second her arms came around her the wanting stood straight up in Vee, full-length and shameless — the mouth, the press, wanted so nakedly that the wanting frightened her worse than the kiss could. Because she couldn't say what taking it now would make it. On the floor it had been a show; Randi had said so, a bit for a boy, *I almost believed it.* If this was that same soft nothing, reaching for it was mad; and if it wasn't, that was worse, and she couldn't follow it any further than that. She didn't know what a kiss would be to Randi. She was more afraid of what it would be to herself.

So at the end of the hug, when Randi's face came in — no hesitation in it, only the old ease — Vee gave her a few degrees of turn, the smallest thing, half an accident, and the kiss meant for her mouth landed at the corner of it and slid off onto her cheek. A miss got up to look like an aim. It was over before either of them could say what it had almost been.

"Walk fast," Randi said, only a shade late, folding it back to lightness. "It's arctic." And she went, quick heels across the dark to the green car, and lifted a hand without turning around.

Vee turned the other way into the wind. She'd half thought the walk would put the heat out; it didn't. It banked, and settled in low, and went on simmering, unaddressed — nothing done about it, nothing decided. And the heat was the least of it, besides; the heat she could have waited out. Under it was the want, greater and more durable, and it had the apprehension by the hand. The two of them walked her home. It wanted a name. She wouldn't give it one — not on the walk, not yet.
