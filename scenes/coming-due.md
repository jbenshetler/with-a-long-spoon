*Vee POV — Sunday through Tuesday, the days after the mixer; Pace's house, the dorm, the anthropology building. Present: Pace (Sunday), Cassie (asleep Monday night; awake Tuesday night), Randi (Tuesday).*

# Coming Due

Sunday she didn't leave until the light was going. It should have worn her out and it didn't. There was always a lot of it with Pace — she'd stopped keeping the count that used to scandalize her back in the fall, the one she'd secretly gloated over — but Sunday ran past even that, and twice, which she almost never did, she was the one who started it: reaching for him in the gray before he was properly awake, and again in the afternoon, taking the book he was reading to her out of his hands. Each time something in her let go for as long as it lasted, and then, after, took itself back up. She put it down to the weekend, to greed, to the good long unhurried plenty of him. She drove home in the early dark full of him and not, somehow, filled.

Monday was worse for having nothing in it. No class with Randi, just a problem set, the dining hall with Cassie — and under all of it, all day, the wanting the weekend had set loose, low and constant, and a worry wound so smoothly through it the two came as one thing. It was still there at lights-out. She lay in the top bunk with the window's thin cold coming in and Cassie's breath going slow beneath her, wide awake and wired, her body asking plainly for the one thing that would quiet it for a time.

She kept her hand on her stomach. Flat, still, an easy reach from where it wanted to be, and she would not let it go lower — not because anyone had forbidden it; if anything the message ran the other way, and Pace had made a point of telling her so, in the dark, in the way that stuck — but because she knew exactly where her mind would go the second she stopped minding it, and she was not going to hand it the room. She'd learned that the night of the dance — undressing in the cold, her hand coming onto herself half by accident, and the name that arrived with it before she could head it off. She'd taken the hand back that night like something caught stealing, and driven the county road to Pace to have it put out. It hadn't gone out — not even with him, not even after. Keyed up, she'd told herself. Sex-drunk. Needing him and getting her wires crossed. In the fall the excuses had held. Tonight they went through her hands like water, and there was no driving this one off. So she kept the hand where it was, a lid held down on a pot, and stared at the rough plaster a couple of feet above her face, and waited to be tired enough that the wanting would let her go — because as long as she didn't give it the thing that would make it true, it could stay only wanting. It took a long time, and it hadn't quite let go when she finally did.

By Tuesday she wanted her head cleared, and at four the pool gave it to her: the black line and her breath and the water over the back of her neck, her head going quiet, the whole tangle of the weekend left up on the deck with her towel. She climbed out clear.

The clear lasted as far as the closet. Dressing for a night with Randi at the end of it was usually the best of the day — its own keyed-up private gladness, the good sweater, the skirt that ran the arithmetic in her favor, deciding what Randi would get to see across a table and then getting to watch her see it. Tonight the gladness wouldn't come. What came instead was Tuesday itself — seven o'clock, the saved seat, an hour beside Randi — arriving all at once while she stood at the rail with a hanger in each hand, and her hands stopped being any use. She got the plum sweater halfway on and stood there with her arms caught in it, unable to say whether she was dressing to be looked at or dressing to be covered up, and the not-knowing was worse than either answer, so she pulled it on and left the question hanging and went.

She was late. A little late, which she never was — most nights she got there early on purpose, for the few minutes with Randi before class, though early never once got her there first: Randi was always ahead of her, however early she came. Tonight she'd cut it close, on purpose, and Randi was already in the seat she always took, back third, on the aisle, her coat folded over the chair beside her, holding it. She found Vee at once — she always found her at once — and the smile came up before it had anything arranged on it, and she lifted the coat off the saved chair and patted the seat.

"Hey, gorgeous."

"Hey, you." Vee dropped into it and got her own coat open, and Randi's hand came over and squeezed hers, once, and let go, and her knee came to rest against Vee's and stayed. The ordinary nothing of a shared armrest — except that nothing about it stayed nothing tonight. She was aware of Randi's knee against her own, acutely, the whole hour, and of holding her own leg still against it, and of what the holding-still was costing her. Randi smelled the same. That was somehow the worst of it: that Randi was in every particular exactly herself, right down to the uncapped pen at the ready, and nothing about her had changed and all of it landed different.

Marsh came in with the canvas bag and set something loose in the room, the same as every night — reciprocity, tonight, the three obligations, to give and to take and to pay back, some cold island where a man could beggar his whole house handing things away because a gift was a debt you laid on someone whether they wanted it or not. Other nights Vee would have gone right down into it and lost the hour. Tonight it slid off her.

Somewhere in there Randi leaned in, her mouth at Vee's ear, and murmured — did she have a Lifesaver. Vee came back to the room to find she'd never gotten them out. She'd handed Randi one every class so far; tonight she'd forgotten clean. She dug the roll from her coat pocket, wintergreen, gone soft, and thumbed one loose.

Randi's fingers found the inside of Vee's wrist on the way to taking it — a graze, nothing, the same as any of the few classes they'd been doing this — and it went through Vee low and complete. The wintergreen going between them, and the warm drag of a fingertip crossing her pulse, and her pulse leaping up under it to be found. She nearly dropped the roll. She shut her hand around it and faced the front and did not take in one more word Marsh said. *Static,* she told herself. The skin was thin there, everyone's was, it meant nothing. She held very still, and it went on not being static.

After that she only watched the clock. She'd been dreading this ending for days, and now she got to see it come — the minutes climbing toward the one thing she hadn't solved: the hug and the kiss at the end of the night, waiting for her whichever way she went. Sit the dinner — two hours across from Randi, and how was she going to do that and keep her face together? — and it would be there when she stood up to go. Beg off, and it only came sooner. Randi's arms, Randi's mouth, the soft second press that ended every good night between them: she wanted it so badly that the wanting was the very reason she couldn't let herself take it.

At 8:20 Marsh let them go. Randi bent for her bag, and Vee's eyes went where they shouldn't — to the hollow where Randi's neck turned down into her shoulder, the soft place she'd put her mouth to twice now, both times before she'd decided to — and the guilt of it turned over in her, and she had not got her balance back when Randi said it.

"Having you at the dance was so great. I've wanted to do that for the longest time."

It reached Vee a half-beat late, the same lag it had had in his bed — the word arriving after the woman who'd said it had already moved on to her coat. *Having.* And behind the word, riding in on it, the thing it carried: that she was something Randi got to have. Had wanted to have, and for a long time. In his bed there had been him for it — the whole solid fact of him under her to pour the heat into, to set it against and call it his.

Here there was no him. Here there was Randi — her best friend, the one she told everything to — shrugging into her coat, smiling at her across the class notes and the soft roll of Lifesavers, over nothing anyone could have taken to a priest, and the heat came up for her all the same. That was the part Vee couldn't get to sit still: that it was Randi, that it was her *friend*, and that her body wanted her anyway, with nowhere to put it and no one to charge it to. She had no say in it.

The heat she could wait out; heat passed. But the heat was only the near edge of the thing. Saturday's joke had cracked open a door, and behind the door stood something larger than heat and more durable, that half of her strained toward, wanting to see it, while the other half would not look. What she felt of it was only apprehension: of something that had already happened and hadn't yet shown its size, a thing that reached, in that one word, everything she had.

She could not sit across a table from this tonight and hold still under it — not two hours of it — and she knew that the way you know a fever coming: not here yet, and already past arguing with. She got up to go.

Then they were up and out with the rest of them, and she went where Randi went. Randi was talking — the small easy after-class things, Marsh, the cold, Thursday — and Vee heard the sound of it and took in none of it, her eyes unfocused a little past Randi's shoulder, her head still full of the one word. She nodded where a nod seemed wanted, with no idea what she was agreeing to.

Out in the corridor, floor wax and radiator heat, this was the part where they walked in the cold to the welcoming place with the table and a bottle of red, their loft full of hushed secrets and laughter. Randi was already angling them toward the doors. Vee heard her own voice begin taking it apart — she was behind on the Marsh reading, her head was doing something, she thought she might be coming down with — and none of it came out the size of a reason, because there wasn't one she could hand across.

Randi didn't take it. "Oh, sweetie, no." Warm, sorry for her, already past the refusal and on to the fix. "Well, then I'll walk you to your dorm. We can chat, and I'll make sure you get home safe." And there was no saying no to that. She'd meant to buy her way out of Randi for the night; somehow she'd bought more.

So they walked it. The cold was clean and hard and Randi kept close against it, talking the easy stream of nothing Vee couldn't have repeated a word of, and Vee got herself through it — answering where an answer seemed wanted, her whole mind bent on the one thing waiting at the end, the thing she had to get through and not take.

At the door Randi pulled her into the hug, the ordinary one, and Vee went into it and let it be good. It was good; it was always good, the warm fit of her, the give. She stood there loving it — and, this time, feeling plainly, right alongside the loving, the other thing the hug was made of, the thing she'd spent the whole day outrunning, wide awake in her now with Randi's arms around her and no pretending left that she didn't feel it.

When Randi's face came in for the kiss, Vee turned her mouth away. Not an accident got up to look like one. She turned it, and took the miss on purpose.

Randi drew back an inch, reading her. "What's up?"

"I don't want you to get what I'm coming down with." It came out ready; she'd built it somewhere on the walk without knowing she was building it.

"Ugh." Randi huffed a laugh and let it go. "Keep your plague, then." But she didn't step out of the hug, and still holding her, she sighed it against Vee's hair: "I'll miss you Thursday, though. After class. Standards committee—" a martyr's groan "—I've got to go discipline a bad girl."

Vee turned her face away — some reflex, a looking-off at nothing — and that was when Randi's mouth found the side of her neck, soft, a wet bloom of heat right where the neck goes down into the shoulder, the exact place she'd put her own mouth twice and been told not to weaponize. The goosebumps went out over her in a sheet. A sound got out of her before she could catch it, and she came off the lips and out of the hug all at once.

Randi laughed, delighted, not sorry at all. "See?" she said. "How do you like it?"

Vee stood there with her heart going and the whole side of her neck ringing where the mouth had been, and out of that — out of the ringing, not out of any part of her that was thinking — came her own voice.

"Brunch." A beat, while the rest of her caught up. "Let's — let's have brunch this weekend."

Randi lit up like she'd been handed a present. "Yes. God, yes. Saturday."

Then she was going — a wave up over her shoulder, quick heels into the dark — and Vee still had the stairs to climb and the door to get through, and she climbed them with the neck still live and the want walking up beside her and the apprehension holding its hand, and not one name she'd give to any of it.

Cassie was on the lower bunk with a book against her knees, and she'd not been there long — her coat still on, the cold still coming off her. She looked up and took the measure of Vee's face for a second.

"What was that with you and Randi?" she said. Not the flat way she said most true things — there was a worry under it, and a thread of disbelief she didn't trouble to hide.

Vee opened her mouth for the easy thing — sorority stuff, you know how she is — and nothing came up to meet it, because she didn't have it. She didn't know. That was the whole of the trouble, standing there in her coat in the doorway: her oldest friend had asked her the plainest question there was, and she could not answer it.
