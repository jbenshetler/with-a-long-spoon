# Among Friends

The kitchen was too small for two people to be useful in. She'd tried, the first weeks, standing to dry while he washed, and they'd bumped hips and elbows until he took the towel out of her hand and pointed her at the little table, and that was the arrangement now and she'd stopped pretending she minded. He cooked, they ate at the table because he sat people down to eat, and then he stood at the sink with his sleeves rolled and she sat with what was left of her wine and talked at his back. His back was a good listener. All of him was, but the back made it easy, made her run on longer than she meant to, and tonight she'd been running on about Randi for a while.

"—so there's no receipt, obviously, there was never going to be a receipt, and the guy at the counter is looking at her over the sweater like he's already said no. And she doesn't argue. That's the thing. She just keeps on being delighted with him. Like he's the best part of her whole errand. And I watched him fold." Vee turned her glass a half-turn. "Store credit and a bag. She let him feel like it was his idea."

"Mm." He set a pan in the rack.

"You'd have hated every second of it. There was a line."

"I'd have kept the sweater."

"You'd have worn the sweater out of spite. It was lavender."

He didn't turn around, but she could hear the smile get into his shoulders. He worked the towel around the inside of a pot, hung it, and said, in the same voice he'd have used for a grocery list, "I want to meet your people."

Vee sat up a little.

"The ones you tell me about," he said.

It arrived as *Randi*. Of course she did — she'd been saying Randi's name for ten minutes, Randi was the one she told, and there it was, arriving like a gift with her friend's name already on it. Something went up in her like the good windows in the athletic dining hall, all that gold at once.

"You could meet Randi," she said. "Properly, I mean. You two said about nine words at the coffee shop and most of them were math." She was already building it — a dinner, somewhere with real napkins, her two favorite people at one small table finally getting to find out about each other. "We could do a dinner. The three of us."

"Bring Cassie, too," he said, into the sink.

He said it with a plate in his hand, no more weight on it than the plate had, and it landed in the middle of her little dinner and sat there. Cassie. Vee opened her mouth to say *of course* and didn't. Cassie would come — that was the thing. For Vee she'd come, cap and all, and be a good sport the whole evening. And Vee ran the dinner forward and couldn't find the restaurant to put it in. The real-napkins kind of place, Randi's kind, would seat Cassie in yesterday's shirt under a pendant light, being fine about it for two hours. Anywhere Cassie would pick, Vee couldn't picture Randi's hands on the menu. She went through every table in town, and every one of them had somebody at it being a good sport, and she wasn't going to build a dinner one of her people had to be a good sport at.

She'd watched somebody be fine about it before. She wasn't going to look at when.

"Okay, so not a dinner," she said slowly, thinking with her mouth open, her one method for anything worth planning. "Cassie doesn't — a fancy dinner isn't Cassie's."

"And Meg," Pace said. He was scrubbing something now, in no rush about it. "The one whose boots you wore up the mountain."

Vee stopped with her glass in the air.

She'd said that once. Maybe once. Somewhere on the trail in October with her thighs burning, a half sentence about the boots being Meg's and a half size big, the kind of thing you say to say something, and it had gone wherever those sentences go. Except it hadn't. It had gone home with him and been put somewhere, apparently, on a shelf with her friends' names on it, and he'd just taken it down one-handed without turning around.

"You remember that?" she said.

"Your heel came up out of the right one when you pushed off." He rinsed. "And Kayla's the one who can talk the paint off a wall?"

That was hers. That was her own line, she'd said it about Kayla probably four times, and hearing it handed back in his flat certain voice did something to her that she would have been embarrassed to describe out loud. He had been listening. Not the polite kind, where a man keeps his face pointed at you while he rests. All fall, all those nights she'd run on at his back about her floor, her people, the small dumb news of her life — he'd kept it. All of it.

"She is," Vee said. "She'd talk the wall's paint into telling her things."

"Then it can't be a dinner table," he said. "Tables have ends. Somebody ends up at one."

And the whole thing turned over at once, the small dinner opening out into something with everybody in it — Kayla and Meg, Meg's Danny, Cassie planted happily at the edge of it with her arms crossed, Randi in the middle of them all, and Pace. Her worlds, in one room, at the same time. The laugh started up out of her before she knew it was coming.

"All of them," she said. "You'd do that? That's — you understand what you're volunteering for. Kayla alone is a lot of person."

"I'd like it."

"And Theo, maybe. He's a friend from my English class last year, he—" and the rest of it came out over the little ledge before she could decide about it. "He asked me out once. Last spring. It's fine, it's nothing, we never even—" She stopped there, not sure why she was explaining a thing nobody had asked her to explain.

"Bring Theo," Pace said, and reached for the next pan.

That was all. He never looked around, never asked the question she'd braced for, and she sat there feeling something she couldn't have named settle in her, warm side down.

She was deep into the roster — who'd come, who'd bring whom, whether Kayla could be trusted around the story of the shirt — when the other thing surfaced. It came up as a picture, whole: a game running on three walls and a fourth screen over the bar, the sound pooling in the middle of the room, and Pace across the nachos with his fork in his hand, squaring it to the edge of the table, turning it, squaring it again. *I listen as hard as I know how and I still can't hear you.* The picture didn't want anything from her. It just stood there until she'd seen it.

"Will you pick the place?" she said. "You know the area. I'd just be guessing off which sign looks friendliest."

The water shut off. He dried his hands on the towel, taking his time about it, and when he turned around at last he was leaning back on the counter looking at her, and there was something in his face she'd have called happiness if he'd let it any further out.

"Let me handle it," he said.

---

The place was twenty minutes out on a road she'd never had a reason to take, and from the parking lot it was nothing — blacktop, a low long building gone the color that wood goes when nobody paints it, a sign she couldn't read until they were nearly under it. She had the address memorized from handing it out all week. *Where even is that,* Kayla had said, thrilled, and Vee had said, *no idea, Pace picked it,* and defended the place like a lawyer, sight unseen, on no evidence at all. They'd come out half an hour ahead of everybody, because it was theirs — his name on the reservation, her people driving twenty minutes into the dark on their say-so — and the lot was thin — a few trucks scattered toward the far end, and one car parked square to the door, and Vee knew it before she was done looking at it. Twenty minutes out, on a road with no reason on it, and Randi had found the place first.

Inside, it was pine. Every wall of it, boards gone a deep honey color that fifty years of other people's good nights had rubbed matte, and the whole room was warm with it, warmer than paneling had any right to be. A tile floor, boot heels and chair legs on it, a speaker somewhere up in a corner playing somebody's steel guitar at the volume of a conversation. No screens — a fact that went by like a room's ceiling height, half a second, gone. Along the back wall a buffet ran under heat lamps — brisket, chicken, corn, green beans, slaw, a vat of mashed potatoes — and to the side, three round tables had been pushed loosely together, and on one of them, tented, a folded sheet of white butcher paper said VEE — three letters, black marker, in a handwriting she'd never seen in her life.

And behind the sign, already on her feet, was Randi. Of course she was. She beat everyone to Vee's things, always had, arrived at them first and fully, a standard other people's RSVPs could only wave at — and here she was, first even ahead of the hosts, at the chair that faced the door, a cold beer already sweating in front of her like she'd been drinking beer all her life, and she'd gotten the room almost exactly right — a chambray shirt with the sleeves cuffed back, the blue doing what blue always did to her eyes, slacks, and then the shoes, little heels a full register too nice for the tile, because there was a floor below which Randi's closet did not go. A few tables across the room were at their dinners and their beers, and none of them looked up, and Vee laughed out loud anyway.

"You're impossible. We came early on *purpose*."

"You did. It didn't work." Randi came around the table already smiling.

Vee had thought she might have to do something tonight. Manage a seam somewhere. She'd carried the thought in with her like a folded map. And here was the room warm and easy, her name on butcher paper, her best friend first through a door she herself had just walked through — and the only thing left to manage was the thing she'd been saving, the thing she wanted done first, before a single coat came through that door.

"Come here. Come meet him. You've technically met him, but that was ninety seconds of standard deviation, it doesn't count." She was pulling her along, laughing. "Randi, this is Pace."

It came out short even as she said it. *This is Pace.* Like he was a lamp. There was more to say and she hadn't quite had the standing to say it, and in the little gap her sentence left, Pace put out his hand.

"Vee's boyfriend," he said.

He said it the plain way he said everything, a man supplying a fact, at a volume for three, and it went through Vee like the first swallow of something hot. There it was. Out loud, in a public room, in front of the one person she told everything to — a word he had never once needed anywhere, spent here, on her. Randi's hand closed hard around hers, hard enough to feel the bones, and Vee squeezed back just as hard, because of course — she got it, she was getting to stand right next to her best friend at the exact moment the best friend heard it confirmed, and if the grip was fierce, well, so was being happy for somebody.

"The boyfriend," Randi said, taking his hand, her voice all warm coin. "Finally. She hides you very well."

"Not anymore," he said, and pulled out her chair.

And that, Vee understood a little later, had been everything she'd braced for, done and dusted before a single guest arrived. The folded map went wherever unneeded things go. The night stood open in front of her with nothing left in it to manage.

Cassie's SUV announced itself from the lot, suspension first, and then the door brought them all in at once — Kayla, Meg, Danny, Cassie last with the keys, coats coming off into a pile, Kayla narrating the buffet to people standing next to the buffet.

"It says *Vee*," Kayla said, to the room, to everyone, already halfway to the brisket.

Theo came alone, ten minutes behind, with the entrance of a man who'd checked the address twice and sat in his car a minute, and by the time he was through the door the room had a running start on him.

They came to the tables in waves off the buffet, and Pace met each wave the same. Kayla arrived with a loaded plate and her free hand already out — "Vee's boyfriend," he said, shaking it. He said it to Meg, and to Danny at Meg's shoulder. He said it to Theo, flat and friendly, and to Cassie, who shook on it once, like closing a deal. The same two words every time, a man with exactly one credential and no plans to produce another, and by the fourth telling the tables were grinning ahead of it, waiting for it, and he never varied it and never once seemed to notice he'd become the night's running joke. Vee heard it every single time. It landed somewhere low every single time. At the buffet, alone with the brisket, she caught herself saying it under her breath, trying it on with her own mouth, and made herself stop before anybody saw.

Dinner organized itself around the buffet, which meant it never organized at all, and Vee watched it not organize with a pleasure she couldn't have explained. Nobody was seated. Everybody was seated, but a different everybody every twenty minutes — the buffet kept dealing them out and gathering them back in new arrangements, Kayla returning with a second pass at the brisket and a fresh audience, Meg and Danny splitting a plate of corn with the calm of the long-settled. And through it Pace moved. That was almost all he did, that she could ever have pointed to — he moved, between the tables, with a pitcher or without one, and wherever he stopped, something got easier.

The buffet dealt her out a while, and then it dealt her back in, and for a stretch she just sat — her plate in front of her, Pace on the far side of the table, and the room's currents washing people up to it. Danny arrived first, with brisket and the look of a man who'd been working up to something all night. He'd heard it through Meg, who could only have heard it through her: that Pace lifted. He said the word *powerlifting* like a man handling somebody else's tools, and asked what Pace's numbers were, and Pace said, "How's your bench progressing?"

Five words. Vee watched them work. Danny sat down like the chair had been pulled out for him and was off — the one-rep max in the fall, the plateau, the grip change that broke it, all of it delivered to a man who leaned in on his forearms and asked the next question, and the next, and never once produced a number of his own. By the time Danny got to creatine — lowering his voice, like it was a medical matter, which apparently it was — he'd been talking for ten minutes and would have sworn they'd had a conversation about lifting. She never did learn what Pace benched. Neither did Danny.

Theo washed up to the table on the next current, shoulders still up around his ears somewhere, and slotted in at the edge of the bench talk with the smile of a man auditing a class. Pace let a lull come, and then said, "Vee says you know jazz. Where does it start, for you?"

And Vee watched a key go into a lock. *For you* — the question had no wrong answer built into it, nothing to flunk. Theo looked at Pace like nobody had asked him that in the entire year since the class, which was probably true, and then his shoulders came down and his hands came up, and he was building it right there over the table — Dixieland with one hand and the blues with the other, bringing them together in the air, the collision that made the whole thing go. Danny asked something. Theo answered it with both hands. When Kayla hauled her off toward the corn a while later, the two of them were still at it, and Pace was leaned back out of the lamplight with his arms crossed, in it and not in it, a man watching two strangers he'd introduced to each other get along.

She caught pieces of the rest of it from her orbits. He asked Meg something about her boyfriend's roommate — he had the story's whole geography, the roommate, the incident, details Vee had forgotten telling anyone — and then he leaned back out of it and let Meg have the room, and Meg, fork in the air, letting every sentence finish, was funnier than Vee had ever managed to make her sound. He set them up. That was as close as Vee got to a word for it, watching from the slaw end of the buffet. He walked around handing people the first line and then he stood back and laughed at their second one, and everyone he left was standing a little taller than when he got there, and not one of them could have said why.

Kayla cornered her by the corn. "Okay, I get it," she said, low, urgent, as if reporting from a war. "The math thing had me worried, I'll be honest. But he asked me about my sister's wedding. *I* barely remember telling you about my sister's wedding."

"I remember you telling me," Vee said. "It took two hours."

"He asked about the *dress* part," Kayla said, and went back for a roll.

She was in earshot for exactly the start of the Cassie one. Pace took the chair next to her with a pitcher and said, "What do you give the Bricklayers this year?" — straight, no wind-up, the team's whole ridiculous name in his grocery-list voice, a name he could only have had from Vee — and Cassie's eyebrow came up under the cap and stayed there a second, recalibrating something. Then the buffet moved Vee along.

When she looked up next, from the far side of the room, Cassie was *talking*. Cap pushed back, Pace in the chair next to her with his forearms on his knees, and whole paragraphs coming out of her roommate. Vee couldn't hear a word and didn't need one, because she knew this story like a song from its first bar — the flat sweep of the hand for the roommate who couldn't get along with her, *with her*, a thing Cassie still relitigated like a bad call; the shrug for her own roommate, who'd quit school by October and left her with an empty bed; and then the cafeteria, the tray set down across from a stranger's tray, two problems solving each other over bad pizza. Cassie glanced over mid-story, found her looking, and didn't stop telling it. Her roommate, who had watched every man in Vee's vicinity for two years like a goalkeeper watching a corner kick, sat there in a roadhouse giving Vee's boyfriend the best of their two years — glowing, insofar as Cassie glowed, which was drily, at low wattage, and unmistakably — and Vee stood holding her sweet tea and felt something enormous and quiet go by underneath her, like a whale under a boat.

She didn't go over. She wanted to keep seeing it from exactly here.

The twinge came later, and it was nothing.

She was coming back from the buffet line and Pace was at Randi's elbow with the pitcher, saying something she couldn't hear, and Randi laughed — head tipping, the ponytail swinging with it — and for one bad half second something went through Vee with a stinger on it. It had a shape she knew from Christmas, from a phone that didn't ring and a kitchen she'd never seen: him, easy, somewhere she wasn't, being wonderful at a woman. Her hand was at her collarbone before she knew it had moved. And then the rest of the room caught up — this was *Randi*, this was her own doing, she had put these two people in one room herself and would do it again tomorrow — and the stinger backed out of it, and she carried her potatoes to the table and by the time she sat down she was smiling at them both. He was being good to her friends. That was the whole thing she'd wanted. It was going right, and apparently some corner of her didn't know how to watch a thing go right without checking it for the trapdoor.

Winter weeknights empty a room early, and this one obliged. It was a Wednesday and everybody's mornings started early, and by nine the tables had thinned to coats over chair backs. Kayla left in a flurry of hugs, having extracted from Pace an actual promise about the brisket place's cornbread. Theo shook Pace's hand and said something that made them both laugh and went out into the cold with Danny and Meg. Cassie went last of that wave. She'd pulled her cap down and had her keys already turning on one finger, and she stopped by Pace's chair on the exit line. A beat, the eyebrow at half. "You can stay," she told him, like the results of an audit, and pushed out the door with her keys still spinning, and Vee watched the taillights of the SUV bounce out of the lot on the tired suspension and understood she had just witnessed a knighting.

And then it was three of them, at one round table, in the warm pine quiet. The buffet lamps were off at the far end. Somebody had turned the speaker down another notch, or the room had just gone quiet enough to make it seem so, the steel guitar walking along under the last of the conversation. Randi had her elbows on the table and her chin on her laced fingers and was making Vee retell the part where Kayla had guessed, in September, off nothing but shoes.

"She's not wrong often," Vee said. "It's actually terrifying. She read one pair of shoes and had the whole—"

"It wasn't the shoes," Pace said. He was tipped back a little in his chair, hands loose on the table edge, the ease of a man whose evening had gone the distance. "I'd bet it wasn't. You could put her in a room blindfolded and she'd still know who'd just walked in happy." He looked at Vee while he said it, and then, easily, the same motion a man uses handing off a pitcher, he handed it on. "You've seen it longer than I have. What's it look like from the start, when Vee comes into a room?"

And he leaned back out of the light exactly as he had all night for the others, and gave Randi the floor.

Randi didn't answer right away, which wasn't like her, and what she did instead was look at Vee. Looked at her the whole way, the blue eyes gone dark and still with the attention, some long arithmetic running behind them, and Vee sat pinned in it, laughing a little, saying *oh no*, because the toast was turning out to be about her.

"The barometer moves," Randi said finally. "That's what it looks like. The whole pressure of a room. You watch everybody tip toward the window and you check, and it's her." She picked up her glass and lifted it, small, no speech in it, just the two of them and the man at the edge of the lamplight. "To my beautiful best friend."

Vee touched her glass and drank and had to look at the table for a second, too full to aim her face at either of them.

Randi set her glass down. She didn't say anything else. She'd gone quiet — a glazed sort of quiet, her eyes somewhere in the middle distance of the tabletop, her fingers turning the glass where it stood, a slow quarter at a time. Vee knew that quiet. She'd seen it a handful of times, always at the same kind of moment — when a story of hers had really landed, when something she'd told got all the way in — and she was privately, foolishly proud of being able to do it, to move Randi, who moved whole rooms for a living and was so rarely moved herself. Vee watched her best friend sit there in the low light and loved her so much it ached. She reached over and put her hand flat over Randi's wrist, over the little cold line of the bracelet, and Randi came back from wherever she'd been and smiled at her, and the smile got loose from her a little, more of it arriving than she seemed to have planned.

They broke it up not long after. Pace was gathering glasses before either woman asked him to; the pine room had that end-of-the-reel feel, warm and done. In the lot the cold hit like a dropped curtain, their breath showing, Randi's car chirping somewhere off in the dark, and Randi turned on the blacktop and pulled Vee in.

The hug was the usual hug, wool and bitter-orange perfume, and the kiss was the usual kiss, the mouth, soft, and the small second press that was only what they did, months of habit now, nothing in it a parking lot couldn't hold.

"Love you, gorgeous," Randi said. "Call me tomorrow. I want the whole debrief of your own party."

And she was gone across the lot, quick steps ringing on the cold blacktop, and Vee stood a second in the wake of it. Pace was three steps away by the truck with his hands in his jacket pockets, looking at the sky like a man checking for weather. He'd seen — he must have seen, the lot was twelve feet across — and there was nothing on him to say what he made of it, girls being girls, the kiss and the endearment, and a wondering went through Vee, light as a moth: what that looked like from over there. What he thought about it. If he thought about it. Then he opened her door — the handle stuck in the cold and gave to him on the second pull, everything in his world did — and the wondering blew off across the blacktop with her breath.

The truck's heat blew cold and then warm. The road came at them in the headlights, hedged with black trees, and Pace drove it with one wrist on the wheel, quiet. It was a quiet she knew the grain of by now — not absence, just a man with the day set down — and she let it run a while, warm inside her coat, her cheeks still holding the room's heat.

"You were —" she started, and gave up on the sentence, and picked another. "They loved you. All of them. Cassie *knighted* you. I saw it."

"I liked her. She argues honestly."

"Kayla's going to tell the sister's-wedding story at your funeral." She watched the trees go by. Somewhere behind her ribs the whole night was still going on, all its lamps lit. "What did you and Randi end up talking about? I kept seeing you two laughing and I never once got there in time."

Pace took a curve, easy, and she watched the corner of his mouth in the dash light.

"We get along," he said. "They share an interest."

"Who does?"

"Randi. Your friends. All of them." A mile marker went by. "You."

Vee looked at him a moment, then out at the road, and let it warm her from the middle out, the plain and obvious truth of it, too sweet to poke at: her people, and the thing they had in common was her, and he'd noticed, because he noticed everything. She put her hand on the bench seat, palm up, and without looking he took it, and the truck ran on into the dark between the trees with the heat ticking and the two of them claimed and easy, and if some small sentence in her was still standing at a window somewhere, watching a glass turn a slow quarter at a time, she never heard it say anything at all.
