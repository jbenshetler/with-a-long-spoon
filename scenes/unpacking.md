*Vee POV — Vawter Hall, the dorm room, Sunday night. Vee and Cassie; no one else.*

# Unpacking

The radiators were back on for the semester and overdoing it, the whole floor smelling of hot dust and somebody's microwave popcorn, and Cassie's window stood open its two inches — defended territory, all winter, every winter — so the thin line of cold off the sill found Vee as she came in from the suite bathroom in her robe with her hair up in a towel, and she stood in it a second on purpose, clean and boiled pink. She'd missed it. That was the strange sweetness of the night, that she had missed a draft.

Cassie's duffel sat on the lower bunk half-emptied, white athletic socks in one stack and graphic tees in another, the unpacking suspended somewhere in the middle in favor of the desk chair and the book. She'd driven the last leg through the afternoon, she'd said, and been in the room an hour when Vee came home, and now the room had both of them in it again, both lamps going, and it felt like a thing switched back on at the wall.

"Anything interesting happen this weekend?" Cassie said, to the book.

"Cassie." Vee sat down on the edge of the bean bag, which took the rest of her without being asked. "Cassie."

Cassie set the highlighter down. She turned the chair around, the whole chair, and folded her feet up under her where they never reached the floor anyway, and gave Vee the entire front of her attention.

"We talked," Vee said. "Friday night. I drove out there and we — Cass, we *talked*, and the whole stupid month just came down. Like a tent with the pole pulled. It's — we're—" She gave up steering it and let her face say it instead, which her face had been wanting to do since the door.

"Okay," Cassie said, soft, and waited, which was the question.

"And then I just — stayed. The whole weekend." Vee pulled the towel off and shook her hair down, damp on her shoulders. "He'd set the bread machine going before it was even light. The whole house smelled like a bakery. And he was already gone to the farmers market when I got up — Saturday, the farmers market, in January — and he comes back in with a bag in each arm and stands there in the door and says, 'I got mushrooms.' Like a man reporting from the front."

"Mushrooms."

"On toast. Cassie, I can't explain it. It was the best plate of food I have had in my life and it was mushrooms on toast." She was up on her knees in the bean bag now, hands going. "And he does this thing with the oranges — blood oranges — he takes the whole peel off with a knife, around and down, in strips, so there's no white left anywhere, and then he cuts every section out of its little skin, so what you get is a bowl of just the — the jewels. And he salts the persimmons."

"He salts the persimmons."

"Three grains. 'They do it that way in Japan.'" She had his flat delivery down cold, and Cassie's mouth went sideways at it, which from Cassie was applause.

"Didn't bring a bag?" Cassie said.

"I left it in the car." A beat. "There wasn't anything important in it anyway."

"And your clothes?"

"Eep—" said Vee, and then her mouth set off without her. "No — okay, so Saturday night he did a load of laundry, because that's — he *does* that, he just does things, and my sweater was in it. The new one, the cranberry one. And something happened, the water was too hot or, I don't know, wool, and it came out about the size of a doll's, and the dye went into my good pants, so everything of mine was — and that's why Sunday morning I was in his t-shirt, which comes down to about here on me," a hand at her thigh, "which is why he burned the toast."

"He burned the toast."

"Only the first round."

Cassie considered her from the chair, feet folded, the highlighter turning end over end in one hand, uncapped nothing.

"Pace," she said. "Shrank your sweater."

"To a doll size."

A silence about the length of a held breath. Then Cassie laughed — put her head back and actually laughed, the rare full one she never spent on her own jokes — and Vee went too, helplessly, both hands coming up over her face, the two of them laughing in the overcooked room with the cold line of air coming in over it, and the record stood exactly where Vee had left it, corrected by nobody.

"How was your break?" Vee said, when she could.

"I wrapped presents for four days." Cassie set the highlighter down. "My mother saves boxes. There's a closet. You wrap a thing, and she looks at the wrapping, and goes and gets you a better box." She considered. "We made Christmas cookies. They were pretty. They'd have been better made out of Play-Doh."

"You ate them anyway."

"I had a civic duty." She tipped her head at the ceiling. "My brother-in-law got snowshoes. He wore them in the house."

"In the *house*."

"Scratched the floors from the door to the kitchen. My sister made him take them off, and he stood there holding them like she'd towed his car." A dry pause. "It was all baby otherwise. The baby is fine. The baby is a baby."

Vee laughed into the collar of her robe. Under the laugh she could feel the weekend still on her like sun she'd been out in — a house where the water came down like it meant it, where the mornings smelled of bread and she had moved through the rooms like a dream she was having on purpose. The shower she shared with Cassie and the two girls next door had run its polite trickle tonight, three kinds of conditioner crowding the little ledge and Cassie's shampoo-and-conditioner-in-one, a suitemate's towel on the hook and the toilet an arm's reach past the curtain, and she'd stood in it under water that was simply less — less pressure, less private, less hers — and been happy in it anyway, happy the whole time. The room went on around her the same, ordinary, in no hurry, asking nothing — the stacks of white socks, the ticking heat, the old tea rings on the desk. She was reaching for the towel where it had slid when Cassie said, in the same register the snowshoes had gotten, not looking over:

"My mother says thank you for the card."

Vee's hand stopped partway to the towel and went, without her, to the collar of her robe instead.

"She wants to know when she gets to see you again."

"Oh," Vee said, and heard how small it came out, and didn't fix it. Something had gone through her middle that she had no intention of examining tonight, warm and specific, like a hand set flat between her shoulder blades. "Tell her — I don't know. Spring break. Sometime. Tell her I said hi." Which was not the size of it at all, and Cassie, who could hear the size of things, only nodded and recapped the highlighter, done with the subject the exact moment before it would have needed anything from either of them.

She went back to her book. Vee traded the robe for the flannel shirt and brought the collar up over her nose on the way into it — him still, but going faint — and decided, buttoning, that he would just have to wear it for her this weekend. She climbed the ladder to the top bunk and got under the covers. The bunk was exactly one girl wide, and when her arm went out across the covers it found the cold painted cinderblock a hand's width off and stopped there, flat against it — two nights of all that acreage, the heat of him at her back, and now this. She left the hand on the wall, and the wall was only a wall: he was out there west of town in the wide bed, hers again, and the week would go and Friday would come. Below her a page turned; the radiator ticked its way down; the window let its thin cold line in, and she was asleep before she decided to be.
