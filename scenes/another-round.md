# Another Round

Two nights after the roadhouse she was back at his little table with the end of her wine, and he was at the sink with his sleeves rolled, and she was still mining the party for him — what the group chat had done with it, which parts were already legend on her floor, Kayla's account of the cornbread promise growing a new clause every telling. His back listened. She was deep into the saga of Danny's egg whites — a tub of them bigger than his head, acquired Thursday in a fit of inspiration that had Pace's fingerprints all over it, per Meg, who was invoicing the refrigerator space to the group chat — when Pace set a pan in the rack and said, to the window over the sink, "There's somebody I'd like you to meet."

She looked up from her glass.

"Sheri," he said.

The name went through her before she could put anything in front of it. The hair went gold. The leg went tan. The body came up out of a December midnight fully built, everything she wasn't, sitting where she'd sat in the wine light — and she knew the face was hers, she'd said so out loud in Randi's room with her own mouth, *I built her on the nights I felt stupid,* and the knowing turned out to be a coat of paint. Under it the cold arrived anyway, unscheduled, a train she'd been told was cancelled.

"Sheri," she said, and heard her voice do it — go small and bright at the same time, a girl called on in a class she hadn't read for. "Yes. Of course. I'd love to."

Too many words, all of them slightly the wrong size. He glanced back at her, took her face in the once, and returned to the pan.

"Saturday," he said. "There's a taproom she likes."

"What's she like?"

He considered it with the pan in his hand, a man asked to describe a country he'd lived in for years.

"Small," he said. "Loud." He rinsed. "We cycle." A plate went into the rack. "She dances."

She kept her face pleasant until he'd turned back to the sink, and then she sat with them. *We cycle* — a *we*, standing, established, out riding the county all this while without her. And *she dances.* Of all the facts in the world he could have handed her, he'd found the one with a blade in it, and he'd handed it over like a dried spoon. His back stayed easy in its work. She drank the end of her wine, and the week ahead of her rearranged itself around Saturday like furniture around a stain.

---

She dressed for the woman her jealousy had built, and she knew it while she was doing it, and she did it anyway.

The shirt was Meg's, borrowed once before — the black one with the keyhole at the neckline that on Meg was a suggestion, and on Vee had stopped suggesting. The skirt was shorter than anything she owned, off a girl down the hall, lent with a long whistle and no questions asked, which was its own kind of mercy. She had the boots in her hand and then she didn't; she reached past them to the back of the closet and came out with the heels — the four inches of thin black strap Randi had knelt on a boutique floor to buckle onto her, bought for her induction on a card with nothing behind it. A taproom, in January, with winter between every door and the next. She buckled them on anyway. She did her face the careful way, the braver-than-she-felt way, and stood at the mirror in the last of it, a girl in a short skirt in the dead of winter, armed sole to collar against a woman she had invented.

The whole production ran long — the skirt checked twice, the face resettled, the heels a second thought and then a third — and when she came down at last, Pace was standing in the lobby with his hands in his jacket pockets, and said nothing about the time, then or after.

The place was in town, a narrow brick storefront she'd seen a hundred times and never registered — a chalkboard of taps behind the bar in five colors of handwriting, wood floors gone soft down the middle, a Saturday-early quiet on it like a church that had loosened its collar. No stage, no screens, the music low and unambitious. Coats went onto the pegs inside the door — and that left her standing in the open of the room in the whole rig of it, the keyhole and the skirt and the heels, nothing woolen left to hide behind.

Vee had the blonde ready. She'd had her ready for a month — tall, gold, built on a fashion model's chassis, coming through every door in Virginia one after another since Christmas — and she was still hunting her over the tables when Pace, beside her, called out "Sheri," across the room, at a volume she'd never heard him spend in public. Glad. The whole word lit with it. Something went through Vee hot and fast: his voice, being glad, at a woman's name that wasn't hers.

The woman who turned at the high table near the back, already parked behind a longneck, came off the stool standing — and standing was five foot nothing, boots included, every inch of her in motion holding still — hair two colors, bleached going dark at the roots, permed where it fell over her collar, and a round face under too much makeup, a little off, worn like she'd dare you to mention it. The blonde didn't die so much as fail to find anywhere to stand. There was no room for her. There was barely room for the coat pegs. And the heat under Vee's sternum stayed exactly where it was. It had never needed the blonde, it turned out.

"You're late," she said, at a volume the room had to make a decision about, and hugged him — both arms, all the way around, her cheek flat to his chest and her eyes shut for the length of one held count, a hug with its whole weight committed. Pace's hand came down on her back like it had a place there.

"You're early."

"Same thing, told different."

It was true, too — the late. It was Vee's fault, ten minutes of it buckled to her feet, and neither of them had glanced her way yet. And the heat sat up confused, because the body was all wrong and the hug was not. Whatever that hug was, it wasn't the kind you gave a lab partner.

Then Sheri stepped back and came around and planted herself in front of Vee, feet set, chin up the necessary distance — the two of them squared off on the soft wood like the start of the world's most mismatched prizefight — and looked at her.

Vee got her smile screwed on. Sheri's was already up, had been up since the stool, at no visible cost.

The look began at her face and came down with no hurry in it at all, and Vee stood in it the polite way, the received way, a woman being introduced. Women looked at women all day long; she'd been fluent since high school — the pricing look, the placing look, the quick math of shoes and hair that ranked a room in the time it took a door to close — and she braced out of habit for whichever one this was.

The look held at the keyhole, a beat past what any look she'd ever been given in public would have dared, and she waited for it to price the shirt — borrowed, trying too hard, any of the verdicts she knew the shape of — and instead the mouth above it went rounder. It took the bare January leg with its time fully taken, no math arriving anywhere, the mouth opening as it went, and by the time it reached the shoes and stopped — stuck — the smile had arrived at a soundless, appreciative *oh*, cocked, held, like a word being kept for later.

And there, at her feet, which had become the most naked thing in the room — a few thin straps against January, the induction shoes on a taproom floor — the week of dread, the midnight blonde, the borrowed skirt straightened twice in the truck, the whole armory buckled on piece by piece against a rival who was supposed to be standing here — she understood all at once what the look had been doing the whole unhurried trip down. It was *enjoying* her.

The laugh came out of Vee before she'd voted on it. Loud, from the middle of her, apropos of absolutely nothing — and with it something let go that she hadn't known she was holding, off the shoulders, out of the ribs, a month of it leaving in one long breath. Nobody had said a word.

"Sorry," she said. "I'm — sorry. It's not—" and there was nothing at the end of that sentence, so she stood in the wreck of it with her hand pressed flat to her sternum, laughing at a joke nobody at the table had told.

"I'm—" She put her hand out into the wreckage, formal as a church greeter, the only rope in reach. "I'm Vee."

"I know who you are," Sheri said. "Come here, you." And Vee was hugged — nothing like the hug Pace had gotten; this one was fast and fierce, a grab and a rock side to side and done, finished before Vee's arms had gotten a vote in. Sheri set her back on her heels and kept one hand on her arm. "You've got him happy. That's the whole book, far as I'm concerned."

Then, to Pace, in an aside that lowered nothing: "And you get to motorboat them things?"

And Pace smiled — and then the color came up after the smile and kept coming, up his neck, into his ears, past anything deniable, and he looked down at the table and rubbed the back of his neck, and it hung there in front of Vee like a card turned over: Pace, embarrassed. She had seen him easy with a room of strangers, easy turning a woman around a dance floor with the whole hall watching, easy in the dark where nobody stays dignified — and she had never once seen him blush. He wore it like he wore everything, without fighting it, and it was the single most disarming thing she'd watched him do.

---

They got settled the untidy way — Vee negotiating the borrowed skirt and four inches of heel onto a high stool with what dignity survived it — and Sheri commandeered the ordering — a second longneck for herself, no glance at the chalkboard; a hard cider for Vee, the bartender's pick after one question; and Pace's beer, which arrived and then stood at his elbow the rest of the evening doing more witnessing than work. The bartender was a tall girl with a sleeve of ivy tattooed to the wrist, and Sheri watched her go back to the taps with open appreciation and no hurry about it, and turned back to the table already mid-sentence, and Vee looked down into her cider and smiled at it, a private toast to a month of her own idiocy.

It went easy from there, easier than any first table she could remember. Sheri ran the conversation like she ran everything, at a volume for five and a full tilt, and Pace sat in it with a lean Vee had never gotten to see — not the host of Wednesday, working a room; a man off duty in the one company that had never wanted a thing off him. Somewhere in the first round Sheri set her bottle down and leveled it at Vee. "He tells me you get the philosophy of his food."

"The—"

"His words. The *philosophy*. Of his *food*." She set the phrase out in the middle of the table where everybody could look at it. "Lord almighty. Two years I've been riding with that man and eating his cooking off a tailgate, and what I get is derailleurs. You get a philosophy." She drank. "He does go on about you."

And Pace took it as he'd taken the blush, without defense, the corner of his mouth going, and Vee understood she was watching a liberty nobody else on earth had — that this small loud woman held some license to him she'd never seen issued to anyone.

And she held her cider and did her best with her face, warmed through in a place the drink hadn't reached — because she could hear him say it, the exact grave weight he'd put on the word, and because he'd said it somewhere she wasn't, to the one person he tells things to.

The glasses ran down. Pace stood for the bar without being asked, a hand on Vee's shoulder in passing, and he was not three steps gone when Sheri leaned in on her forearms, small and level across the table, and dropped half her volume like a coat.

"I'll tell you how I met him, since he tells it wrong. He says we're dance friends, like that's a thing you trip over." She tipped her head toward the bar. "Hall out on 460, country and western, live band Saturdays. I've been going since I could drive. And I'll tell you what every man in that hall is doing, dancing at you — he's making a down payment. Three songs and he figures he's bought something, and by ten o'clock he's drunk enough to come collect." She said it without heat, a woman reporting soil conditions. "That one shows up alone, drinks maybe one all night, and dances because the dancing is the point. Two years I have watched him turn a woman out on that floor like she was the only thing happening in the state, walk her back, thank her, and go get a water. Hands stay where the dance puts them. Every time. The whole hall's given up on him."

Vee found she was holding her glass and not drinking it.

"Why's he like that," Sheri said, rhetorical, straightening as Pace's boots came back across the boards. "No idea. But I'll tell you the rest another time — you don't praise a man at his own table."

The beers came down; the subject was already three fields over by the time he sat. That was the other thing Vee was learning about her, watching the pivot happen with no seam at all: the volume was real and the makeup was crooked, but there wasn't one uncalibrated thing about what Sheri chose to say and when.

The dance came up, because with Sheri apparently everything came up. "Brooks and Dunn cover band, at the hall, Saturday next," she told Vee, peeling at her label. "He's coming — that got settled back in December, he knows it's settled." She looked up. "You're coming too. I told him then: he's dancing, and if the redhead had opinions about it she could bring them to the hall." A tip of the bottle at her. "Bring your opinions."

"I—" Vee looked at Pace, who was looking at Sheri with something suspiciously like resignation, and felt the evening tip her another inch toward joy. "I'd love that."

"Course you would. You got the legs for it." A jerk of the bottle across the table, at Pace. "What'd I tell you back in December. Bet those legs feel like Christmas." And his ears went again, and Vee decided she could watch that happen all night.

---

The second time he went for the bar, Sheri sent him — "same again, and see if she's got the porter on yet" — and watched him the whole way across the floor, and Vee asked the thing she'd been carrying since the first round.

"How did you two get to be friends?"

"Everybody figures the dancing." Sheri picked at the label of the empty. "It wasn't the dancing. I came out last March." She said it like a road condition. "My mother. My daddy — Marine Corps, retired, which is not a thing that retires. My brother, also Marine Corps. My church, my Bible study, the girls I'd driven to Roanoke and back a hundred times. All of it inside of a month. Whole tablecloth come off the table and nobody caught a dish." She looked at the bottle, flat. "I cried about it plenty then. I'm not crying about it now, so don't you start either."

Vee sat very still. Her hand had gone to her collarbone at some point without her; she took it down.

"People I hardly knew kept telling me it'd be all right," Sheri said. "Strangers will say that for free. Easy words, for a hard thing. The ones who owed me words weren't spending any." She set the bottle down. "Some Saturday in there I went to the hall anyway, because it was the one place left with my name on it, and in the middle of a two-step I started crying. At a dance hall. Which is not the place for that nonsense — you go home to cry, that's what home's—" and she left the sentence where it broke, and tipped the empty up for the last of it, and got nothing, and set it down shaking her head.

"He drove me home. Didn't ask one question the whole way. And next morning he's on my porch with coffee and a box of donuts, which I needed, after the kind of night it had been. Then he fixed the lock my landlord had been promising since August. Stopped the faucet dripping. Saw the bike leaning on the wall and said he rode Sundays, did I want to come. Bought me a bacon cheeseburger after." She shrugged, one shoulder, done with the hard part. "We talked. He never handed me one false word — no *they'll come around*, no *their loss*. I could tell he understood it, and I didn't ask how. He was there. That's all a body can be for you at a point like that. There. And nobody else was. Not my family, not my church, not one of the people I loved. Just this odd man who showed up and listened."

The bar was thirty feet away and Pace was at it, his back to them, waiting on a porter that may or may not have existed, and Vee looked at that back — the same back she talked at over dishes — and something moved through her chest slow and enormous, and she kept her face still because Sheri's was still.

"He knows how it goes, is the thing," Sheri said. The label-picking stopped. "Went bad with his daddy. He hasn't been home since he was sixteen." A silence she didn't decorate. "Two years he's been my friend, and that is every word of it I've got."

Then the glasses came down on the table, and Sheri came up to full volume between one breath and the next — "no porter, that girl's been saying June since March" — and the folder was shut, no seam, no glance across the table to see how it had landed, and Pace slid onto his stool asking whether anyone was going to help him with the pretzels or whether taking care of this table was on him too, and Vee laughed, and the laugh had something underneath it, and nobody at the table asked what.

---

The night got loud, and then narrow, in the pleasant order. Somewhere in there the room developed a soft tilt, and Vee did the arithmetic late, the drink counting itself up only at the end — the cider had kept arriving, that was the thing about Sheri ordering, and Sheri herself was two ahead and telling a story about a horse that required both hands. Pace's beer stood at his elbow where it had stood since the first hour, mostly full, long gone flat.

He settled with the bar at some point nobody witnessed. He got their coats at another.

The stool let her off less gracefully than it had taken her on — the floor had gone approximate somewhere in the third cider, and the heels met it like a rumor — and before she'd finished assembling her feet under her, Pace's arm came around her waist. The whole arm, solid as a porch rail, settled in like it meant to stay. She leaned into the side of him and quit negotiating with the floor. It read as affection. It worked as ballast. With him, she was coming to understand, those had never been two different things.

Outside, the cold dropped on them like a curtain, and Sheri's breath went up in a flag while she dug for keys, and Pace said, "Leave it."

"It's a truck, not a—"

"Sheri."

She looked at him a second, keys in her fist. "Run me out before ten," she said. The arm walked Vee through the cold and handed her up into the cab before it let her go, and Sheri got in like the truck was hers, which, Vee gathered, in some standing arrangement it partly was — the middle of the bench conceded to the smallest by ancient law, boots on the hump, narrating the road in both directions. Two songs came through the radio on the way out, and Sheri sang over both of them — her own versions, filthy from the first line and committed to the last — and Vee laughed until the seatbelt got involved, laughed until she was begging, and knew even while she was laughing that both songs were ruined for her for life: that she'd be standing in some checkout line years from now when one of them came on, and lose it all over again.

Sheri's place was half a duplex at the end of an unpaved road, one window lit blue — siding gone chalky, a gutter hanging its head at one corner, the kind of address a full-time job and part-time classes could just about cover. She was out with the door half a goodnight — "Saturday, both of you, and leave them shoes home — can't two-step on stilts" — and up the path, and Pace sat with the truck in park and the headlights held on her door, and didn't move, and didn't say anything about not moving, until the door had opened and shut and the porch light came on over it. Then he backed down the run.

Vee slid over into the space Sheri had left, against him, and rode there with the heater ticking up and her head gone pleasantly heavy on his shoulder.

"I like her," she said.

"She likes you."

"She told me things." The words came out smaller than she'd sized them, the drink doing the tailoring now. "Good ones. Not telling you which."

"No," he agreed, and took them onto the county road.

The vents had gone from cold to warm without her catching the moment it happened. The dark ran by, the fields under their old snow, and she thought about a porch she'd never seen and thirty miles of nothing and a dog, and about a man standing his headlights on a door until the light came on, which he had done just now the exact same easy unannounced way he apparently did everything, everywhere, for everyone he kept, and had never once mentioned.

"Bacon cheeseburgers," she said, to the window, to nobody.

"What?"

"Nothing."

She rode the rest of the way with her body leaning on his, watching his hands take them home — both of them on the wheel, ten and two, steady as a table — like something full he was carrying, and had no intention of spilling.
