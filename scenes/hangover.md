# Hangover

The light was wrong before anything else was. It came at the window from a low angle that meant late morning, not the honest gray she'd have earned by getting up when she'd meant to, and it found the backs of her eyes and pressed. Vee lay still and let the day assemble itself one careful piece at a time, because turning her head seemed like a decision that ought to be made slowly, after review.

His side of the bed was cold. Pace was up. Pace was always up, she was learning, by some hour that kept no honest name either.

On the nightstand, where she'd find it before she found anything else, stood a tall glass of ice water, the ice long melted to slivers, a ring of sweat around its base soaked into a folded square of paper towel he'd put down so it wouldn't mark the wood. She got up on one elbow and drank it down without stopping, and it was the best thing that had ever happened to her.

The night came back to her out of order, the way a dropped deck comes back — not the game, just the cards. She'd fallen asleep in the truck; that one was near the top, the heater ticking and her cheek on the cold window and then nothing. Somewhere lower there was the bathroom, the hard white light of it and him crouched in front of her with a glass in his hand saying *drink this, all of it,* patient as a man watering something. Two small brown pills in her palm. A gap where the walk from the truck to the door should have been, that she reached for and didn't find. And then, last, the things that had stuck: his hands drawing one of his own undershirts down over her head, the soft worn white cotton of it swallowing her to the knees, the smell of his soap in it; being steered by the shoulders to the bed; his mouth, once, on her forehead, in the dark, after he thought she was already gone.

She lay in the shirt now. She hadn't undressed herself. She hadn't done one single thing for herself since the third cider, and here she was in his bed under his blanket with water inside her and the worst of it already handled, and she had a sudden vivid sense of how much of the night must have been just him, quietly, doing all of it.

She got herself vertical. His robe was on the back of the door where it always was, terrycloth gone soft with washing, and she put it on over the shirt and tied it and followed her nose.

The smell reached her in the hall and got better the whole way down. When she came into the kitchen he was at the sink with his sleeves rolled, and on the counter between the table and the sink, cooling on a rack, was a loaf of bread, dark-crusted and studded, that had not existed when the world ended last night.

"You made bread," she said. Her voice came out with gravel in it.

"You were asleep for a while."

"You made *bread.*"

"Cinnamon raisin." He said it as if that settled the strangeness of a man baking a loaf from scratch while a woman slept off three ciders in his bed. He turned from the sink and looked at her in his robe and his shirt and whatever her face was doing, and something moved at the corner of his mouth and he let it go. "Sit. You need to eat before you decide how you feel."

She sat. The counter was between them, and she was glad of it; she was assembled out of spare parts this morning and wanted a table's width to hold them together. He put things in front of her one at a time, the way he did everything — a mug of tea going gold with honey, then two slices of the bread, buttered and gone translucent at the edges, then eggs, soft, folded, salted exactly right. She started on it before she'd meant to and made a sound over the first bite of the toast that she'd have been embarrassed by on a better morning.

"I like her so much," she said, when she surfaced. "Sheri. I like her *so* much."

"She likes you too."

The words landed with a little echo on them, and Vee heard, faintly, that she'd said this before — leaning on his shoulder in the truck with the fields going by, *I like her,* small and drunk and certain. She'd said it then and she was saying it now, and the second time was somehow funnier, being sober and ruined instead of drunk and sincere. She let out a laugh, and then a real one came up behind it, because a picture had arrived — Sheri planted in front of her on the taproom floor, feet set, taking her time down the whole length of her, that appreciative soundless *oh* at the shoes.

"She *liked* me," Vee said, and put a hand flat to her chest, and laughed some more. "That look. In the taproom. She looked at me like—"

"That way too," Pace said. "She can be like that." He said it without a flicker, easy, matter-of-fact. He'd caught exactly what she meant; he always caught it. He just never pretended not to have.

"No—" Vee got herself back. "No, we're good. It was—" She waved the fork. "I liked it, honestly. Best thing that's happened to my legs in a Virginia January." She ate another bite, warm all through in a way the tea hadn't done by itself, and the ease of it, the whole soft domestic ordinary morning of it, opened the next thing in her the way one thing always opened another with him.

"I'd love to meet your other friends," she said.

He'd turned back to the sink. He didn't turn around for it. "Sheri is my friend," he said, to the window.

Vee smiled at his back and reached for her tea. "Of course she is." It wasn't an answer to anything; it was the kind of thing you said while a real answer came. "She told me, last night — how good you were to her, when she came out. When everybody else—" She stopped, because it was Sheri's to tell and she'd already carried more of it out of that taproom than she'd meant to. "You mean the world to her. Do you know that?" She drank. "But I meant your *other* friends. The guys."

He rinsed a pan and set it in the rack, and there was a pause in him that she'd have missed on any morning her head wasn't turned all the way toward him. Then he turned around and looked at her across the counter, and said it plainly, the way he said everything he'd already decided not to dress up.

"Sheri's my friend," he said. "My one friend. There isn't anyone else."

The kitchen went very slightly still. She sat with it and waited for it to rearrange into something she recognized, and it wouldn't. Nobody had *one* friend. One friend wasn't a number a person had. She looked for the joke in his face and there wasn't one, and she looked for the door he'd left himself and there wasn't one of those either.

"But—" She set the fork down. "But everybody likes you. You're—" She heard herself and kept going anyway. "I *saw* you. At the party. People you'd never met, people who aren't anything like you, and by the end of the night they were all standing around a little taller and none of them could've told you why. You're the most comfortable person with people I've ever — you could have *friends,* Pace. Anyone. Why?" It came out more helpless than she'd meant it. "Why don't you have friends?"

He considered it with the honest attention he gave every real question, the second or two it took him to find the actual answer and not the near one.

"Women misunderstand it," he said. "The friendship. It gets awkward." He said it flat, and let it be small, and didn't sharpen it.

He didn't have to. Vee sharpened it herself, sitting there — finishing the missing word in her own head where he'd left it out. *Your* friendship. They misunderstood *his.* A man who looked like that and paid attention like that — who came into a room and was entirely inside it, as present over dishes as he'd be in the dark — a woman couldn't stay just his friend for long; it curdled, it wanted to be the other thing. And she thought, wincing a little, of Theo, whom she'd kept at a careful friend's distance all last spring while he found reasons to fall into step beside her, and how there'd been a season of awkward in it before it settled, and how she'd been the one holding the line then. She knew the shape of it. She'd just never stood on his side of it.

"And the men," she said. Gently. She kept her voice a place he could put things down. "Not them?"

"Different reason." He leaned back against the counter, arms crossing, in it and not in it. "I went to college at fifteen."

"I know."

"Men make friends by competing." He said it like a fact he'd handled a long time, worn smooth. "That's the age it happens — thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, all shoulder to shoulder, all measuring. And I couldn't get into a single arena they were measuring in." He ticked it off with no heat at all. "Not size. Not — the other thing, girls, I was somewhere else on that already. Not money; I had my own by then, which is worse than having none. Not age. There was no lane I could stand in and lose to somebody the ordinary way, and lose again, and come out the other side friends. And by the time I could have — by the time my own age caught up to a room I was in — the window was shut. Men don't start at twenty-two. That door's on a timer."

"That's—" she started, and he wasn't finished, and she let him have it.

"And the ones already there, when I was small among them," he said, and here the voice went the last degree flatter, the careful way he carried a thing that was full, "either weren't safe or weren't welcoming."

He didn't say a name. He didn't have to say a name.

She held very still with her tea going cold in her hands and did the arithmetic she hadn't been asked to do. *Weren't safe.* She had a rock in her, from a January night by a black window — *less than staying,* he'd said, when she'd asked wasn't he scared to leave home at fifteen, so plainly it had taken her a second to understand it was the worst thing he'd told her all night. *I was not a good fit where I was.* And Sheri, last night, low across a taproom table with her volume dropped like a coat: *went bad with his daddy. He hasn't been home since he was sixteen.* The pieces slid together and made a thing she was never going to be shown directly, and didn't need to be.

"It's a small thing to explain it with," he said, misreading her quiet for doubt, or not misreading it, "but people underrate it. Friendships get deep in the ordinary questions. *What are you doing for Christmas.* *Your folks coming up.* *You want to bring anybody.*" He turned the cold mug a quarter turn on the counter, and she knew that gesture, she'd watched that exact quarter-turn come out of him once before over a scar he wasn't ready to name. "Every one of those has a gap under it, for me. You can't hand those back and forth easily, so the thing they're the doorway to doesn't get built. That's most of it. It isn't tragic. It's just a door that didn't open, and then you're grown."

*It isn't tragic.* He believed it, the way he'd believed the two years with Daphne had been luck. He set it in good order and the good order was the tell.

And the enormity of it came over Vee slowly, from underneath, the way the drink had come over her last night — late, all at once, and more than she'd counted. She thought about her own life, the actual marrow of it. Meg, whose closet she raided for anything that had to be braver than she was. Kayla, who reached across a dining-hall tray and took half her fries as tax, every time, without asking. Cassie, who'd hear out her whole heart on a Tuesday afternoon and say *oh, Vee,* in the voice that had already caught the part Vee hadn't said out loud. Randi, who she'd laughed with helpless on the carpet of a shoe store, who got the secrets, who did the triage on every heartbreak she'd ever had. She tried to lift them all out of her life, every one of them, and stand in their place the single person Pace had had — and the arithmetic wouldn't run. One person couldn't be all of that. Nobody could hold that much of somebody.

He'd never had any of it. Not one. Not ever.

Because he'd had the one. Only the one, doing the work of all four, in a building full of grown men where there was no one else his own age at all — Daphne. And she'd carried more of him than a person should have to, and — Vee made herself look at it head-on — she'd had that much of him to begin with only because of the thing about it that was wrong, the thing Vee still would not set a word to. She tried to hold it as he held it, the best two years he'd had, and not as the ugly grown-up word that kept forming under her tongue and that she kept pressing back down. She tried to see it as he had lived it, and not as the wrong shape it had actually been, and she could only manage the two of them stacked and unreconciling, both true at once, and it hurt to hold them both. And then Daphne had gone — *sensibly,* he'd said, *it would have been stranger if she'd stayed* — and when she went, he'd had no one. Not one person left. No Randi to bring it to. The one had been all of it, and then the one was gone, and there was nobody left in the world to take the heartbreak to.

She thought about the boys she'd known at that age — freshmen, loud and preening and certain, and under all of it so frightened, taking each other's measure by the hour, working out who was biggest and who'd been with a girl and who was going to matter. None of that had been Pace. He'd stood off the side of it: too far ahead to be worth beating, and too far past them in the one thing they'd have hazed him for to want to try — less than any of them in every arena they competed in, except the two that counted for nothing on that field. He was smarter than the whole room. And he had already been with Daphne. And by the time his own age might have set him down in among those boys, he wasn't there to be set down — while they carried trays through a cafeteria and fell asleep over freshman English, Pace was a graduate student with a patent and money of his own and a house, teaching himself to cook alone in his kitchen. He'd been handed a whole life a person was supposed to grow into slowly, years before the growing, and left to eat it by himself.

She looked at him now, leaned against his own counter in his rolled sleeves — the man who'd baked her bread while she slept, and left water where she'd reach for it, and taken his mother out of the answer, months back, when she'd asked what his mother called him and he'd handed her his own name instead, *Peter,* and let that be all of it. His one friend on this earth a five-foot woman who could never want him — the only kind, she was sure now, the world had ever quite let him keep.

She got up. She came around the counter that had been between them the whole conversation, and she didn't say she was sorry, because sorry would have asked him to hold still and be comforted for a thing he'd handed her as plain fact. She just crossed the kitchen and put herself against him, both arms, all the way around, her cheek flat to his chest, the way Sheri had held him in the doorway of the taproom, with her whole weight committed.

His arms came around her after a moment. Not fast. He set his chin on the top of her head and looked out over it at something past her, the window or the far wall or the middle of the morning, and he wasn't happy and he wasn't sad. He was somewhere else with it, thinking, the way he got, and he held her and let her hold him and didn't try to make either one of them feel better, and outside the low light came in wrong and bright off last week's snow, and the bread cooled on the counter, and neither of them said anything for a while.
