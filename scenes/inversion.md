# Inversion

*Diptych. Panel one: Vee POV, close third — Thursday, 8:20, Dr. Marsh's room letting out; Randi leaves for Standards. Panel two: Randi POV, close third — the sorority house, the Standards room, the hall; Vee is not present.*

---

Thursday Marsh had talked about kinship for an hour and twenty minutes, who a village counted as family and who it didn't and how much of the law was only the counting, and Vee had taken in about half of it, which was twice what she'd managed on Tuesday. Randi's knee had been against hers the whole hour.

Tonight it was Randi who had the candy — a ragged roll, the wrapper worked half off, down near the last of it — and partway through the hour she thumbed one loose under the desk and pressed it into Vee's palm without looking over. Vee had it in her mouth before she saw the color.

Lime. Cold and green and sharp, and it put her straight back onto the dance floor with the margarita still on both their mouths and Randi's tongue, and she shut that down and sucked the candy to nothing and kept her eyes on the front and her leg still against Randi's and did not look over.

Her own roll sat in her coat pocket, the wintergreen, untouched; she'd had it ready the way she always did, and tonight Randi had not needed it. Tonight Randi fed her lime.

There was no dinner tonight. She had known that since Tuesday, and had spent two days being glad of it, and now, with the room standing up around her and Randi bending for her bag, she was not glad at all.

Out in the corridor the night had already emptied it, the long waxed hush of a building after its last class, a few students hurrying the other way and out into the cold.

Randi stopped short of the doors and turned and pulled her in, and this hug Vee felt in a detail she had not let herself feel them in before: Randi's arms gone in low under hers, the way the shorter woman had to come up into her to do it, and then Randi's head settling heavy on her chest, the small certain weight of it over her breastbone. The bitter orange, the green at its edge.

Vee stood with her chin in Randi's hair and was aware of the whole of her, the fit of her, the press of her all down the front — and worked, the entire time, at not remembering how that same body had felt against her back on the floor at the mixer, which was its own way of remembering it.

She had a semester of Randi's hugs to set this one against, and she reached for them like her favorite sweater — the easy warm ones after a meal, Vee the one holding on a beat past where a hug stopped and the beat after that; the goodbyes, continental and generous. That was Randi's friendship, only Randi being Randi, as Vee spent part of every day remembering.

The dance floor had been a joke — Randi's joke, got up for a boy named Harrison — and Vee was the one who had taken it too far, her mouth, her hands, the whole of her going where it had no business going, and Randi had laughed it off and not let it be a thing between them, had handed the night back folded and easy.

Vee was grateful past saying. She treasured what the two of them had, and she knew Randi did too. It would be a tragedy, an actual one, if her sex brain went and wrecked something that good.

This was not one of those hugs.

Randi stepped back with both hands still on Vee's arms, and the relief went through her first. The other thing came right behind it, and she could not have said which of the two she'd have picked.

"I hate this," Randi said. "I'd so much rather be across a table from you, hearing all about that man of yours." Her mouth went rueful and her eyes didn't; her eyes had a gleam in them, bright and private, the look of a woman on her way to something she was going to enjoy. "But duty calls." She squeezed Vee's arms once. "A girl needs a spanking!"

Vee laughed. It came out a half-beat late and a note too high, and under the laugh her body had already heard the word and gone where the word went: a kitchen, a wet hand, the small sound of it and the sting that wasn't small; a bare bottom and a bet she'd lost; Randi across a table with her head tipped asking *did you deserve it*, and herself saying *probably*, and Randi's smile going down through her like a swallow of something warm.

All of that in the time it took to laugh. Heat, low and sudden, and the shame right there on top of it, and the shame did nothing to put the heat out.

"Go," she said. "Be kind to the girl in trouble."

"It's not just my decision," Randi said. "But she'll get what's coming to her, I'm sure." Her hand came up and put a piece of Vee's hair back behind her ear, and Vee felt it all the way down. "Saturday, gorgeous."

Then she was off down the corridor toward the cold, quick heels on the waxed floor, her coat swinging, and Vee stood where she'd been left with her own coat open and watched her go.

She put her hands in her coat pockets against the cold, and her fingers closed on the roll there — her own, the wintergreen, forgotten since she'd set it ready at the start of the hour — and she did not take it out, and she went out the other way.

---

Randi crossed back over the campus in the dark and the cold, a walk she had made a hundred times to go and run these meetings, and made it tonight the way she made it every other time — at her own clip, early, the chair ahead of her board. She was never behind herself.

She had made herself leave the warm room — stood a beat too long in a hug she had not wanted to end, and left with the shape of the girl still on her, all down the front of her, and carried it the whole way like a thing she would not be let keep.

Randi let herself in out of the cold and the house closed its noise around her — the common room going, somebody's speaker. It ran warm in the middle and cold at the edges, the way it always did, the old house too big and too thin-walled to hold an even degree anywhere in itself: the center stifling and the corners cold and no room in it a person could simply be comfortable.

Nobody looked up when she came in, which was its own kind of information. She went for the stairs.

"—up for review tonight, did you hear? Standards."

"Who?"

"I don't know — somebody had a guy up in her room, is what I heard. After hours. Never signed him in, nothing." A little thrilled by it. "Brooke's running it herself."

She had her foot on the first stair and did not slow down. She winced for the girl all the same — she had the shape of the complaint, and the hall had it wrong the way the hall always had it wrong: the hours off, a guest made into a guy up in her room after hours, the lurid version, the one that traveled. None of it true. And none of it true in a way that did the girl the least bit of good, because wrong as the house had the details, there was still a line crossed under all of it, and getting the details wrong had never once un-crossed a line. Then the second girl said, "God, I'd hate to be her," and laughed, and Randi went up.

The Standards room was the small meeting room off the second-floor landing, and the door was open, and the light was on, and they were already in it. That was the first wrong thing.

She was never the last to a Standards meeting. She ran them. She came early, set the water out, took the seat at the head with the window behind her. Tonight the seat at the head — her chair, the one she set the water from and called the room to order from — had Brooke in it, and the board chair on the near side had a sophomore named Caroline in it with a legal pad.

And the only seat left was the one that faced the table, the one the girl under review sat in, pulled a little out from the wall so there was nothing behind it or beside it, a glass of water set out in front of it by someone who was not Randi.

Somebody closed the door. She heard it go behind her, the latch, and did not turn around to see who.

"Randi." All concern — but she did not get up, and she did not say sit; she let the one open chair do that. "Thank you for coming up."

Randi sat. She put her hands in her lap and left the water where it was. Brooke's hand rested on the closed folder, the nails a flawless French, white-mooned and perfect. Caroline's pen moved over the legal pad in a fist tipped candy pink. Randi's own nails lay bare in her lap, buffed to a dull shine under a single coat of clear.

"You know the procedure better than anyone at this table," Brooke said, and there was the rueful half-beat, gone as soon as it came. "So you'll let me keep it properly. I've taken the chair in your place tonight, and Caroline will sit the board and take the minutes. There is a written complaint, and it cites the standard. I'll put each part of it to you in turn, and you'll answer to each as you wish. We'll observe the forms. I think you'd want us to." Businesslike, not unkind; she let it settle.

The pace was deliberate, the one speed a thing like this ever moved — a machine, a system, something older and bigger than Randi, bigger than this one room and this one night. It was the rules themselves, the ones she had kept and made other girls keep, moving now at the speed rules move when they finally turn and come for you. 

The bones of it right, the order right, and the finish skipped — the small cares that were Randi's own, the ones she had never once left out: the half-second of quiet held before a charge, so the girl heard it coming and had to sit in it; the warmth laid down only where it worked and kept off the rest, where it would only have given the game away.

Randi had the finer hand and nowhere left to put it. She found nothing anywhere in the form to stand on and refuse. The form was correct. Correct was the whole of what it was for.

"Of course," Randi said.

Her voice dropped off the procedure, lower and softer. Randi couldn't say where the sorority ended and the girl began in Brooke, not now, not ever. "I didn't want this to be a docket," Brooke said. "I want you to know I held it off as long as I could."

Brooke drew the papers from a fresh manila folder and set them neatly on top of it. The folder was new, opened for this, for her, and her name was on the tab — handwritten, in Brooke's round ballpoint. Randi would never have handwritten it. She printed the label and set it on square, so the thing read as procedure and not as a woman taking a pen to your name, and she was grading the crooked tab before she had taken in a single word of the page.

She had kept folders like it on other girls, a printed label on every one, and she had sat where Brooke was sitting with the girl across from her going pale. She had never once looked at the page while she spoke; you did not need to, you had it. Brooke looked at the page. Not so the room would catch her — Brooke was better than that — but Randi caught her, the small drop of the eyes to the line a beat before she said it, the tell of a woman who did not quite have it by heart.

"The complaint speaks to a sister's conduct this year," Brooke said, and read from it without appearing to read, "grown — their word — *loose. And sloppy.*" The concern stayed on the words, which was worse than if it had gone. "We take these one at a time, before a thing becomes a pattern. That is what tonight is."

---

The first of it was guests, and it did not come from the guestbook. The guestbook sat where it always sat, on the hall table by the front door, cream and gilt-edged with a pen on a little brass chain, the wedding-register kind; it came out for pledges and legacies and nobody else, got signed in careful round hands, and meant nothing. This came from statements. Sisters had written them out and put their names to them — girls Randi had to reach for, girls she could not have found in a crowd — and each one was a date, a time, and a single line. *A guest, with Randi, in her room.*

"Do you dispute these?" Brooke said.

Randi did not. Every date was a date Vee had been there — the stats friend, the redhead, the nine-in-the-morning girl Brooke herself had let in, the wrecked one months later with her whole red-eye still on her. The house had set it down as a guest and carried it up the stairwell as a guy, and between the writing and the whispering it had managed to miss the only true thing in it, and Randi was not going to be the one to hand that over.

"They go to the pattern," Brooke said, and moved on.

---

Then the nights, and the nights were Brooke's own — not a sister's statement but the count a president kept of who slept under the roof, which was hers to keep.

"'A member intending to be absent from the chapter house overnight,'" Brooke read, "'shall obtain the approval of the House Mother in advance, to be recorded on the overnight card.'" She looked up. "We have you out rather a great deal."

There is no card, Randi thought. There is no box to put the card in. No House Mother has slept in this house in a generation; the post was folded into a management company before any of us were born. The rule was a fossil nobody had bothered to strike, because a fossil costs nothing to keep and you never know when a dead rule will come in handy. This one had only had to wait.

And the dead rule, tonight, was not even wrong. She did not know if they had every date right; she knew the pattern was. Night after night she had not slept here because she had slept in Pace's arms — no card, no box to have put one in — and the lie had reached down the years and laid its finger on the one plain truth.

She was out of the room before Brooke reached the next line — back to a hot green afternoon on the common in early August, the heat sitting wet and heavy over grass that never went gold here no matter the month, and her at the center of her own ring, holding court on it in a miniskirt she was glad of and wedges and a little top cut to leave her middle bare — and a man walking up into the middle of them with nothing in his hands. Not gorgeous. Not tall. Wearing the wrong everything.

The ring did to him what it did to anything it had not already cleared, the small cuts it made to see if a thing would bleed — Sloane's smile gone to glass, two of the girls starting in on him sidelong — and he took every one and handed it back turned somehow into a kindness, set a joke up so the girl cutting him got to land the laugh and shone landing it, and inside fifteen minutes the ring was his and smiling and did not know how it had happened.

Nothing they threw stuck to him. He did not appear to notice it was being thrown. He looked at her through all of it, direct, not pushing, certain of himself and not selling it, and when he asked her to dinner he dropped his voice to do it, only for her.

She had said yes. Not because he was anything the ring wanted — he was nothing it wanted — but because she had stood inside that ring her whole life being precisely what it wanted, and here was a man it could not lay a hand on, and she had to know what that was. She drove him to dinner in her own car, and she drove the rest of it too, that same night — took him home and took him to bed before he could think to set the pace himself — out ahead of him, as she was of everyone. That was the night her life first turned toward being hers.

She knew now what the hurry had cost her. Not the night — the long approach to it she had never let there be: his sweetness, the want given time to grow between two people until it ached, how he had looked at her and let her feel herself wanted, the one kiss that put no claim on her and left her all the room in the world to choose. That was the part worth having slowly, and she had taken it fast, taken what she wanted the first night she wanted it, not knowing as she took it what she was spending. She had watched him be unhurried with someone else since — the waiting, the thing allowed to arrive when it came — and only then had she understood the size of it. She had never given the house one word of any of it, and she was not giving them one now.

"I don't have anything to say about the nights," Randi said.

---

There was a line on the page that was not a guest or a night. It was a man, and what he did.

"He's a graduate student," Brooke said, and let it sit a second, all concern, so that the room could feel for itself the weight of what was coming. "An instructor. At the university." She said *instructor* the way you'd say a word you were being very careful with. "And I think we'd all just feel more comfortable — it doesn't look right, is all. A sister and an instructor. However it started. You see how it looks."

He taught one section of one course that had nothing to do with Randi and nothing to do with the redhead and never had. Brooke knew that; Brooke had sat Randi down the morning after the first night and gone all the way around to it, light as anything, whether he taught her section, and had gone limp with performed relief when the answer was no. 

The rule did not care whose section. The rule said *instructor,* and the word did the work, and nobody at the table was going to be the one to say that a man who drove a fifteen-year-old pickup was not exactly what the word was built to keep out.

---

And then the mixer, and for the mixer the complaint went broad.

"'Conduct,'" Brooke read, "'tending to impropriety, or to the appearance of impropriety, such as would call into question the character of the member or of the chapter.'"

A sentence cut to hold anything at all. Girls danced together at every function this house threw. Girls kissed girls two and three times a semester, on a dare, for a boy, for the fun of the gasp, and no one ever wrote it up. You wrote it up when you had already decided, and then you reached for the sentence broad enough to carry what you'd decided. Randi knew the sentence to the comma. She had reached for it herself, more than once, across this same table.

They had kept the mixer for last, the way you kept the witnesses for last, and for the mixer they had two.

"She brought a girl," Sloane said, careful, fair, making it worse with the weight of being so fair. "As her date. To a mixer. And she had her hands all over her out on the floor — hiked that little skirt right up, for the whole room to see — and at the end she kissed her. Not a peck. A real one, with everything in it, in front of everybody. That's not how we're supposed to behave. I'm not saying anything by it. I just thought the board should know."

Kinsey had been waiting all her life to go second at something.

"Her hand was down the front of her bra," she said. "The redhead's. And the other one up her skirt — up it, I saw it, with the whole back of her out to the room." She did not have the redhead's name, so it was *the redhead* all the way through, and the room drew a small breath and kept it.

It would have taken Randi one moment to end her. *My hand was never in her bra, Kinsey. It was flat on the outside of a skirt, for a boy named Harrison, with the whole floor watching me do it — and I did not put my hand up her skirt.* Not that she hadn't wanted to. God, she had wanted to.

She had helped them look at her, though. God forgive her, she had. But that was hers to carry, and you did not hand a board the thing it was already reaching for.

The wanting was the real crime. Just not the one they would name.

But the sentence that killed the lie came with the truth riding its back, and the truth was only the smaller scandal, not the lesser crime — it broke the very same rule, it only broke it quietly — and to set it down in front of Caroline's moving pen she would have had to tell them to the inch how much she remembered and how much she had wanted, and the wanting was the one thing she had spent her whole life making sure no one ever saw.

So she let Kinsey have the bra. She let her have the hand up the skirt. It was her own trap handed back to her, the one she had set from that very chair: dispute it and hang faster, correct it and hang slower, and either way the board has you. She sat with her face arranged and let the fat lie stand, because the thin truth cost more.

"Thank you, Kinsey," Brooke said.

"Do you dispute any of it?" Brooke said. Gently. The door held open one more time. "Any of the particulars — the dates, the account of the mixer. Anything you'd want Caroline to note."

Randi thought about the particulars. She knew every lever on that page; she had pulled most of them herself at one time or another. She could dispute the bra. She could dispute the overnight bylaw as a dead letter nobody enforced. She could make the whole thing take two hours and come out of it with half the facts struck — and the other half admitted in her own mouth to strike them, and not one charge dropped for the trouble.

"No," she said. "I don't dispute any of it."

Caroline wrote that down.

---

They sent her out to the landing while they decided, which was the part she had always liked least from the other side — she had never thought it necessary, sending the girl out, and had done it every time because the ritual wanted the girl to stand alone somewhere and hear the murmur of her own case through a door and never the words.

Now she stood where the girls stood, and she did the thing she had kept herself from doing all night. She left.

She went back to a Sunday in October, the first time Vee ever got into her car — the top down, the day too bright, Vee coming out of the dorm in something she had plainly thought about and was too hopeful to hide, hopeful to be out with her, to be let in — and the whole slow lunch of her, the way she laughed with her entire body, the way she talked with her hands and forgot to guard herself.

At the end of it, stopped crooked in the fire lane, she had told Vee to come here and drawn her across the gearshift — Vee leaning in for what she must have taken for the cheek-thing, the goodbye; Randi never did know what she thought was coming — and taken her by the jaw and kissed her on the mouth instead, soft, lips to lips, once, and let her go. She had wanted that kiss for weeks. She had told herself it was a game she was running for Pace, and she had wanted it anyway, and it had been good — and it had been nothing at all beside the one she used to lie in the dark and build.

That one she had finally taken at the mixer, in the only shape her world would let her have it: a show for a boy, Vee's mouth opening under hers in a room that thought it was watching a joke. It had been even better than the version she had worn smooth with imagining.

And she had gone to that mixer meaning to burn the whole careful thing to the ground. She had decided it before she ever asked Vee to come, and decided it again the night before the dance, lying awake with it: tonight she would tell Vee. Not the friend's *love you, gorgeous* she handed over at every goodbye, the one Vee heard one way and she meant two. The real one. The whole one. She had found the nerve for it because of the kiss Vee had brought her in January, out of nowhere, both hands on Randi's face — the first kiss in four months that Vee had started herself. If Vee had started that, then maybe.

And then she had the kiss, the real one, at last, and it was so far past what she had let herself know it would be that the telling went out of her like air out of a room. She could not. She could not stand there with her heart out in her two hands and watch Vee's face do whatever it would do. So she had reached for the part of herself that ran every room and found it still there, and put her own face back in order before it could give her away, and made the kiss the joke it had to be — teasing, weightless, the thing two girls did for a boy — turning it loose before Vee could see what it had cost her.

*I almost believed it.* She had believed every second of it. Of all her cowardices it was the one she would take back if she were allowed to take back one, and she had lived inside it for five days, and she would go on living inside it, because the only door out led to a life that might have no Vee in it — might, she did not know — and that was the one picture her mind would not hold.

The house brought her back. It had opinions, and they came up the stairwell in pieces the way they always did.

"—remember when she made that freshman cry? The one who—"

"—oh my God, over the *recruitment* thing, yes—"

"—put Hannah on probation for basically nothing, so—"

"—so maybe it's a little, you know. What goes around."

She opened her clutch to do her mouth before they called her back, because you went back in with your face right, that was simply true, and the lipstick turned in her cold fingers and went out of her hand and dropped, and the runner took it without a sound. She looked at it lying in the carpet, the gold tube in the long dim nap of the runner, and she did not bend for it, because the door was opening.

She went back in without it.

---

The sentence, when Brooke read it, was one Randi knew from the first two notes — the rules drawn narrow and brought down all at once, the penalty kept small because small was where the humiliation lived. Thursdays, every one of them, mandatory, the dinners gone. Attendance probation, the thing they put freshmen on. A woman who had sat at the head of this table handed the punishment of a girl caught skipping a car wash.

It was the room they had built around Lauren Pike, and she had helped. Narrow the rules, keep the penalty humiliating, make the space so small the girl walks out of it on her own and nobody ever has to be the one who put her out. 

"We really do just want to see you doing well," Brooke said, and folded the page in half.

Randi said that she understood. Her face held. It had held for twenty-one years and it held now, and nobody at the table saw anything but a sister taking her medicine like a good one.

"And listen." Brooke's voice dropped lower still, gentler, the *can I ask* face coming up one more time. "None of this has to go on anything. The record, I mean. It's all still soft right now, it's all still just us." She let that open, generous, a door swinging wide. "If you wanted to come to chapter — just stand up, just briefly — and say you'd heard the concerns. That your sisters come first, the way they used to. That you were letting the outside things go quiet for a while — not being seen out with them, on campus or off, until the dust settles. The friend. The gentleman. That's all. And it all goes away, and it's like we never had to have tonight at all."

Just stand up, just briefly. The two smallest words in the house, and Brooke had set them down on top of the only two things Randi had ever reached for that were hers.

She let herself weigh it. She made herself, because it was not nothing, and she would not insult it by answering fast.

It was not a little distance. It was standing up in front of the chapter and setting them both down — and then living it. Never seen with either of them, on campus or off. She was popular, but even popular girls had enemies, and she had been Standards; girls held grudges, and some of those grudges were hers. Someone would see her. Someone would talk. And it would come back around to this room, to Brooke — and there would be nothing soft about it the second time.

Pace she could keep. She could go to his place and no one on earth could say for certain where she'd been, and he would make it easy, take whatever it cost him and never once let it reach her.

Vee there was no quiet place for. No room of her own to vanish into, nowhere the two of them could be that the house could not see. She could sit beside her in a lecture hall, maybe, eighty strangers between them and the world, and nothing past that. With Vee it was near to everything.

She would give up the house before she gave up Vee; she knew that the way she knew her own name. But it would not come to that, and she made herself see that too, because clear-eyed was the only way to take a thing like this. Decline tonight and she took the humiliation and the mandatory Thursdays, and all the Thursdays took from her was the hour after class that had been Vee's. That was the whole bill. She kept the Tuesday dinners. She kept the shopping, the long way back, the two of them laughing across the middle of campus. She kept Vee. She only lost Thursdays.

She had answered this once already, in a loud bright room with her arm around Vee's waist. They were only making her answer it again.

"No," Randi said. "Thank you. But no."

Brooke looked at her for a moment, and Randi could not read it.

"All right," she said, and that was all, and she did not ask again, and she did not wait for a reason, the way she never waited; she was already slipping the papers back into the folder and turning to Caroline about something else, and the meeting was letting out around Randi where she sat, the sisters rising and gathering their phones, the ordinary warm noise of the house coming back into the room.

Randi stayed in the chair a moment longer, the one pulled out from the wall with nothing beside it, and then she got up and went to find her lipstick in the hall.
