# Inversion

*Diptych. Panel one: Vee POV, close third — Thursday, 8:20, Dr. Marsh's room letting out; Randi leaves for Standards. Panel two: Randi POV, close third — the sorority house, the Standards room, the hall; Vee is not present.*

---

Thursday Marsh had talked about kinship for an hour and twenty minutes, who a village counted as family and who it didn't and how much of the law was only the counting, and Vee had taken in about half of it, which was twice what she'd managed on Tuesday. Randi's knee had been against hers the whole hour.

Tonight it was Randi who had the candy — a ragged roll, the wrapper worked half off, down near the last of it — and partway through the hour she thumbed one loose under the desk and pressed it into Vee's palm without looking over. Vee had it in her mouth before she saw the color.

Lime. Cold and green and sharp, and it put her straight back onto the dance floor with the margarita still on both their mouths and Randi's tongue, and she shut that down and sucked the candy to nothing and kept her eyes on the front and her leg still against Randi's and did not look over.

Her own roll sat in her coat pocket, the wintergreen, untouched; she'd had it ready the way she always did, and tonight Randi had not needed it. Tonight Randi fed her lime.

There was no dinner tonight. She had known that since Tuesday, and had spent two days being glad of it, and now, with the room standing up around her and Randi bending for her bag, she was not glad at all.

Out in the corridor the night had already emptied it, the long waxed hush of a building after its last class, a few students hurrying the other way and out into the cold.

Randi stopped short of the doors and turned and pulled her in, and this hug Vee felt in a detail she had not let herself feel them in before: Randi's arms gone in low under hers, the way the shorter woman had to come up into her to do it, and then Randi's head settling heavy on her chest, the small certain weight of it over her breastbone. The bitter orange, the green at its edge.

Vee stood with her chin in Randi's hair and was aware of the whole of her, the fit of her, the press of her all down the front — and worked, the entire time, at not remembering how that same body had felt against her back on the floor at the mixer, which was its own way of remembering it.

She had a semester of Randi's hugs to set this one against, and she reached for them like her favorite sweater — the easy warm ones after a meal, Vee the one holding on a beat past where a hug stopped and the beat after that; the goodbyes, continental and generous. That was Randi's friendship, only Randi being Randi, as Vee spent part of every day remembering.

The dance floor had been a joke — Randi's joke, got up for a boy named Harrison — and Vee was the one who had taken it too far, her mouth, her hands, the whole of her going where it had no business going, and Randi had laughed it off and not let it be a thing between them, had handed the night back folded and easy.

Vee was grateful past saying. She treasured what the two of them had, and she knew Randi did too. It would be a tragedy, an actual one, if her sex brain went and wrecked something that good.

This was not one of those hugs.

Randi stepped back with both hands still on Vee's arms, and the relief went through her first. The other thing came right behind it, and she could not have said which of the two she'd have picked.

"I hate this," Randi said. "I'd so much rather be across a table from you, hearing all about that man of yours." Her mouth went rueful and her eyes didn't; her eyes had a gleam in them, bright and private, the look of a woman on her way to something she was going to enjoy. "But duty calls." She squeezed Vee's arms once. "A girl needs a spanking!"

Vee laughed. It came out a half-beat late and a note too high, and under the laugh her body had already heard the word and gone where the word went: a kitchen, a wet hand, the small sound of it and the sting that wasn't small; a bare bottom and a bet she'd lost; Randi across a table with her head tipped asking *did you deserve it*, and herself saying *probably*, and Randi's smile going down through her like a swallow of something warm.

All of that in the time it took to laugh. Heat, low and sudden, and the shame right there on top of it, and the shame did nothing to put the heat out.

"Go," she said. "Be kind to the girl in trouble."

"It's not just my decision," Randi said. "But she'll get what's coming to her, I'm sure." Her hand came up and put a piece of Vee's hair back behind her ear, and Vee felt it all the way down. "Saturday, gorgeous."

Then she was off down the corridor toward the cold, quick heels on the waxed floor, her coat swinging, and Vee stood where she'd been left with her own coat open and watched her go.

She put her hands in her coat pockets against the cold, and her fingers closed on the roll there — her own, the wintergreen, forgotten since she'd set it ready at the start of the hour — and she did not take it out, and she went out the other way.

---

She crossed back over the Drillfield in the dark and the cold, the walk she had made a hundred times to go and run these meetings, and made it tonight a step slower than she had ever made it in her life. She was never behind herself.

She had left the warm room a few minutes late because she had stood too long in a hug she had not wanted to end, and the warm shape of the girl was still on her, all down the front of her, and she carried it the whole way like a thing she would not be let keep.

She did not hurry to catch the time back. She did not slow further either. She went because not going was the one move that would make the house look harder at her, and she had spent four months seeing to it that no one did. Reluctance she could feel. It was just that it had nowhere to go.

Randi let herself in out of the cold and the house closed its noise around her. The center of it ran too warm, the way it always did — the common room going, somebody's speaker, the heat cranked up for the girls in leggings, a candle lit for no reason anyone could name — and the corners of it ran cold, the way they always did, the old house never once able to hold an even degree anywhere in itself, so that there was no room in it a person could simply be comfortable.

Nobody looked up when she came in, which was its own kind of information. She went for the stairs.

"—up for review tonight, did you hear? Standards."

"Who?"

"I don't know — somebody had a guy up in her room, is what I heard. After hours. Never signed him in, nothing." A little thrilled by it. "Brooke's running it herself."

She had her foot on the first stair and she did not slow down. A guy up in her room, after hours. They had the shape of it and not one true piece — the hours wrong, the girl wrong, the guest a guy, which it had never once been — and they did not know whose case they were carrying up the stairwell. Then the second girl said, "God, I'd hate to be her," and laughed, and Randi went up.

The Standards room was the small meeting room off the second-floor landing, and the door was open, and the light was on, and they were already in it. That was the first wrong thing.

She was never the last to a Standards meeting. She ran them. She came early and set the water out and took the seat at the head with the window behind her, and tonight the seat at the head with the window behind it had Brooke in it, and the chair Randi sat in — the board chair, the one on the near side, angled to the room — had a sophomore named Caroline in it with a legal pad, and the only chair left was the one that faced them all, the one the girl under review sat in, pulled a little out from the wall so there was nothing behind it or beside it, and a glass of water set out on the table in front of it by someone who was not Randi.

Somebody closed the door. She heard it go behind her, the latch, and did not turn around to see who.

"Randi." Warm the way she was always warm, a voice she had like other people had a scent — but she did not get up, and she did not say sit; she let the one open chair do that. "Thank you for coming up."

Randi sat. She put her hands in her lap and left the water where it was.

"You know the procedure better than anyone at this table," Brooke said, and there was the rueful half-beat, gone as soon as it came: the chair of Standards was Randi, and a sister did not sit her own board. "So you'll let me keep it properly. Caroline is seated in your place and will take the minutes. There is a written complaint, and it cites the standard. I'll put each part of it to you in turn, and you'll answer to each as you wish." She let that settle, unhurried, pleasant. "We'll observe the forms. I think you'd want us to."

She would have. That was the thing. It was, near enough to the word, how Randi called a board to order herself — the alternate seated, the complaint named, the standard cited, the matter put part by part — and she sat and heard the order of service she had built read back to her by a woman who kept it a half-degree cleaner than she did, and found nothing anywhere in it to stand on and refuse, because there was nothing wrong with the form.

The form was correct. Correct was the whole of what it was for.

"Of course," Randi said.

The brightness went out of her voice, and what came in under it was lower, and worried. Randi couldn't say where the sorority ended and the girl began in Brooke, not now, not ever. "I didn't want this to be a docket," Brooke said. "I want you to know I held it off as long as I could."

Brooke drew the papers from a fresh manila folder — Randi's name on the label — and set them neatly on top of it. The folder was new, and it had been opened for her, and her name was on the tab; Randi took that in before she took in a word of what was on the page.

She had kept folders like it on other girls, a dozen of them, had sat in the chair Brooke was sitting in with the girl across from her going pale, and she had learned, as Brooke plainly had, not to look at the page while she spoke. You did not need to look at it. You had it.

"The complaint speaks to a sister's conduct this year," Brooke said, and read from it without appearing to read, "grown — their word — *loose. And sloppy.*" The warmth stayed on the words, which was worse than if it had gone. "We take these one at a time, before a thing becomes a pattern. That is what tonight is."

---

The page had dates on it. Brooke read the dates.

The first ones were guests. A guest signed in at no hour anyone could find, one October morning, and then the same guest again in January, both times before ten, and the January one gone up to a member's room with the door shut and the member not signing the book either time, and a goodbye that a sister happened to see, downstairs, under the composites, that had gone on past the length of a goodbye.

She kept her face still and let the one morning open behind it, just the once: the olive coat done up to the throat and the wrecked face over it, Vee on the front step at nine in the morning with her whole plane ride still on her, needing her, and Randi bringing her up the stairs fast and away from the eyes and shutting the door and getting her arms around her before either of them had said a word.

Downstairs, under those framed dead-eyed faces, later, the girl had turned around with her hand already on the door and come back and taken Randi's face in both hands. *That girl had kissed her.* That was the goodbye a sister happened to see. She let it close.

"These are just attendance-adjacent," Brooke said, kindly, as if excusing it. "Guests are a courtesy thing. We note them."

"All right," Randi said.

---

Then the overnights. The page had those too — a count of them, going back to the fall, nights the board could establish Randi had not slept in the house and had filed no overnight card with the president, which the bylaws asked a sister to do, which no sister had done since the bylaw was written, which Randi herself had never once done and had never once been asked about, until the dates of it were a column down a page in a folder with her name on it.

"That's a lot of nights, honey," Brooke said. Not unkind. Reaching, almost, offering her the easy out: *a boy, isn't it.* She did not say it. She had learned, since the kitchen in January, not to hand Randi the safe answer.

Randi looked at the column of dates and she was, for a moment, somewhere else entirely — a bright cold common in late August, a closed ring of her own people on the grass, and a man she had never seen walking up into the middle of it with nothing, no name any of them knew, no car they'd have looked at twice, a face you would not have picked out of a crowd.

The ring had gone cool at him the way the ring went cool at anyone it had not already approved, and she had watched him not mind it. He had not pushed. He had made himself small and useful and funny, set a joke up so that Sloane got to land it and shone landing it, and inside fifteen minutes the ring was his and did not quite know how he'd done it.

Then he had turned to her, and his voice had gone down a register, just for her, and asked her to dinner that night, and she had said yes — not because he was anything the ring valued, he was nothing it valued, but because she had just watched a man walk straight through the whole apparatus she lived inside and want not one thing it was selling.

She had driven them to dinner. She had driven him home after. That part she had never told the house, and would not be telling it now.

"I don't have anything to say about the nights," Randi said.

---

There was a line on the page that was not a guest or a night. It was a man, and what he did.

"He's a graduate student," Brooke said, and let it sit a second, warm, so that the room could feel for itself the weight of what was coming. "An instructor. At the university." She said *instructor* the way you'd say a word you were being very careful with. "And I think we'd all just feel more comfortable — it doesn't look right, is all. A sister and an instructor. However it started. You see how it looks."

He taught one section of one course that had nothing to do with Randi and nothing to do with the redhead and never had. Brooke knew that; Brooke had sat Randi down the morning after the first night and gone all the way around to it, light as anything, whether he taught her section, and had gone limp with relief when the answer was no. The relief had not survived into February.

The rule did not care whose section. The rule said *instructor,* and the word did the work, and nobody at the table was going to be the one to say that a man who drove a fifteen-year-old pickup and sewed his own shirts was not exactly what the word was built to keep out.

---

And then the mixer. They had saved it for the end — you ran the witnesses last, when the room was warm for them, and she had run them that way herself — and for the mixer they had two.

Sloane went first, and Sloane told it mostly straight, in the breathless way Sloane had of making a thing sound worse by being so careful to be fair about it: the dancing had gotten, you know, a lot, and the skirt was tiny, and then at the end there'd been a whole — kiss, like a real one, in front of everybody, and she wasn't saying anything by it, she just thought the board should know it happened.

Kinsey went second, and Kinsey had been waiting all her life to go second at something, and Kinsey had Randi's hand up inside the back of the redhead's bra and down the front of her skirt, and the redhead's name, which Kinsey did not have, so it was *the redhead* the whole way through, *her hand right up under, I saw it,* and the room took a small breath.

That was the one Randi could have stood up and killed. It would have taken a sentence. *My hand was on the outside of her skirt, Kinsey, I had a handful of her ass for a boy named Harrison and the whole floor watching, there was never anything under anything* — and the second she said it she would have been telling them, in front of Caroline's moving pen, exactly how far it had gone and exactly how well she remembered, which was to the inch.

So she let Kinsey have the bra. She let her have the hand up under. She sat with her face arranged and let the worse and falser version stand, because the true one was the one she could not afford to own.

And under the arranged face, the true one ran anyway, the way it had run for five days: the floor, and the song breaking, and Vee reeled in by the wrists, and the held inch she had made herself take, a breath short of the girl's mouth, long enough that it stopped being a joke and they both felt it stop; and then the kiss, which she had sold to the room and to Vee and to herself afterward as a show for a boy neither of them could have picked out of a lineup; and the one thing she had not sold anyone, because she had not said it out loud even alone, which was that she had meant it. She had meant all of it.

That was the part no witness in this room would ever have, and the only part that was true.

"Thank you, Kinsey," Brooke said.

"Do you dispute any of it?" Brooke said. Gently. The door held open one more time. "Any of the particulars — the dates, the account of the mixer. Anything you'd want Caroline to note."

Randi thought about the particulars. She knew every lever on that page; she had pulled most of them herself at one time or another. She could dispute the bra. She could dispute the overnight bylaw as a dead letter nobody enforced. She could make the whole thing take two hours and leave with half of it struck.

"No," she said. "I don't dispute any of it."

Caroline wrote that down.

---

They sent her out to the landing while they decided, which was the part Randi had always liked least, from the other side of it — she had never believed it was necessary, sending the girl out, and had done it every time because the ritual wanted the girl to stand alone somewhere and hear the murmur of her own case through a door and not the words.

Now she stood where the girls stood. The murmur came through the door, Brooke's voice carrying its warmth even through two inches of oak and saying nothing she could make out.

The house went on below and around her, and it had opinions, and the opinions came up the stairwell in pieces the way they always did.

"—remember when she made that freshman cry? The one who—"

"—oh my God, over the *recruitment* thing, yes—"

"—put Hannah on probation for basically nothing, so—"

"—so maybe it's a little, you know. What goes around."

Lauren Pike's name went by in there somewhere, soft, the way you'd say the name of someone who used to live here and didn't anymore, and Randi had sat on the board that heard Lauren Pike, had voted the way the board voted, had walked past the emptied room without slowing down. She knew exactly how her own name sounded going up a stairwell. She had made other names sound like that.

She opened her clutch to do her mouth before they called her back, because you went back in with your face right, that was simply true, and the lipstick turned in her cold fingers and went out of her hand and dropped, and the runner took it without a sound. She looked at it lying there in the carpet, the gold tube in the long dim nap of the hall runner, and she did not bend for it, because the door was opening.

She went back in without it.

---

The decision was the one she would have handed down herself, which was the last joke of the night and the one nobody in the room could see. Thursdays, all of them, mandatory, through the end of the semester — the dinners gone, the one standing hour of her week that was hers. Attendance probation on top of it.

And the part Brooke read last, in the gentle voice, watching her: that a further lapse — any further lapse — would cost her the seat on the board and the single room the seat had earned her. The governed door. The one door in this house she got to lock from the inside.

"We really do just want to see you doing well," Brooke said, and folded the page in half.

Randi said that she understood. Her face held. It had held for twenty-one years and it held now, and nobody at the table saw anything but a sister taking her medicine like a good one.

"And listen." Brooke's voice dropped into something warmer still, something almost tender, the *can I ask* face coming up one more time. "None of this has to go on anything. The record, I mean. It's all still soft right now, it's all still just us." She let that open, generous, a door swinging wide.

"If you wanted to come to chapter — just stand up, just briefly — and say you'd heard the concerns, and that you were going to put a little distance between yourself and the, the situations that caused them. The friend. The gentleman. That's all. And this all just goes away, and it's like we never had to have tonight at all."

So there it was, laid out neat on the table between them, the thing the whole night had been built to reach: clemency, for a sentence. All she had to do was stand up in front of ninety girls and set them both down — the man she had driven home from dinner in August, the girl who had come up her stairs wrecked and kissed her under the composites — call them *situations,* and walk out clean.

"No," Randi said. "Thank you. But no."

Brooke looked at her for a moment, and something moved behind the warmth and did not surface, and then the warmth closed back over it, smooth, complete.

"All right, honey," she said, and that was all, and she did not ask again, and she did not wait for a reason, the way she never waited; she was already slipping the papers back into the folder and turning to Caroline about something else, and the meeting was letting out around Randi where she sat, the sisters rising and gathering their phones, the ordinary warm noise of the house coming back into the room.

Randi stayed in the chair a moment longer, the one pulled out from the wall with nothing beside it, and then she got up and went to find her lipstick in the hall.
