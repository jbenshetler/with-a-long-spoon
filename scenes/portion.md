# Portion

*[SCENE] · Pace's POV, close third · Sunday evening, the day of Randi's brunch with Vee · Pace's kitchen, then his bed. The woman is **Randi**; Vee does not appear — they talk about her.*

---

The pot had been on since three. Chicken and potatoes in a white sauce, the peas green in it and the carrot orange, the wine gone into it early and the cream at the end. The house had smelled of it all afternoon. He had a loaf out of the machine cooling on the board and the salad washed and waiting in the colander, the dressing in a jar he would shake at the last minute, and the bottle on the table was the one the pot had drunk from, a white, open an hour. He had set out two bowls. Hers was the small one, the one he sliced her apples into.

He had put on a henley and jeans. The synth thing she liked was going low in the front rooms, a dark voice over machines, every sound in it polished. He poured her glass when he heard the gravel.

The deadbolt went over. He came out of the kitchen and she was setting her bag down on the chair by the door, and he saw that and set it aside. Then the little back-and-forth they did, his backing off and her leaning in, her mouth cool from outside and her perfume arriving after it, the citrus and the cold thing under it. She had her hands on his chest. She let him go and lifted her face and the kitchen reached her.

"You cooked." A beat. "You shouldn't have."

He pulled out her chair.

She sat, and he set the glass in front of her and she picked it up and drank, and he went to the stove and ladled her bowl and brought it to her, the small one, and filled his own, and shook the jar and dressed the salad and set that between them with the bread, and sat down across from her at the little table with his back to the stove.

She took the spoon. She drew it through the sauce, not down into the bowl, along the top of it, and tasted what it brought up, and put the spoon down on the cloth beside the bowl.

"It's wonderful," she said.

He heard it and did not know where to put it. She did not say things like that to him. She said them to waiters, to girls in her house on the phone, to Vee, he would have bet, all day long; in his kitchen she had never once told him a thing was wonderful, and the food had been better than this. He tore the end off the loaf and put it on her plate. She did not touch it.

"How was this morning?"

"Good." She turned the glass by the stem, a quarter turn, and back. "She's happy."

He waited. He did not ask what Vee had said. That was Vee's.

"She told me about Friday."

It went through him and came up in his face before he could do anything about it, and he did not try. Friday, and her driving home Saturday with no bag, and by Sunday morning happy enough to sit across from her friend and tell it. He could see it. He was glad all the way down.

Randi watched him be glad. She did not say anything else about it. She took a fork to the salad and ate a leaf of it, and then another, and the bowl in front of her did not move.

He ate. The stew was good. He watched her fork go to the salad and come back and go to the salad, and her bowl sat where he had put it with the one line drawn through the top of the sauce, and he noticed it, and left it.

"How long, Pace?"

He looked up. "How long what?"

"Vee." She set the fork down. "How long are you going to take?"

"As long as she needs." He said it plainly. "She's not ready."

"She's not ready." The words came back at him flat, with nothing on them, and he had never heard her voice do that. "Or you're not."

"I want her to get there on her own."

"On her own." A small nod, as if he had confirmed something. "With you."

"That's how it works."

"That's how it works for you."

He put his spoon down.

"You've got her Friday to Sunday," Randi said. Her hands were flat on the cloth on either side of the bowl she had not eaten from, the nails bare, the white-gold band at her wrist. "You've got her all weekend, every weekend, and you're in no hurry, and I can't think why you would be."

"It isn't for me."

"Isn't it?"

He looked at her. Her face was doing very little. The blue of her eyes was on him and level, and the thing at the corner of her mouth that came a beat late when she was holding something was not there, because she was not holding anything. It was out.

"It's selfish, Pace."

He felt it land. Not where she had aimed it; somewhere to the side of that, in the place that had knelt an hour at a pedestal not touching a girl who had been wet through her clothes for him, and finished the hem. He heard his own voice go exact.

"How do I get her from where she is to us, on your schedule?"

She did not answer.

"She chooses it, Randi. Every step of it. Or it isn't anything."

He heard how it had come out. He did not take it back, because it was true, and because he did not know how.

She reached across the table and took the bottle by the neck and poured her own glass, a full one, and set the bottle down. He had poured every glass she had drunk in this house. He watched her hand do it and it went in somewhere and stayed, and he could not have said where.

She ate the salad. All of it, down to the leaves at the bottom that had taken the most of the dressing, with the second glass, and when it was gone she laid the fork across the plate and sat back. The bowl had not moved. He finished his own, because it was in front of him and he was who he was, and it did not taste like anything.

She got up. She did not come around the table and put her mouth on his. She went out of the kitchen and he heard her heels go down the hall and around the corner, and he sat a moment with the two bowls, and then he got up and followed.

She was already out of the skirt. She had him out of the henley and down onto the bed before he had said her name, and up over him, and she did not do the thing she did, the inch and the stop and the eyes. She put him inside her in one motion and drove. Her eyes were shut. Her nails were in his chest. She did not say *tell me*. Every night in this house she had said it, at his ear, against his mouth, and he lay under her with his hands on her hips and waited for it and it did not come. After a while he understood it was not going to, and that whatever this was, it was not for him. He knew that much and not the rest, and he gave it to her anyway. She was quick about it. She rode him hard and fast with her eyes shut and her hair down over her face and came loud, her forehead dropping to his shoulder, and he was a breath behind her, his hands locked on her, and then it was done.

She went off him at once.

No staying down. The chill took her, as it did, and she slid off him and found the cashmere blanket at the foot of the bed and rolled herself in it and lay on her side with her back to him, and did not say anything, and did not move.

He lay there. He went over it. The speed of her, and the eyes shut, and no tell me, and now the back. He had all of it. He set each piece beside the next and they would not go together into anything he could name; it was the want, he thought, with an edge on it tonight, from somewhere. He did not put his hand in her hair. He lay behind her and listened to her breathing not change for a long time, and then change.

When it had, he got up.

The kitchen was as they had left it. The pot on the stove with most of it still in it. Her bowl on the table where he had set it down, the sauce gone to a skin over the peas and the carrot, the one line she had drawn through it still there. He looked at it. He did not know what he had done. He looked for it, back through the evening from the gravel forward, and it was not there.

He covered the bowl with a plate and put it in the refrigerator, for her, and turned the light off over the stove.
