# School Nights

*Vee POV. Mid November, a school night.*

She came in past eleven with the night still on her, the warmth of his car and the warmth of the evening before that, and the thin cold off the window found her at the door as it always did. Cassie kept it cracked its two inches against the building's cooking, and Vee came into that cold lit up and didn't feel it.

Cassie was folded into the bean bag with a book against her knees. "You're doing the streetlight thing again," she said, not looking up. "You're a public utility."

"I am not."

Cassie turned the book facedown and looked, the dry look held a beat and then gave over, glad. "Go on, then."

And Vee went on, because there was no holding it once Cassie opened the door. She spun her desk chair around and sat in it backward with her arms along the top, and gave Cassie not any one night but the shape her weeks had taken, the nights all running into each other. How he cooked with intent, something different every time and never out of a box, things she had no name for and a few she did. How he watched her eat it like that was the show. How he noticed things before she did. She told her about the baked tomatoes — breadcrumbs, cheese, fresh herbs. So good she'd gone greedy, leaned in for the next before she'd swallowed the last, and one slid off the fork and caught her.

Her hand went to the spot as she told it, the side of her breast, without her knowing it moved.

He'd pressed a cold cloth to it before she'd got the first swear out, gentle as anything, bent over her.

"There's only one way *that* burn happens," Cassie said, wry. "Not enough on."

But Vee was already past it, already on to how he'd fussed over the burn for days, like the small hurt was a big thing. And how the house stayed warm now, warm enough that she never once thought about how much she had on.

Cassie listened with no face on at all and let Vee talk herself out. Then she said, "You don't come home school nights."

Not a question. Vee opened her mouth to argue it and Cassie just waited, and Vee went back over the week she hadn't counted and found her own bed had sat empty more nights than it hadn't. "It's not every night," she said.

"Mm," said Cassie, which was Cassie for whatever you say. "I don't get it." Kindly, though. "You've had boyfriends. You never went like this over one."

And Vee tried to say it, and what came was the small word, fine. The ones before him had been fine. Nice enough, a good enough time, and she'd gone along thinking that was the whole of what there was to have. Pace wasn't fine. She reached for the word after it and didn't have one, and laughed at herself reaching. She put both hands up at all of it instead, at the cooking and the watching and the whole of himself turned on her until the rest of the room went dark around her.

Cassie watched her run clean out of words, which Vee never did. She let it sit. "Do you love him," she said, the same flat way she'd said the rest of it.

And Vee, who hadn't stopped talking since the door, heard herself go small. "Maybe," she said. "I think so." And then, because that still wasn't the size of it, "It's all so fast."

Cassie let the maybe stand. She didn't say a thing against it, and she didn't say I know, only went back to her book. "Go to sleep, streetlight," she said. "Some of us have class."

---

The pool at four on a Tuesday was a different building than the pool on a Saturday afternoon. The bleachers were folded back against the wall and empty, the big room had its echo to itself, and somebody had run the lane lines out across the water so the whole surface went striped and orderly, blue and white, each lane a corridor with a black stripe laid down the floor of it.

She took the third one in from the wall. Not by any arrangement anybody had made. She'd taken it her first week back and kept taking it, and by now the man who swam butterfly badly and the woman with the good flip turn arranged themselves around her without a word ever passing between the three of them about who went where.

She pulled the cap on at the edge, tucking the red up under it in the two motions it took, and stood a second on the wet tile in the old smell of chlorine and floor cleaner. The green-and-white suit had nowhere left to go on her and hadn't for years. She looked at the water instead of at that. Seventy-five minutes on the clock, and she had not come here to stand.

She never had to decide what to swim, any more than she had ever once decided the second verse of a hymn. You were just already in it. Four hundred easy to start. Then the long middle, which was the same long middle it had been since she was twelve, broken into pieces with the clock for a fence around each one. Then whatever she had left.

She went in off the wall.

The first hundred was always a small argument, the body objecting on principle, and then it stopped objecting and the thing turned over and ran. Breathe every third. Hand in front of the shoulder, not past it. The black line came under her and went. At the wall she rolled and drove off it and the wall went behind her, and then it came again, and she rolled again, and the room and the lights and the flat boom of the space folded down into a strip of blue about a yard wide, her own breathing, and the water going over the back of her neck with every stroke.

*Do you love him.*

It got in off the third wall, whole, in Cassie's voice, flat the way she'd said it with her book still open on her chest.

Vee had said *maybe.* She had said *I think so,* and then, because that had not been the size of it either, she had said *it's all so fast,* and Cassie had let the maybe stand and gone back to her book and told her to go to sleep, streetlight. And in the dark afterward Vee had lain there running the sentence over, with the answer sitting plainly in her, easy to reach, easy to say, and no idea in the world why it had come out of her mouth as *maybe.*

She took it into the set with her. There was nowhere else to take it. Her own bed had been empty more nights than not, which she had not known until Cassie said it flat and left her to go back over the week and do the counting herself, and there was the thing with her hairbrush and her earrings and her book scattered down his one clean counter, which he had looked at and said nothing whatever about, and which she had been turning over since Saturday. *Did he mind.* All of it came in and got in the lane with her.

Off the wall. Breathe every third.

*Do you love him.* The wall, and the roll, and the black line coming under her again. *Did he mind.* Breathe every third.

By the fourth piece she was working too hard to hold the words on them. *Do you love him* was a pressure under her ribs with a dread riding it, and *did he mind* was nothing at all, and the count was there whether she wanted it or not. Sixty-one, sixty-two. Hand in front of the shoulder.

By the sixth she had *him*, and the ache, and the count.

Then the count.

She was strong through the middle. Not fast — she had not been fast in years and did not care — but the engine went and went and asked her for nothing. Her legs were doing something honest. Her hands knew where to be. Nobody was counting but her and nothing was riding on it, and her body, which had opinions all day long every day of her life, had none at all down here. It only worked.

Around the seventh she got up into the good part, the part where it goes quiet and wide, and the thing about Pace turned over once in the quiet and she could not tell, for about four strokes, whether the man she was turning over was Pace or was somebody she had built out of wanting him — whether he was that good or she had made him that good, out of wanting, out of two months of being looked at like that. Four strokes was all it got. The wall came, and she rolled, and the next hundred was already going.

She swam the set out. She had a little left at the end and spent it, badly, and touched the wall with her lungs going and hung there on the gutter with her heart banging while the room came back — the lights, the boom, the lifeguard's radio, the butterfly man's slapping.

Her legs had gone loose under her. She left them that way, and the water moved them around without asking her anything about it, and she let it, and her breath came down by degrees until it was only breathing again.

Cap off, and a rinse in the pool showers to get the chlorine before it set into her hair. The hot water came down over the back of her neck and she stood under it a while with her eyes shut. It never answered anything, the swimming. That was the whole use of it. It took the words off things one at a time until what was left was the shape underneath, and a shape you could carry around all evening without it asking you for anything.

Sweats over skin that was still damp and would stay damp. Out through the double doors into the cold with her wet hair up and her ears ringing pleasantly and her arms feeling like they belonged to somebody generous.

The cold got into her hair and she walked faster. There was the rest of the evening to get through and a stats reading she had not touched and a sandwich to think about first. She thought about the sandwich. Her head was as quiet as it had been all week, and she went up the hill in the dark with it that way, and did not go looking for it.
