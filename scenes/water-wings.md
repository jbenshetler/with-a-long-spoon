# Water Wings

*Early fall. Vee's POV. The desirability anchor — her body in the world, before the machinery. Vee + Cassie at the university pool.*

---

The rec pool made you wear a real suit — no gym shorts, no sports bra, a lifeguard at the desk to check — and Cassie fought her way into hers like it had insulted her family. "Who designed this? Genuinely. What man?" The suit was navy and plain and older than their friendship, and she wore it because it had a job to do and not because she liked it. Cassie did not go to a pool to be admired; she'd sooner put her boxy frame in a t-shirt and cut-offs. The suit didn't do her any favors. Cassie knew that, and it didn't bother her one bit.

Vee stood on the wet tile in the old smell of chlorine and floor cleaner, already in her green-and-white one-piece — the one she'd worn at sixteen, for synchronized swimming, when there'd been less of her to fill it. There was more of her now. That was the trouble with Vee's suit: it fit, and it gave her nowhere to hide.

Cassie banged her locker shut and they headed out, Cassie looping the stretchy wristband with the locker key over her wrist.

Vee rolled her towel tight from one end, her hands still knowing how, and carried it down at her side — not up against her. She put her face on: chin level, shoulders open, the walk of a girl who had *meant* to arrive. The only way to the water ran the length of the bleachers, so she took it like an entrance.

It came about halfway down, lobbed easy: "You won't need floaties with *those*!" — the bleachers breaking up behind it, the laugh that's really the guys applauding each other. Vee gave it a long blink, half her mouth pursing, less than a grimace. Then, further along, lazy: "Legs for *days*." That one got the eyeroll, unbothered, aimed at the ceiling.

Cassie said it low, just for the two of them, still walking. "Tossers." A beat. "They will never know the love of a woman." And Vee laughed — more than the joke had earned, the game face breaking on it, fifty yards of held breath going out at once. Cassie could have pitched it up into the bleachers; she had the lungs, used them all rec season. She'd kept it for Vee.

Cassie waded to the middle of her ribs and stopped, planted, arms spread along the surface like a woman leaning on a fence. This was as far as Cassie went, and the water knew it. "Go on," she said, chin at the deep. "I'll be right here. Standing. Like a person." Vee set the rolled towel on the bench and went on — the floor sloping away, tile giving to a colder smoothness, and then the dark stripe painted across the bottom where the shallow gave out. She crossed it, let her feet lose the floor, took the breath she'd taken ten thousand times, and went under.

The room switched off. The applause-laughter, the lifeguard's radio, the flat boom of the big space, Cassie — all of it gone in the swallowing green, and there was only the cold coming up from below and the light coming down through the surface in slow sheets and her own heartbeat, close and steady. She hung with nothing holding her and nothing to hold. Weightless the way you only are down here. Something in her chest that had stood at attention all afternoon quietly sat down.

Her hands found the old shape before she asked them to — the slow scull, palms feathering the little figure-eight that keeps you down and turned and yours — and she let them, and turned, and looked up at the bright moving ceiling of the surface from underneath. Nobody up there could see her. Nobody down here wanted anything. She had loved this before she had a body to be a problem, and the water was the one part of it that had never been taken. She stayed until her lungs asked, and a beat past that, in the quiet and the old uncomplicated belonging, and grieved, a little.

She came up. The surface broke and the room came back with it — light and noise and chlorine and the whole business of having a body in a place where people were — and she blinked it into focus, a little slow, still half in the green.

Cassie was watching her. Not hard, not asking; just seeing that her friend had gone off somewhere and come back with her face on wrong. Cassie never asked — asking was for people who wanted the sad version to get bigger. What she did — from ten feet off, without moving a step closer — was sink her cupped hands to the wrists and drive them forward, fast: the trick that *shoots* it, a low sheet of water leaping the whole gap to break light over Vee's head.

It barely got her. That was the genius of it — outrage out of all proportion to the actual amount of water, which was the point. Vee blinked it off and then she was coming, crossing the ten feet of deep in a few strokes she didn't have to think about, into the shallow where she could get her feet and both arms under it and give it back for real. Cassie took the wave full in the chest and did not so much as change expression, which was somehow the funniest thing in the world, and Vee heard herself make a sound she hadn't made in a while — the shriek-laugh, the ugly one — and dug in and got her again. Whatever she'd carried up from the bottom didn't survive it. You can't grieve and fight your best friend for a mouthful of pool at the same time; the splashing won. It always did, back before any of it. The body that was such a problem stayed up on the deck with the rolled towel, where it could wait.

---

The towel was where she'd left it on the bench, still rolled. Cassie was dressed before Vee had wrung her hair out. Cassie did locker rooms like a fire drill. "Meet you back," she said, hair dripping down the back of her shirt, and went, and the door let a draft in from the hall and sighed shut on it.

Vee took the end shower, the one with real pressure, and turned it as hot as it would go. The chlorine came out of her hair in no hurry, and she didn't hurry it.

He'd asked her at the end of the walk last night, at the corner where she turned off for Vawter. Saturday, he'd said — would she have dinner with him. She'd had the yes most of the way out, she could still feel the shape of it in her mouth, when he said, "Don't answer tonight."

That was all. No reason after it. He'd smiled like it was a perfectly normal thing to ask of a person and gone off with his hands in his pockets, and she'd stood on the corner with her mouth still open on the yes.

Who did that? Who asked a girl out and then told her not to answer?

She shut the water off and sat on the bench in her towel with her sweats folded beside her and didn't put them on. A wet curl had come down over one eye. She caught it and wound it around her finger, snug, and let go, and it sprang halfway back up her cheek, and she caught it again.

He wasn't gorgeous. She'd decided that on the walk, trying to be fair about it. You'd pass him in the bookstore and not look up. A regular face, hair cut short by somebody who wasn't trying to sell him anything, and young. Put him in a hoodie and he could have been any boy from the dorms, hauling a laundry bag down to the basement. But every boy she'd ever gone out with sat down like a dropped backpack, and he'd folded into that chair at the coffee shop like he'd measured it first. He'd helped her with her calc problem, gently, and just enough. He'd let her talk about her research project for five whole minutes and then asked the one question that made it sound like a real project. He'd held the froyo door open with his back.

A grad student. She'd never gone out with one. She'd never really talked to one who wasn't standing at the front of a section.

Odd. He was odd. She liked it more than she'd have admitted to anybody, and she hadn't admitted it to anybody.

The air was cold on her shoulders where the towel stopped. The rest of her had kept the heat of the shower and wasn't giving it back. His hands, the knuckles thick on them. At the coffee shop she'd wondered what he did with them, besides math. She was wondering it again now, in a towel, on a wet bench, which was really not the place.

She got her sweats on pretty fast after that.

Outside it had gone late-afternoon gold, and the wind found her wet hair and went straight through to her scalp. She walked with her hands pulled up into her sleeves. At the crosswalk she took her phone out and opened the thread — just his one text, still asking whether she liked frozen yogurt — and her thumb hung over the keys while the light went to WALK, and back.
