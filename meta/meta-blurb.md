## Test-epub blurb (Volume 1 · test readers · ~Aug 2026 · decided, not final)

> Two people have planned the best year of her life. She doesn't know there's a plan.
>
> Vivienne "Vee" Thorne is twenty-one, a college junior, and quietly starving. The men have been fine. The friendships have been fine. Fine has never once been enough. So when her glamorous new friend, Randi, steers her — lightly, laughing, as if by accident — into the path of a young mathematician named Pace, Vee walks toward him because everything in her has been waiting to.
>
> Pace is nothing like the boys she has known. He sees Vee completely, and under that gaze the shame she has carried since girlhood turns, impossibly, into heat. Courtly, exact, he asks before he takes and leaves her the room to choose, until choosing becomes the most erotic thing she has ever done. What grows between them is the best of her life, and every step of it she gives freely, gladly, with her whole body. Over lunches too intimate to name, she tells Randi everything — and Randi listens, and draws her further open, and welcomes her into a world she'd only ever watched from outside. And Vee never lets herself notice how much she grows to want Randi, too.
>
> What Vee cannot see is that the invitation reached her long before she knew there was one — that it began as a game between the two people now closest to her, a secret choice made about her. >
> Warm, wickedly explicit, and psychologically merciless in its tenderness, *A Polite Invitation* follows a young woman all the way inside an appetite that can only give her the best of herself by taking it — and lets the reader, ahead of her from the start, fall for it exactly as she does.
>

>
> *Book One of* With a Long Spoon. *Literary erotica for readers of Anne Rice's* Beauty *trilogy who want it contemporary and psychologically exact — a seduction at full heat and full tenderness, a woman waking to a man and, without quite letting herself know it, wanting the woman beside her, where every open door is a temptation and every step is hers.*

## Blurb design goals — the yardstick (change only intentionally)

*What the current Test-epub blurb (above) is built to do, recorded 2026-08-09 so any future change is a deliberate decision, not drift. Grade a revision against these before shipping it.*

**Attract the right reader**
- Sell the positive position: warm, sexy, character-driven, explicit, full-consent.
- Perform (don't label) strong interiority and rich characterization — the literary claim and the retention promise ("it's actually *written*").
- Signal shame as a psychological engine — the sex has stakes and meaning, not just mechanics.
- Carry the appetite/hunger theme (food as metaphor; the title is an eating proverb).
- Speak to the *Fifty Shades*-dissatisfied reader: consent *as* the eroticism (its inversion), and explicit-on-the-page (the word plus the *Beauty* comp). The jacket confirms this reader; ad/social copy — where *Fifty Shades* may be named — acquires them.

**Repel the wrong reader (prevent 1-star mismatch reviews)**
- Filter out pure-stroke seekers via interiority + literary voice, never a disclaimer.
- Signal the girl-girl attraction reader-side (Vee doesn't see it), to warn the f/f-averse.
- One clear dark cue; no HEA-shaped language. Residual risk is the erotic-romance crossover, handled by the dark cue + the *Beauty* comp — not by darkening the warmth (erotica and literary fiction both license the non-HEA ending; only romance punishes it).
- Say "explicit" plainly.

**Be legible to someone who knows nothing of the book**
- Name the three principals unambiguously — **Vee** (protagonist), **Randi** (her glamorous new friend), **Pace** (the mathematician) — with no character carried on a bare pronoun. (This fixes the original blurb's mathematician confusion, the reason for the rewrite.)

**Make no promise the book doesn't keep**
- No threesome promise (Volume Three payoff); no school named; the deception is a game that turned real on all three (keeps Randi and Pace off the predator read); consent as her agency (the freely-given yes is the point).

**Stay true to the book's structure**
- Dramatic irony, not mystery — the reader is ahead of Vee from the start; reader complicity is the hook.
- Warmth leads, the trap felt underneath; never cold or sinister.
- Never disclose the ending; no thesis vocabulary a first-time reader can't decode; no heat overclaiming.

## Test-epub assembly (Volume 1 · packaging ~Aug 2026 · spec for the build pipeline)

Reading order and placement — an epub has no back cover, so the blurb lives in front matter:

1. **Cover** — owned by `meta-cover.md` (Cover text section): three elements, series line / volume title dominant / author; no tagline on the front — it closes the blurb page instead.
2. **Blurb page** — first page after the cover, *before* the title page (simulates the retail listing). Layout: the two blurb paragraphs → white space → the tagline **centered, alone** (the eye's last stop) → white space → the italic Book One / *Beauty* comp line, smallest type, bottom.
3. **Title page** — title, *Book One*, author.
4. **Copyright page** — boilerplate plus one line only: *"An erotic novel, for adult readers."* No itemized content-warning list for the test round (the blurb self-selects; a CW list telegraphs). Revisit for wide release.
5. **Chapter one** — nothing between front matter and the bench: no epigraph, no note-to-readers, no priming.
6. **Back matter** — *Note to test readers*: thanks + feedback questions. Asks live here, never up front (first read unshaped, reflection directed). Core questions: *did you keep falling for Pace and enjoying the brunches even though you knew?* / *was there a point where the warmth curdled?*
7. **Metadata** — description field: the explicit short ("Miranda has everything…"); series: With a Long Spoon, Book 1; title: "With a Long Spoon: Book One" (whether volumes get their own titles, Beauty-style, is open — not needed for the test).

Deliberately absent: elevator pitch and unused taglines (query/ad material, not product); any mention of trilogy total scope ("Book One" says it all).

## Genre & positioning (decided 2026-07)

**Shelf: erotica / dark erotic fiction.** "Literary" is the differentiating adjective, never the category. The acquisition/retention split governs everything: **acquire like erotica** (cover, blurb, category, chapter one — chapter one is the delivery mechanism and needs no overclaiming), **retain like literature** (the word-of-mouth this book earns is "it's actually *written*"). The anti–Fifty Shades argument — consensual, meticulous, hot because of the relationship, not despite it — only works in front of that book's readers; shelving away from them defeats the corrective.

**Never romance — including dark romance.** Romance carries a hard HEA/HFN reader contract (Happily Ever After / Happy For Now — the central couple survives the last page, however dark the middle); the closed ending violates it and that shelf punishes contract violations. Erotica licenses tragedy.

**Amazon category strategy (recorded 2026-08-02): survive the classification, don't evade it.** The explicitness is beyond what the literary-fiction shelf carries (*Acts of Service* would pass a rated-R cut; this book would not), and Amazon re-categorizes on content review and reader reports — so plan for landing in the erotica classification regardless of shelving intent, and make the book **illegible as pulp within it** through packaging: literary trade-dress, the no-single-comp blurb, comp-line register. *Beauty* is the existence proof — decades in Amazon's erotica categories, confused with its shelf-neighbors by no one; category is where you are, packaging is what you are. **The ad/browse suppression cost of the erotica category is accepted, because category browse delivers nothing anyway:** the author ran the experiment (hours searching the Amazon erotica shelves, motivated ideal reader, ~2026-08 — found nothing worth a second visit and would have bounced in minutes; dated here because it will read as opinion later). Discovery for this book runs on **curation, not category**: the author found *Beauty* 35 years ago because a Texas university-bookstore buyer stocked it as fiction — the modern equivalents are the recommendation layer (Goodreads lists, subreddits, BookTok, "books like X but explicit" roundups) and the channels the author's own years-long search mapped (Literotica et al. — that search history is the market map of where these readers congregate and fail to find the thing). Amazon is fulfillment, not discovery. Open mechanics (currently permitted category pairs, adult-filter line in practice, browse-viable niches inside the erotica tree) belong to the ALLi-grade platform consult (`meta-plan-lawyer.md`, two-track team).

**Series framing:** presents as **Book One of a trilogy** — three continuous Beauty-shaped volumes, each with a designed curtain — never as one 350–400k novel. Retail copy sells Volume 1's arc plus the one open door.

**Volume titles (decided 2026-07-30): each volume carries its own title, Beauty-pattern** — the series relationship rides the series line and metadata, never a bare "Book One" subtitle (a titled volume claims its own arc and protects the closed curtain). **Volume One: *A Polite Invitation*.** Surface: warm, mannered, clean — no erotica-category pattern-match. Charge (post-curtain): the apparatus of Volume One *is* politeness — asking-before-taking, the flawless social surface, the engineered collision the redhead apologizes for; the courtesy was the costume. Chosen over *Come Hungry* (the come-pun shelf-signals low-end genre) and *An Open Invitation* ("open" beside a third-in-their-bed blurb pattern-matches open-relationship/ménage category — wrong readers in, right readers out). Title-engine caveat knowingly spent: "polite" carries a faint eyebrow at the cover moment; it promises the category of pleasure, no plot fact. **The trilogy titles are one dinner in three phases, a single register climbing in temperature (Vol 2 decided 2026-08-21):** *A Polite Invitation* → ***A Warm Reception*** (Volume Two, **decided**) → ***A Hot Meal*** (Volume Three, **working**; alternate ***A Proper Serving***). Two arcs run at once — the dinner phases (invitation → reception → meal) and the temperature (polite → warm → hot) — and on reread the rising warmth *is* the cooking: the reader takes the climbing heat for deepening romance, when it is the guest of honor being brought up to temperature (the tenderness is the oven). Each title holds the Vol 1 trick: an innocuous, mannered domestic surface detonating on reread. *A Warm Reception* — surface, a gracious welcome; charge, she is *received* = taken in (and *taken in* = deceived), the warmth is the heat rising, and "a warm reception" is also what you call walking into hostile fire. *A Hot Meal* — surface, a wholesome hot dinner (the thing you make for someone you love); charge, she is the meal, served hot and eaten, nurture inverted into predation. **Vol 3 fork, preserved:** *A Hot Meal* completes the temperature ramp and is content-true to the hottest volume (the courtly veneer burns off to raw heat); *A Proper Serving* trades the temperature for a manners bookend (*polite … proper* — the courtesy that never drops, arguably truer to "merciless in its tenderness") and a richer noun (*serving* = a portion, the act of service, a comeuppance dealt), at the cost of the ramp and with a cooler, less erotic retail surface. **Rejected for Vol 2:** *A Place Setting* (cutlery, not a gesture — inert where *Invitation* is a relational act, one person to another), *Table Setting* (flat noun), *A Standing Invitation* (a second "Invitation" flattens the set), *A Fine Hunger* ("fine" is this book's word for starvation-in-disguise — inverting it on the cover breaks the key; "hunger" shelf-signals genre). Note the Vol-1 telegraph objection to table/meal titles dissolves from Vol 2 on: the reader has known the configuration since chapter one, so a title names the dread, not the machinery. **Cover rendering of the title is owned by `meta-cover.md`** (Cover text section: three-element hierarchy, no tagline on the front). Retail title field: *A Polite Invitation (With a Long Spoon, Book 1)*.

## Craft discipline (obey in every blurb)

- **Foreground the plan; protect the landing.** The design — Randi and Pace are lovers, and they have chosen Vee and are drawing her in — is open from chapter one. The book runs on dramatic irony (dread, not mystery); the reader stands ahead of Vee. Naming *the plan* is the *hook*, not a spoiler — but the **consummated threesome is a Volume Three payoff, never promised on the Volume One/Two jacket** (author ruling 2026-08-09): foreground the choosing and the steering, not a "third in the bed" this volume does not deliver. What recontextualizes is the *ending* — the inversion of who sees, the fused good/harm verdict, the orphaned wound. Never disclose how it lands.
- **Warmth leads; the trap is felt underneath.** The friendship, the love, the tenderness are the truest and strongest things in the book — lead with them. The configuration is the hook, but it must never read cold or sinister (the blurb obeys earn-the-dark too): the reader feels the warmth first and the trap beneath it, never gets sold the machine. "Two people run a con on a third" is the inverted, wrong blurb.
- **Both seducers serve Vee's needs — and never push.** Pace opens doors and beckons; he never coerces — his attention serves her *safety and pleasure* and makes her feel **seen** ("seen" is Pace's word, never Randi's). Randi *elevates and listens* — she makes Vee feel *chosen*. Both make her feel special and chosen; the service is the seduction, not a softening of it. Never write Pace as acting without asking, and frame the deception as a *game* that turned real on all three — Randi and Pace trapped as surely as Vivienne (keeps them off the predator read).
- **End on the tightening, not the consequence.** Close on the deepening/exposure; never on catastrophe, the threesome's aftermath, or who is destroyed.
- **Never name the appetite.** Show aliveness (the girl who laughs with her whole body); don't explain it. The contained-girl-points-at-the-unguarded-one image carries the thesis silently. Naming it kills it — same earn-the-dark-by-being-light rule as the prose.
- **Pace is older in bearing, not in years** (≈22–23 to Randi's 21). Render him as out of phase with campus — a math PhD candidate who started college at fifteen, living alone off the highway — never as a May-December "older lover."
- **One dark cue per retail blurb; no HEA-shaped (happily-ever-after) language ever.** Every retail-length blurb carries one unmistakable this-does-not-end-well cue so romance-expectation readers self-select out before they can feel betrayed — a tagline does it ("Every yes was freely given. That was the problem." — "trap" was retired 2026-08-09 for implying a malice the book refuses). Compatible with "end on the tightening": the cue is the tightening, never the consequence.
- **The names are play and promise.** Formal names on the jacket (**Miranda**, **Vivienne Thorne**); filed-down names in the book (Randi, Vee).
