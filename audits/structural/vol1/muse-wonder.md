LETTER
Dear Helen,

The strongest spine in this volume is not the one the jacket advertises. Vee's movement from bodily shame to chosen exposure is built patiently and pays off: the girl who cannot eat comfortably, cannot be seen in a swimsuit without management, and hears her mother's vocabulary inside her own desire ends by taking the long step at the induction and giving the room her leg on purpose. The dress gives that change an object, a chronology and a public test. By the time she chooses the step, we understand both what it costs and why it exhilarates her. That arc can satisfy a reader.

The advertised central build cannot yet do the same. Randi and Pace choose Vee in Chapter 3, Randi cultivates her, Pace courts her, Randi extracts increasingly intimate reports, and Pace explicitly decides in Chapter 16 not to tell Vee about the existing relationship or the intention behind her recruitment. From there the triangle's pressure is repeatedly approached and privately discharged: Randi questions Pace in Chapter 19, enjoys Vee's confidence in Chapter 18, and finally steals and impersonates a message in Chapter 25. But Vee ends the volume knowing no more about the design than she did when she met Pace. The climactic discovery, confrontation, refusal or consent—the event toward which the whole mechanism has been pointing—does not occur. What closes instead is the dress plot. That is a real payoff, but it is not the payoff promised by “Randi and Pace are lovers, and they have planned the best year of Vivienne Thorne's life.”

This matters commercially as well as structurally. A separately sold first volume can end on a hook, but it still needs a consequential turn inside its own covers. Chapter 25 is an effective violation and a good hook: Randi uses Pace's phone, sends “The way that dress hugs your tits makes me wish I was a dress,” then deletes the evidence. Yet because Vee never learns of it, no relationship changes on the page. Randi has crossed a line only the reader and Randi know she crossed. The ending therefore promises a future explosion rather than delivering a present one. I would urge you either to bring one irreversible revelation or choice by Vee into this volume, or to reposition the volume's promise so that the induction is unmistakably its climax rather than the dress rehearsal for the real plot.

The middle is where momentum most often leaks. Vee's courtship is sensuously detailed, but its scenes frequently repeat the same transaction: Pace attends, waits, asks permission, feeds or clothes her; Vee discovers that surrender is pleasurable; she reports the discovery; Randi converts the report into further intimacy. The Randi lunches in Chapters 9, 15 and 18 are especially similar in shape—Randi offers a comic sexual anecdote, draws out Vee's latest experience, names the desire Vee is avoiding, and leaves her grateful for being understood. Each contains good material, but together they make the extraction mechanism feel procedural rather than escalating. Pace and Randi's “Tell me” scenes likewise recur in Chapters 6, 11, 16 and 22 without each new report sufficiently altering what either will do next. The result is a long central plateau between selection and induction, rich in texture but short on irreversible events.

Pace is the largest characterization risk. He is twenty-two, a nearly finished mathematics PhD, a patented inventor, a competitive powerlifter, a furniture maker, a tailor, a cook, a patient listener and an almost infallibly calibrated lover. Individually these are vivid; accumulated, they cease to create pressure because no limitation meaningfully inconveniences him. More damaging, the narration repeatedly asks us to admire his perception while requiring him not to perceive Randi. He notices the pressure of a thumbnail, a changed laugh and a hand held too long around a cup, yet after her jealousy, sexual urgency, uneaten meals and direct challenge—“You've got her Friday to Sunday”—he still concludes that he cannot find what he did wrong. His ethical position is clearly stated: “She chooses it, Randi. Every step of it. Or it isn't anything.” But because he neither tells Vee nor seriously revises the plan, that principle functions as a rationale for continued concealment. If his blindness is deliberate self-deception, the volume needs a moment where the cost of maintaining it registers more sharply.

Randi, by contrast, has the book's most volatile inner life, but her escalation is often signaled through repeated physical tells rather than new decisions: the thumb under a nail, the arranged smile, the hand at the water glass, the unfinished meal. Chapter 22's completed plate is powerful precisely because it breaks that pattern. I wish there were more breaks of that scale earlier. Her desire for Vee, jealousy of Pace, hunger, social fear and need to control the timetable are all present, yet they tend to circle the same scene of erotic reporting. The phone theft works because she finally acts. She needs an earlier act with comparable consequence.

The supporting world is vivid in flashes but thin where pressure should come from. Cassie is excellent—funny, observant and morally useful without becoming a lecture—but the narrative increasingly routes around her so that Randi can occupy every confessional space. Brooke is established as a formidable social intelligence in Chapter 2 and then used mainly as an offstage reason Randi cannot attend events. Meg, Kayla, Sloane and Kinsey supply convincing social weather and then vanish. Pace's university, finances and family are similarly suggestive rather than grounded; the patent explains the money after the question has become conspicuous. Daphne and Pace's scar are opened late and left deliberately untouched, but alongside the bench, the undisclosed relationship and the planned threesome, they increase the number of secrets the reader is carrying without a corresponding return in this volume.

The opening is bold but disorienting in a way worth examining. Chapter 1 gives us a virtuoso erotic scene between Pace and an initially unnamed woman, reveals “There you are, Randi,” and only later introduces the jacket's apparent protagonist. That delayed identification is clever; it also means the reader invests first in a relationship whose terms remain partially withheld for twenty-five chapters. Vee does not enter until Chapter 3, and her point of view does not govern until Chapter 4. If she is the volume's center, she arrives late. If Randi is, the ending's refusal to give Randi a witnessed consequence is even more frustrating.

None of this should obscure what readers will love: the patience of the erotic attention, the food and clothing as languages of permission, Cassie's dry counterweight, and the way Vee's pleasure keeps complicating rather than abolishing her shame. Protect those. The revision I am asking for is not more incident everywhere; it is sharper differentiation among incidents that currently rhyme, and one decisive turn that makes this volume's ending land in Vee's life, not only in Randi's phone.
