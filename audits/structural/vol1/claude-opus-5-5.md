LETTER

Dear Helen,

A Polite Invitation has an unusual and strong design. You give the reader the plan in chapters 1 to 3, before Vee is in it. After that, every Vee chapter plays against what the reader already knows. Randi's "Paul?" (Ch 9), her hand going to her coffee cup while Pace "discovers" Vee (Ch 5), the two towels on the rod (Ch 13): these work because the reader is ahead of Vee. Keep that. My concerns are about what the volume does with the advantage, and where it ends.

The biggest issue is what pays off and who is in the room when it does. The jacket sells a triangle: the lovers' plan, the wish Vee won't admit to wanting Randi, and "how real it will get for each of them." What actually pays off inside this book is the Pace–Vee romance: first kiss (8), first night (17), the dress (20). It also pays off Vee's arc out of shame, which closes on the long step across the stage (24). That arc is well built and truly earned. But the induction happens with neither lover present. Pace is in Berkeley, Randi is at Brooke's meeting, and only Cassie sees it. Worse, the three principals are never in a room together after Chapter 5. The triangle the jacket sells moves forward only in parallel two-person scenes: Randi with Vee at meals, Pace with Randi in bed, Pace with Vee everywhere else. A reader who bought Book One for the triangle gets a well-handled romance and a twist ending. The twist (Ch 25) is a terrific hook, but it is a setup for Book Two. It is not the payoff of Book One. I'd ask you to find one scene, late in the book, where all three share space and the plan visibly bends.

Second, the retellings. Again and again an event is dramatized and then retold, sometimes twice. The dinner (8) is retold to Cassie and to Randi (9). The hike (10) is retold to Cassie at once and to Meg and Kayla two chapters later (12). The fitting (14) is retold to Cassie and to Randi (15). The first night (17) is retold to Cassie and to Randi (18). Pace's absence from the induction is explained in 23 and explained again in 24. The Randi retellings mostly earn their place, because what Vee leaves out and what Randi pulls out of her is the real scene. The Cassie retellings and Chapter 12 mostly don't. Together they make the stretch from Chapter 12 to the first half of 15 the slackest in the book.

Third, Randi. The jacket's emotional core is that Vee "is the first woman Randi has ever let herself want." Randi has POV in Chapters 1, 2, 6 and 25, and nowhere between 6 and 25. Her falling for Vee, her jealousy of Pace's Friday, and her sudden exit at brunch ("Hold that thought, honey") all reach us through Pace, who misreads them, or through Vee, who explains them away. That is clever, and in Chapter 22 it pays off beautifully. But Chapter 19, the most important fight in the book, reads as opaque as well as painful, because the reader is as lost as Pace is. Decide whether you want the reader in the dark there, or only Pace.

Fourth, repetition. The four Randi–Vee meals (4, 9, 15, 18) follow nearly one template. There is a glamorous room, Vee reads the prices first, Randi tells a sex story she has invented, Randi draws out Vee's confession, and they part with a hug or kiss. The kisses escalate, which helps. The invented "my disaster date" opener appears in three of the four. The goodbye hug in 4 and 9 is written in nearly the same words. One of these meals needs to break the pattern.

Fifth, Pace. His consent ethic is the book's eroticism: the safeword, "May I choose?", "Don't answer tonight." But he never feels that ethic grind against the lie he is living with Vee. In Chapter 16 he decides not to tell her, on aesthetic grounds ("You did not tell someone what a gift was"). The Daphne conversation in 23 gives you a powerful mirror: he can't see a harm done to him, and he can't see the one he is doing. Make sure the book can see both, even if Pace can't. He is also very heavily endowed with gifts: PhD at 22, an MRI patent, furniture, tailoring, cooking, powerlifting. Meanwhile his world is nearly empty. There is no department, no colleague we see, no gym scene. Brooke's question about whether he teaches (Ch 2) goes nowhere.

Smaller matters are in the findings. Datelines don't match their content in 9, 15, 18 and 21. Chapter 6 opens in a confusing time frame. The phone passcode is Vee's birthday, which is a big reveal with no setup on Pace's side. Cassie's suspicion is set up and then dropped.

What to protect: the dramatic irony; the Pace–Randi bedroom scenes as a barometer of the relationship (the deadbolt, the bag on the chair, "Tell me," staying or leaving); the food motif; the fitting set piece; Kentucky as Randi's tell; and the ending.

With warm regard,
Your editor
