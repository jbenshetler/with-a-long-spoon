LETTER

This volume has a compelling psychological engine, an unusually coherent language of appetite, and a central dramatic irony strong enough to sustain a series. Vee believes she has stumbled into the two relationships she needs; the reader knows Randi and Pace selected her. The material is most powerful when an apparently benign act—ordering dinner, choosing shoes, asking a question—simultaneously gives Vee agency and advances a design she cannot see. Protect that doubleness.

The largest structural problem is that Book One ends one movement too early. Chapters 13–20 invest heavily in the burgundy dress, the induction, and Vee’s imagined entrance into a watching room. The dress is completed and the shoes are bought, but the public event never arrives. Meanwhile, the conspiracy established in Chapter 3 never comes appreciably closer to exposure. Chapters 19–22 therefore provide mounting intimacy without a corresponding turn in danger: Vee wears the dress privately, Randi helps complete the outfit, Randi and Pace settle into tenderness, and Vee learns Pace’s history. The final sentence—“She had never been so fortunate, or so happy”—has dramatic irony, but it confirms a condition the reader already understands rather than changing it. For a separately sold volume, the ending needs a more decisive culmination or destabilization: the induction, a consequential Randi slip, Cassie acting on suspicion, a crack between Randi and Pace, or another event that makes the next volume necessary rather than merely desirable.

Relatedly, the secret game remains structurally static. The reader learns “For us” in Chapter 3; through Chapter 22 Vee remains nowhere near understanding that her friendship, the coffee-shop meeting, and the reports of her sex life were engineered. Her attraction to Randi escalates, and Randi’s jealousy becomes real, but neither development puts the original deception under enough pressure. The premise promises that freely given choices will become a problem. This volume supplies many choices and very little consequence.

Randi provides the best source of escalation, especially in Chapter 18, where she calls Pace selfish and challenges his timetable. That rupture is excellent because it exposes the incompatible desires inside the plan: Pace wants Vee to choose at his pace; Randi wants access and is hurt by exclusion. But the book does not dramatize the bridge from that confrontation to Chapter 21’s domestic tenderness. By then Randi can confess that Vee “got past” her, ask to be fed, eat a full plate, and stay the night. Those changes are meaningful, but the missing relational reckoning makes them feel partly unearned.

The middle also repeats its principal scene shape too often. Vee experiences something with Pace, tells Cassie a partial version, then tells Randi a fuller version over food while Randi draws out increasingly intimate details. This is thematically apt—Randi is conducting the second seduction through disclosure—but Chapters 9, 15, and 17 perform close variants, and Chapters 12 and 20 add further recounting and wardrobe discussion. The later instances need either more resistance, a mistake, an external interruption with consequences, or less page space.

Vee’s transformation is the strongest completed arc. She moves from covering herself, portioning appetite, and treating desire as evidence against her to choosing food, exposure, sex, color, and display. Her agency is repeatedly shown through action rather than declared. Her growing attraction to Randi, however, needs one more interior beat between explaining away the first kiss in Chapter 15 and actively meeting the second in Chapter 17. Denial is the intended state, but psychologically exact denial still requires pressure, rationalization, or recoil.

Pace’s attraction is convincing; his total competence is less so. In rapid succession he is a mathematical prodigy, patent-holder, powerlifter, woodworker, tailor, cook, sexually exact dominant, and near-perfect listener. His blind spots about Randi and the ethics of the game could counterbalance this, but the narration often endorses his self-understanding rather than bringing those blind spots into sharper relief. Decide whether he is meant to be an erotic ideal whose flaw is concealed control, or a psychologically realistic man who is genuinely mistaken. The current balance sometimes weakens friction precisely where the plot needs it.

The setting is vivid at scene level: campus paths, dorm rooms, restaurants, the mountain, the workshop, and the warm house all stand firmly. The institutional world becomes thinner in the second half, however. Randi’s Standards role, Brooke’s authority, Vee’s academic life, and Pace’s doctorate mostly stop exerting pressure once the triangle moves into private houses and restaurants. Bringing one of those external systems into collision with the secret would enlarge the stakes without sacrificing intimacy.

What must remain is the book’s sensory and thematic architecture: food as permission, clothing as self-construction, looking as both danger and gift, and consent as an action rather than a slogan. Cassie’s plain loyalty is also essential ballast. The volume already contains a powerful first movement. It needs its final movement to convert accumulated happiness into irreversible story.
