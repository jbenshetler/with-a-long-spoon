LETTER

Dear Helen,

A Warm Reception has a better architecture than most second volumes. It runs from a jealous outburst in October to a reconciliation on a January doorstep. The relationship question builds slowly. The dramatic irony is real, and the volume ends on a strong emotional peak. Randi's arc carries the most weight in the book. Below are the problems I would fix first, most important first.

1. The long middle has no pressure. From the apology in Chapter 26 to the crackers sentence in Chapter 38, Vee is happy, and nothing outside her pushes back. Discovery after discovery (the kitchen swat, the bet, the costumes, the photograph, the drawer) is shown, then retold to Randi, then enjoyed. The tension in this stretch rests on the reader knowing what Vee doesn't, and you remind us of that only twice: Chapter 25 and Chapter 35. Between those two chapters there are nine Vee chapters in a row with nothing new on the conspiracy. The "no name" thread, which carries the last third, is barely sown in the middle ("He never said," Ch. 32; "There was never a note," Ch. 33). I'd like that unease planted earlier and in more places. I'd also like a few more glimpses of what Randi is doing with what Vee tells her, so the irony keeps getting refreshed.

2. Vee never thinks about kissing Randi. In Chapter 45 she goes back from the door and kisses Randi "until the thing in her chest had somewhere to go." It is the boldest thing she does toward Randi in the whole volume. Chapter 46 then gives us a whole evening inside her head and never mentions it. It could be denial or it could be an oversight, and the page doesn't tell us which. If it's denial, show her pushing it away. One beat would do.

3. Pace's scruples never get tested against the deception. He says "She consents — every time — or I'm done" (Ch. 35). Yet Vee doesn't know her confidences go straight to him, and he plays along when she introduces Randi as a new friend (Ch. 32: "Sure."). His three POV chapters cover fear, grief and desire, but not guilt. A reader who likes Pace will notice that the book never makes him look at what he's doing.

4. The ending. The volume's central question is the unsaid word. Vee cuts it off at "Vee, I l—", and then the reconciliation happens through sex and pancakes, which is exactly the "believe what I do" deal she rejected in Chapter 41. That reversal may be the point, and it's a strong one, since Randi's coaching ("Words are the whole war") sends Vee back into Pace's own philosophy. But the text should know it's an irony, and right now I can't tell whether it does. Then the last page belongs to Cassie's "Hmm." The real hooks are Brooke's ultimatum, Randi's new hope, the Valentine's dance, and Sheri, who knows both women. All of them sit in Chapters 44–45, ahead of a long climax that lets them fade. Think about bringing one of them back on the last page.

5. Too many things are retold word for word. The blonde's lines appear three times (Chapters 23, 24 and 26), and Chapters 24 and 25 retell the Rusty Nail night two more ways. Two meal-epiphany metaphors restate a thesis the book has already proved. Two bathroom scenes end with the same cold-tap-on-the-wrists beat. Three reunion chapters (32, 37, 46) each open with the same "they couldn't make it to the bed" sequence. Chapter 40's lines about the name and her thumb over the text field come back almost word for word in Chapters 39 and 43. Each time something comes back, it should come back changed.

6. Some big turns happen offstage or as summary. Randi and Pace stop seeing each other for six weeks after Chapter 35, and we learn it in one paragraph of Chapter 44. Daphne, the reason Pace can't say the word, arrives as backstory dumped in the middle of a crisis, after no setup anywhere in this volume.

7. The volume sells separately, but it doesn't orient a new reader. "Peter," the panties from the first night, the cherries, the pedestal, the bench: all go unexplained here. A newcomer will be lost in places.

Things to protect: Randi's arc. The way you build the sex scenes so they carry information (Chapters 25, 29, 35, 46). The way Vee's displays of her body escalate. The goodbye kiss that gets a little longer each time. The paired Chapters 42 and 43, which show the same text exchange from both ends. And the small planted payoffs: the outlier lecture, the squared fork, the pastry glaze. Don't trim any of these away to solve pacing problems somewhere else.

With admiration,
Your editor

SEVERITY: major
CLAIM: For twelve chapters nothing outside Vee threatens her, and the dramatic irony that should supply the tension is refreshed only once (Ch. 35) after it is set up in Ch. 25.
