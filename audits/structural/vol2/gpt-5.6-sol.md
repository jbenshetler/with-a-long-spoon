LETTER

This is a psychologically rich middle volume with a strong governing design: Vee learns to inhabit appetite, Pace gives love in every form except language, and Randi’s calculated seduction becomes an attachment she can no longer control. The dance lesson in Chapter 23 gives the book its structural vocabulary—leading, following, bracing, yielding—and the subsequent shifts among bodies, food, clothing, and disclosure keep returning to that vocabulary. The volume’s central problem is not lack of material but accumulation: too many scenes demonstrate the same emotional fact before the story forces that fact to change.

The most important issue is the ending’s relationship to the volume’s central question. Chapters 38–41 make Vee’s need for a name unavoidable. “Words are cheap, and you still won't spend them on me” (Chapter 41) is the clearest statement of the wound. Chapter 44 then reveals that Pace understands both the wound and his own fear. By Chapter 46 he is apparently ready to cross the threshold—“Vee, I l—”—but Vee interrupts him with the coat reveal, sex follows, and the narration concludes that “nothing needed saying now.” This is potent tragic irony if the intended ending is Vee abandoning a legitimate need under Randi’s manipulation. But the chapter’s warmth and completion also invite the reader to experience it as a true reconciliation. Because this volume sells separately, the distinction matters. At present, the plot resolves the sexual separation while leaving the actual relationship conflict untouched, then emotionally scores that non-resolution as fulfillment.

The long middle, especially Chapters 29–37, needs compression. Its repeating engine is excellent once or twice: Vee has a new erotic experience with Pace; domestic food and aftercare convert it into belonging; Vee tells Randi; Randi eroticizes or relays it; Pace and Randi use Vee’s disclosures in their own sex. But repeated at full length, the pattern loses escalation. Chapters 29, 31, 34, and 35 circulate substantially the same spanking material. Chapters 29, 32, 33, and 37 each use sex followed by cooking or a domestic gift to show that Pace treasures Vee. Chapter 30’s costume display is sensuous and thematically apt, but it changes little after Chapter 29 has already established Vee’s new appetite and willingness to be seen. The explicitness is not the drag; duplication of function is.

The secret triangle also remains too safe for too long. The jacket promises that every confidence travels farther than Vee knows and that this has begun to cost all three of them. The cost is real inside Randi—especially in Chapters 35 and 42—but discovery risk barely escalates. Pace and Randi exchange intimate information without a consequential slip, contradiction, near-discovery, or demand for accountability. Pace’s statement, “She consents — every time — or I'm done” (Chapter 35), exposes a useful moral blind spot: Vee consents to each act she knows about, not to being selected jointly or having her confidences circulated. The volume needs to decide whether that distinction is a central dramatic indictment or merely background irony.

Vee and Randi change substantially. Vee’s induction walk in Chapter 28 is especially valuable because her public self-possession belongs to her rather than to either lover. Randi’s movement from operator to frightened participant is likewise earned across Chapters 25, 35, 42, and 45. Pace’s change is less fully prepared. His Daphne history arrives in Chapter 44 as a concentrated explanation after his refusal has already driven the major rupture. Earlier hints establish that he “doesn't go back,” but they point toward family, not toward a formative romantic loss. The explanation is persuasive, yet its late, summarized delivery makes it feel supplied to unlock the climax rather than grown through the volume.

The secondary threads are promising but incompletely integrated. The PPP/MIRA discovery in Chapter 36 opens a major family-corruption thread and demonstrates Vee’s perceptiveness and loyalty, then vanishes. Brooke’s “You need to choose” in Chapter 45 finally gives Randi an external consequence, but Chapter 46 leaves that pressure behind. Either is a viable next-volume setup; together, introduced this late, they compete with the primary climax without affecting it.

The world is vivid whenever the book enters a specific institution—the dance hall, induction, statistics project, sorority house, ski resort, diner—but Chapters 29–35 narrow heavily to Pace’s house and brunch tables. The recurrence supports the cocoon Vee is entering, yet the academic, financial, and social life she risks losing becomes less visible precisely when her dependence grows. Cassie usefully restores perspective; Meg disappears after opening the volume, and Sheri and Brooke vanish for long stretches before returning to carry major structural work.

Protect the dance vocabulary, the food/appetite motif, Vee’s bodily humor, and the exactness with which desire alters self-perception. The book’s finest achievement is that pleasure, shame, nourishment, secrecy, and class are never separate subjects. The revision task is to preserve that density while making each recurrence advance the triangle toward a consequence the previous recurrence could not have produced.
