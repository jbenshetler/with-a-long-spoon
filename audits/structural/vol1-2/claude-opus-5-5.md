LETTER

Dear Helen,

Book Two is hotter than Book One and more confident. Several of its set pieces are the best writing in the series so far: the spanking that starts by accident at the sink, the induction walk, the Halloween "costumes," the holiday chapters that give each of the three a cold bed in turn, and the doorstep ending. The dramatic irony the jacket promises really does run the book. The reader is a step ahead of Vee in nearly every scene with Randi, and the two scenes where Randi relays Vee's confessions to Pace (25, 35) are the most charged in the volume. Protect all of that. My concerns are about shape. Most of them come down to the book running the same moves twice where it should be raising the stakes.

The biggest problem is that the volume circles. It opens with a rupture: Vee lashes out at the bar, Pace answers her plainly and goes still. She goes to Randi's room. Randi coaches her, lends her heels and a plan, and sends her to his door. She arrives, they have sex, he feeds her (23–26). The volume closes on the same sequence: rupture (41), Randi's room, Randi's heels, Randi's plan (45), the door, sex, pancakes (46). The second time round is bigger, since this time she's naked under the coat, but it is not a different event. A reader can feel the book going back to where it started. Between those two bookends, the "Vee accuses, Pace goes still" shape happens a third time at Chili's (39). I'd ask whether the closing reconciliation could break the pattern in some way: Vee not using Randi's plan, Randi's plan failing, or Pace moving first.

Second, the ending lets the central question go. The jacket and the second half of the book are built on one demand: say it. In 46 Pace starts to say it, "Vee, I l—", and Vee cuts him off. After that she never once thinks about what she nearly heard, and the chapter concludes "She had everything she had driven out through the cold to get." That isn't true to what she drove out for, and the narration doesn't seem to know it isn't. If you mean it as self-deception, make the reader feel the gap. If you don't, it reads as the book settling its main conflict with sex. The real hook for Book Three is Brooke's ultimatum at the end of 45, "You need to choose." It's buried one chapter before the end, and the volume's last beat is Cassie's "Hmm."

Third, the middle stalls. From 29 to 37 almost every chapter is the same evening: arrive, have sex, he cooks in loving detail, sometimes have sex again. Each one adds a gift (the scrunchies, the photo, the drawer, the flannel), and those gifts are lovely. But between the bet in 29 and the "crackers" sentence in 38, the relationship barely changes, and the conflict over the missing word isn't allowed to build. Interleaved with these evenings is a second loop: Vee confides in Randi, gets aroused, and Randi's goodbye kiss goes one notch further (24, 27, 31, 34, 45). That ladder is the right spine for the book, but twice the scene ends with Vee fleeing to a bathroom (27, 31), and three times it signs off "See you soon, gorgeous." I'd cut or merge at least two of the Pace evenings and one confession scene, and give the leftover space to consequences.

Fourth, the most important event between Randi and Pace happens offstage. In 35 Randi cries over Vee's green panties, Pace "let it be hormones," and she walks out. We only learn in 44 that she hasn't come back for six weeks. Her own chapter (42) is about John, and by her coda in 45 she has turned to Brooke and Vee. That rupture is the volume's biggest change in the three-way structure. Readers deserve to see it from Randi's side, or at least to be told it is happening while it happens.

Fifth, the deception never comes close to being found out. Pace frets about consent, "She consents — every time — or I'm done," while hiding from Vee that he's her friend's lover and that her confessions reach his bed. The only near-miss is 32, where Vee describes Randi's kiss to him and he mimes cheek kisses. The book never makes him face the contradiction, and nothing threatens to expose it. That keeps the stakes flat even as the heat rises.

Smaller points. Pace's chapter 44 spells out what the reader could already infer. Cassie's suspicion of Randi, set up in Book One, is dropped. Sheri's continuity has some snags. And Vee's misreading of Randi's accent comes up once too often. Details are below.

This book knows what it's doing scene by scene. What it needs is to stop doing the same things twice.
