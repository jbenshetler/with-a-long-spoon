LETTER

Volume two has a compelling emotional spine: Vee discovers that appetite can be ordinary, sustaining, and hers, then discovers that care without definition can still leave her starving. Her movement from the dance-floor rupture in chapter 23 to the direct demand in chapter 41 is earned. The problem is that the volume does not complete that movement. Vee asks the book’s governing question — “What am I to you?” — and the climax answers with sex, food, and care, the very forms of answer she has already declared insufficient. Pace nearly says the missing word in chapter 46, but Vee interrupts him; afterward he apologizes and says he missed her, not what they are. This can be an intentional relapse, but the chapter presents it primarily as triumphant reconciliation. A separately sold volume needs either a real change in the relationship or much clearer recognition that Vee has abandoned her stated need.

That abandonment is especially troubling because Randi scripts it. In chapter 45 she tells Vee, “You don’t do it with words. Words are the whole war,” then sends her to answer a dispute about language with her naked body. Vee executes the plan boldly, but it is still Randi’s plan, and it serves Randi and Pace’s concealed arrangement. Vee’s sexual agency is growing beautifully; her relational agency is being redirected whenever it threatens the triangle. The novel should know the difference more sharply than Vee does.

This leads to the largest series-level issue. Pace insists that Vee consents every time, yet neither he nor Randi confronts how much of her consent rests on withheld information. Randi extracts intimate disclosures, carries them to Pace, and eroticizes them with him. Vee explicitly believes Randi is the one person to whom she can safely show everything. The contradiction is rich and clearly intentional, but across two volumes it has barely advanced toward exposure. The conspirators’ feelings deepen; the ethical danger does not become materially harder to contain. Brooke’s ultimatum finally creates external pressure in chapter 45, but it concerns Randi’s status and attraction to Vee, not the central fact that Randi and Pace selected Vee together and remain lovers.

The middle of the volume is sensuous but structurally overfull. Chapters 26-35 repeatedly cycle through erotic discovery, food or domestic care, and an intimate debrief. Chapters 27 and 31 are particularly close in shape: Randi leads Vee through a sexual disclosure, Vee becomes physically overwhelmed, and Vee escapes to a bathroom. Each recurrence advances the temperature, but too many consecutive chapters perform adjacent work. Chapter 36’s statistics project is welcome partly because it is the first substantial interruption by the outside world. Bringing academic, financial, sorority, or friendship consequences into the earlier middle would give the erotic escalation resistance to work against.

Chapter 36 itself opens a potent thread and then drops it. The discovery that Randi’s family businesses are the worst visible outlier in the PPP data gives Vee knowledge Randi does not know she possesses. It could test their intimacy, Vee’s loyalty, Randi’s class performance, or the project itself. Instead it disappears for the remaining ten chapters. If it belongs to a later volume, volume two still needs a consequence from it now.

Randi has the strongest completed internal movement. Her encounters with Vee, the green panties, the emotionally empty fairytale with John, and Vee’s initiated kiss force her to admit that what she wants is no longer a game. Brooke’s “You need to choose” is the book’s cleanest next-volume hook. Unfortunately, the long erotic reconciliation that follows pushes that choice out of the reader’s foreground. Chapter 45 nearly supplies a stronger ending than chapter 46’s comic return to Cassie.

Pace’s realization in chapter 44 is likewise excellent but late. His recognition that he chose “the smaller truth” on the porch makes his refusal legible without excusing it. Yet the climax prevents him from acting on that realization. The volume therefore completes his diagnosis, not his change.

The long domestic and erotic scenes remain one of the series’ chief pleasures. Food, clothing, furniture, dancing, and sex all express the same governing idea: attention can be a form of love, control, and danger at once. Protect that. Also protect the concrete social world — the Rusty Nail, sorority house, statistics project, diner, family homes, and Gstaad — because the book is strongest whenever the private triangle is forced to exist inside institutions and class structures.

There is also one clear chronology error: the dance occurs before the Halloween chapter, but chapter 44 places it “at the start of November.” Fixing that will help an otherwise legible semester progression.

The revision priority is not less erotic content. It is making the erotic content carry more consequence: compress repeated discovery shapes, braid the outside threads through the middle, and let the climax answer — or consciously fail to answer — the question Vee has finally learned to ask.
