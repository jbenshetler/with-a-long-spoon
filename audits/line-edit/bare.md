The chapter is very close to finished, and its best writing is unusually clean of the faults below. The reversal at `:19` — "And it was warm because she was warm… the heat in the shirt was her own, given back. He wasn't in it." — is the whole vignette in three sentences, and the two short flat ones do the work the long one sets up. The scent inventory at `:17` earns its fragments ("The things he made with his hands. The rooms he kept warm for her. The plate he set down once he'd built the hunger in her.") because the list is *sorted*, ascending in intimacy, and then dismissed in one clause. The closing tricolon at `:21` — heap, Cassie, grey window — is perfectly weighted, and "she did not sleep" answering `:7`'s "and Vee, up top, did not" is a bookend that needs no help. ## Findings

1. **`:9` — "a girl too far gone in a boy, the look enough on its own."**
   Context: end of the shirt-in-the-hall paragraph, after "the mornings Cassie was up first and might look and wouldn't have to say anything."
   *Class: overwork.* "Might look and wouldn't have to say anything" already establishes that the look suffices; "the look enough on its own" restates it in the same breath, and it restates the weaker half — the appositive "a girl too far gone in a boy" is the fresh information and the sentence's real last beat.
   **Recommendation:** cut "the look enough on its own" and let the sentence end on the appositive.

2. **`:13` — "there was no text that made it come clear;"**
   Context: the middle of the four-part impossibility run — "nothing she could put in there… nothing she could ask him… You couldn't send a message and get out of a person the shape of what you were to them; there was no text that made it come clear; the ones she thought of were all small…"
   *Class: overwork.* Four consecutive assertions of one impossibility, and this is the flattest: it names no mechanism, no addressee, and no consequence, where the clauses on either side do all three. It also sits between the paragraph's most concrete formulation ("the shape of what you were to them") and its most damning ("would be answered small and warm, and would leave her exactly here"), diluting the step between them.
   **Recommendation:** cut the clause and let the semicolon join the message sentence directly to "the ones she thought of were all small."

3. **`:17` — "and she breathed it off her own shoulder and it was all of him" … "All of it was in the cloth, every last thing he did."**
   Context: four sentences apart, opening and closing the scent paragraph.
   *Class: within-chapter repetition.* The totalizing claim is made twice in the same paragraph in nearly the same terms ("all of him" / "All of it… every last thing he did"), and the second is doing double duty as the setup for the paragraph's real payload, "and none of it told her what she was to him." Because the intervening fragments have *enumerated* the totality, the reader does not need it re-asserted; the second occurrence reads as the sentence clearing its throat before the turn.
   **Recommendation:** cut "and it was all of him" from the first sentence, letting the shoulder-breath end plainly and reserving the totalizing claim for the position where it is immediately contradicted.

4. **`:19` — "and there was no name in her for it, not the big one and not the small one, only every breath of him now and the silence at the center where the word should be."**
   Context: the chapter's final interior sentence, closing the reversal paragraph.
   *Class: overwork.* "No name in her for it, not the big one and not the small one" is exact and concrete — it makes the reader supply *love* and *like* — and it has already landed the absence. "The silence at the center where the word should be" then explains the figure in abstraction after it works, and it is the third rendering of the same absence in two paragraphs (`:17` "none of it told her what she was to him"; `:19` "not the one thing he wouldn't say"). The paragraph is one beat late.
   **Recommendation:** end the sentence on the "big one / small one" clause, or on the breath, and drop the "silence at the center" coda.

5. **`:15` — "It wasn't enough, the shirt held off her skin over the other one."**
   *Class: word precision.* "Held off her skin" garden-paths: the reader takes "held off" as the phrasal verb (warded off) for a beat before the intended sense (held away *from* her skin) resolves, and the recovery costs the sentence its plainness at exactly the moment the chapter is at its most tactile. "Over the other one" then re-states a layering the previous paragraph already fixed in the reader's head.
   **Recommendation:** rephrase the clause so the shirt's separation from her skin is stated with an unambiguous preposition, and consider dropping "over the other one" as already known.

6. **Chapter-wide — the noun "thing."**
   Occurrences: `:9` "just a thing she wore," `:13` "the thing she needed," `:17` "the other thing" and "every last thing he did," `:19` "That was the thing she couldn't stop feeling" and "the one thing he wouldn't say," `:21` "where a dropped thing lands."
   *Class: word precision.* Seven in fifteen sentences. Several are load-bearing and should stay — `:17`'s "the other thing" is deliberate evasion (the smell she won't name yet), `:19`'s "the one thing he wouldn't say" is the chapter's subject, and `:21`'s "a dropped thing" is protected by triage. But the density is high enough that the strong ones lose force, and at least two are placeholders where a concrete noun is available: `:13`'s "the thing she needed" and `:19`'s "That was the thing she couldn't stop feeling once she'd felt it."
   **Recommendation:** replace one or two of the placeholder uses (`:13`, `:19`) with concrete nouns so the load-bearing ones read as chosen.
