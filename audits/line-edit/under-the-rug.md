## Findings

1. **`:7` — Overwork.** "She was afraid of the distance it would have to cross to reach him, and of the chance it came back empty — that she'd say it and he wouldn't, and she'd be left lying here having said it into a room that didn't say it back." The fear is rendered three times in one sentence: *came back empty*, then the em-dash gloss, then *a room that didn't say it back*. The figure lands on the first; the gloss explains it and the third restates it in plainer terms.
   → Cut one of the three renderings — most likely the em-dash gloss, which is the least figurative.

2. **`:15` / `:19` — Within-chapter repetition.** "in the warm handspan between his chest and her mouth" and, four lines later, "far too much voice for a man lying a handspan off." *Handspan* is distinctive enough that a reader feels it twice, and both uses do the same work (measuring the gap her volume violates).
   → Keep `:15`, which owns the image and is the more sensory of the two; vary the measure at `:19`.

3. **`:19` — Rhythm.** "She lay still and listened to herself not having whispered, and knew precisely how she'd sounded, and could not, having heard it, pretend she hadn't." The paragraph's true last beat is "listened to herself not having whispered" — a superb phrase. The two following clauses re-report it as knowledge, and the section-shaped stillness of the moment fights the long grammatical unwind.
   → End the paragraph on the whisper clause and drop the two trailing clauses.

4. **`:25` — Overwork.** "with no defense built into it and nothing in it to push against. She felt the thing she'd been winding up in her chest swing out, meet all that calm, and find nothing to catch on." The same observation — his answer offers no purchase — is made three times in two sentences (*no defense built into it*, *nothing to push against*, *nothing to catch on*).
   → Cut one of the first two abstract statements and let the swing-and-miss image carry it.

5. **`:29` — Overwork.** "a man handed a problem he knew how to solve and glad to be solving it, doing the thing she'd asked because she'd asked it." The final clause is the same characterization at a lower resolution than the clause before it.
   → Cut the trailing "doing the thing she'd asked because she'd asked it."

6. **`:33` / `:37` — Within-chapter repetition.** "She'd gotten what she wanted and couldn't find the part of herself that was supposed to be glad of it," then four lines on, "working to keep it light now that they were here and she'd gotten what she wanted." The phrase is the chapter's irony and it is spent twice in the same shape within a page.
   → Keep `:33`, which earns it as a paragraph close; cut or recast the clause at `:37`.

7. **`:41` — Word precision.** "something crossed his face that she had genuinely never seen there — not in all these months, not once —." *Genuinely* is an intensifier propping up a claim the appositive already proves absolutely, and it is a shade too modern-conversational for the narration around it.
   → Cut *genuinely* and let "not in all these months, not once" do the work alone.

8. **`:43` — Overwork.** "and he couldn't hold his eyes on her face for the length of one sentence." `:37` has just dramatized this in detail (the lift, the slip, the bringing-back, the fork). Having it re-reported as narration inside her conclusion flattens a paragraph whose power is that it's her *inference*, not the evidence.
   → Cut the clause; the accusation reads harder without its citation.

9. **`:45` — Overwork.** "She chewed. It was to food what a billboard is to a painting. And she thought, helpless, of how he cooked…" The billboard/painting simile announces the art comparison the palette figure is about to make concretely, so the palette arrives as illustration of something already said rather than as the thought itself. (Not a re-litigation of the palette sentence, which triage preserved and trimmed — the flag is on the pre-emption.)
   → Cut the billboard sentence and let "She chewed." run straight into the cooking.

10. **`:9` / `:45` — Within-chapter repetition.** "and this was the part with teeth in it" and "that was the part with the ache in it." The same self-annotating construction, both marking Vee's own worst place. Two of a distinctive frame in one chapter reads as reflex rather than rhyme.
    → Keep `:45`, which is the quieter and better-placed of the two; recast `:9`.

11. **`:53` — Word precision.** "the ease came back into him like a light turned up — himself again, easy again." *Ease* and *easy* inside eleven words, with "himself again, easy again" already doubling on its own rhythm.
    → Vary one of the two.

12. **`:55` — Word precision.** "and the patience was suddenly the most unbearable thing in the room." *Suddenly* narrates the abruptness the sentence structure already delivers, and *the most unbearable thing in the room* is a superlative where a concrete reaction is available.
    → Cut *suddenly* at minimum.

13. **`:65` / `:71` / `:81` — Within-chapter repetition.** "Whatever had been holding her up went out of her," "the fight went out of her all at once," "the patience went out of him." Three instances of one construction in seventeen lines, all marking a collapse of resistance. (Book-wide, "went out of her/him" also runs in `above-him`, `peekaboo`, `in-her-place`, `a-round`, `vee-on-the-bench`, `burn`, `grace`, `nothing-underneath` — see Echo candidates.)
    → Keep `:81` (the payoff the whole sex scene is built toward) and one of the earlier two; vary the third.

14. **`:71` — Pre-ruled echo (FIX-AT #6, echo-rulings.md ruling #29).** "the fight went out of her all at once, like a held breath let go." `standards.md:141` is the ruled Vol-1 keeper; `under-the-rug:71` is named as a remaining occurrence to vary. Note ruling #29's general form: prefer a replacement that earns something later over a like-for-like swap — here, the next sentence's "The fear that had stood on her chest… stepped off" is already the better breath-adjacent image, so the simile may simply be surplus.
    → Vary or cut the simile; the sentence loses nothing if it ends at "all at once."

15. **`:75` — Rhythm.** The paragraph is one 300-word block containing three distinct movements (the undressing and neck, the breasts, and her wanting his control to break). Its natural break is after "her back lifting off the bed to feed him more of it" — the last sentence of the second movement and the paragraph's best line — after which the prose changes subject from what he does to what she wants.
    → Break the paragraph there.

16. **`:75` — Within-chapter repetition.** "undressed her without hurry," "his tongue first, unhurried," "in no hurry to be anywhere lower" — three hurry-negations in one paragraph. (The two *unhurried* linter hits are triage-acked; the flag is the local density of the whole family, not either ack.)
    → Vary "without hurry" or "in no hurry" so the paragraph carries at most two.

17. **`:81` — Within-chapter repetition.** "He came up over her then… he got over her and into her almost in one motion." *Over her* twice in two sentences, the second inside the chapter's most kinetic line, where the repetition drags on the momentum.
    → Recast the second occurrence; the sentence wants a verb that isn't a restaging.

18. **`:83` — Word precision.** "and the relief had her yet again." *Yet again* is an authorial counting-word imported into a sentence otherwise made of pure sensation, and it faintly editorializes weariness the paragraph doesn't intend.
    → Cut *yet again*.

19. **`:87` — Within-chapter repetition.** "The sweat they'd made was cooling on her now… as though the kisses were still set there, cooling with the rest of her." *Cooling* twice in three sentences, both literal-thermal, without the second being a designed return.
    → Vary the second, or let the kisses simply be "still set there."

20. **`:89` — Overwork.** "felt him go helpless and urgent and past all his control, felt him shake and give and empty himself into her." This re-narrates `:81` ("shoved deep and held and spent himself in her, shaking, undone, hers") in nearly the same nouns eight lines later, and the second telling is the weaker — the first has *hers* at the end of it.
    → Compress the recall to a single clause; the reader is still holding `:81`.

21. **`:91` / `:93` — Rhythm.** `:91` ends "It had no name. He'd given her all of it and not the name," and `:93` immediately reopens with the same want and then names the *boyfriend*/mother/father problem, which is the coda's real last movement. `:91`'s final two sentences pre-state `:93`'s subject and cost the last paragraph its arrival.
    → End `:91` at "could not take a bit of it in" and let `:93` open the naming.
