## Findings

**1. `:7` — "It rode light in her hand." … "and the bag had gone light around the absence of it."**
*Overwork.* The opening sentence already told us the bag is light; the intervening list (the drawer, the brush, the scrunchies) shows *why*; the closing clause then re-announces the lightness a third time. Recommendation: cut the trailing "and the bag had gone light around the absence of it" and let the drawer inventory end the sentence on "a whole drawer of her set down in a clean man's house."

**2. `:7` — "the private foolish pleasure of it, hers." / `:47` — "the foolish hot prick of tears"**
*Within-chapter repetition.* "Foolish" is Vee's self-deprecating modifier in both places, at the chapter's opening and its emotional peak; the second is the one that matters, and the first pre-spends the word. Recommendation: drop "foolish" from `:7` (the phrase survives without it) and leave `:47` alone.

**3. `:9` — "coming back to the dorm on four hours and somebody else's shower, of opening a textbook and reading the same paragraph four times"**
*Word precision.* Two "four"s eleven words apart in a paragraph whose whole rhetorical engine is a *counted* number ("Five nights. She'd lasted five."), which makes the accidental numeral echo compete with the deliberate one. Recommendation: change the second number so only "five" is doing counting work.

**4. `:11` — "only the warm of the house at his back" … "the cold of her cheeks against the warm of his palms"**
*Within-chapter repetition.* The `the warm of ___` nominal is a signature construction, and it fires twice in three sentences here (then again at `:53`, "his own warm kitchen"). The second is the load-bearing one — it's touch, and it's paired against "the cold of." Recommendation: recast the house instance so the palms keep the construction.

**5. `:15` — "just how true the story had been."**
*Word precision.* The clause is doing an irony that inverts under a second reading: her throat is showing that the study story did *not* account for what she'd been holding, so "how true" reads as the almost-right word for "how little of it was true" — a rare place where the sentence's meaning has to be reconstructed. Recommendation: settle which direction the irony runs and make the final clause say it.

**6. `:15` — "He was in no more hurry there than anywhere."**
*Overwork / repetition.* The paragraph opens on "unhurried hands" and "the patience of it was its own small cruelty," and the next sentence ("He always came around to her first, always…") is the real statement; this middle sentence is a third rendering of the same fact and dilutes the "always, always" line following it. Recommendation: cut the sentence.

**7. `:15` — "and she lay there after with her face hot, caught out again, her body having just published the number she'd gone to such trouble to keep private."**
*Rhythm.* Three consecutive statements of one exposure — hot face, caught out, published the number — at the end of an already very long paragraph, so the strongest of them (the published number, which answers `:11`'s "running tally her mouth would never once have read out") arrives after the reader has been told the same thing twice. Recommendation: cut "caught out again" so the paragraph runs face-hot → the tally called in.

**8. `:17` — "He moved slow even then. He always had a slowness left in him when she had none"**
*Overwork.* The second sentence restates the first in a slightly grander register, and "slow" recurs a third time nine words later ("a deep slow roll"). Recommendation: cut the first of the two sentences; the "always had a slowness left in him" version says it and characterizes.

**9. `:17` — "holding still and deep and shaking just slightly, the only time the control ever left him."**
*Overwork.* The shaking *is* the control leaving him; the appositive diagnoses the image half a second after it lands, in a chapter that elsewhere trusts observation (`:19`, `:23`). Recommendation: cut the trailing clause and end the paragraph on "shaking just slightly."

**10. `:21` — "dough he'd set going before she ever arrived" … "a pan had been going low and slow since before she'd come"**
*Within-chapter repetition.* The same preparation-predates-you clause twice in one paragraph, in the same grammatical shape, when the point (he started before she got here) only needs making once — and "going" carries both. Recommendation: keep the pan's, cut the dough's; the bread machine already implies the lead time. Same paragraph: "tasting it off the spoon" / "with the back of the spoon" — vary one.

**11. `:25` — "his attention helplessly down on it" … "the shape his attention took when it came down on a thing, and tonight some of it had come down on her and some on a ball of dough"**
*Within-chapter repetition / overwork.* The attention-coming-down figure runs three times in one paragraph, and the second half of the paragraph explicates a thesis the two preceding sentences ("He wasn't performing any of it for her… He'd have made the pizza exactly like this for an empty house") had already delivered clean. Recommendation: cut "his attention helplessly down on it" from the first sentence and let the closing figure land once.

**12. `:29` — "It was nothing like the pizza she knew, the flat orange sameness of that…" / "It was the difference, she thought, between cotton candy and a bowl of cut strawberries."**
*Rhythm.* The paragraph reaches its true last sentence at "let her count them," then supplies two more contrasts for the same idea — the orange-sameness pizza and the cotton-candy/strawberries figure — so the better of them (strawberries) has to compete with a version of itself. Recommendation: keep one contrast and cut the other; "she thought" can go with either.

**13. `:31` — "He was watching her when she looked up, pleased, the small private smile, as if her face had just told him something he'd wanted to know."**
*Rhythm.* A three-part appositive stack — pleased / the smile / the as-if — where each element says the same thing at a different level of abstraction, in a paragraph that then ends beautifully plain ("held her plate out for another"). See P1: this is also the chapter's spendable smile. Recommendation: cut "pleased," and keep either the smile or the as-if clause, not both.

**14. `:35` — "the one she'd slept in every single night she wasn't here, swimming in the soft worn bulk of it"**
*Overwork.* This is the narration delivering, eight lines early, the exact news Vee's confession at `:43` exists to deliver ("I wore it every night I wasn't here"), so her hardest admission arrives as a repeat. Recommendation: cut the "every single night" clause here and let her say it.

**15. `:43` — "I wore it every night I wasn't here and it went stale, and then it went to nothing, and one day last week it didn't smell like anything at all, so I washed it."**
*Dialogue shape.* "It went to nothing" and "it didn't smell like anything at all" are the same sentence twice, and the whole speech runs grammatically complete and well-ordered for a woman the narration says is shoving it out "all at once to be done with it." Recommendation: cut one of the two nothing-clauses so the line compresses toward the speed the beat is described as having.

**16. `:47` — "\"All weekend,\" he said. He pulled it on right then, over the shirt he already wore, the big soft flannel settling across the breadth of him where it had been built to sit, and the foolish hot prick of tears came up in her out of nowhere and she blinked them down before they could fall. \"You'll have it back Sunday.\""**
*Dialogue shape / repetition.* A long beat carrying two separate events (his action, her tears) is wedged between two short lines of his that should ride together into "Smelling like you." / "Smelling like me." — and "big soft flannel" repeats `:11`'s "one of the big soft shirts" in a sentence that already has both shirts in it. Recommendation: move the tears to after "You'll have it back Sunday," and vary one of the two big-soft shirts.

**17. `:53` — "She didn't say any of that. There was no need to." … "and that was the whole of his answer, as it always was."**
*Rhythm / overwork.* Two closing redundancies: "There was no need to" restates "She didn't say any of that," and the final clause explains the picture of a man standing in his kitchen in two shirts, which is self-evidently the answer. Recommendation: cut "There was no need to" and end the chapter on "because she'd told him to."

## Echo candidates

Distinctive phrasings in this chapter that may recur elsewhere. Not rulings; raised for the ledger. `new-ordinary` is roster #39, so it is the **later** occurrence in the first two.

1. **"came around to her first … before he took a single thing for himself"** — `new-ordinary:15` ("He always came around to her first, always, brought her the whole way over before he took a single thing for himself") vs. **`above-him:13`** ("it always came around to her first, brought all the way over and usually more than once before he took a single thing for himself"). Near-verbatim across three clauses, same subject, same claim. This is the strongest echo in the chapter and reads as recycling rather than motif; `above-him` (#34, already edited) holds it first and frames it as the thing she can't get used to. **Strong FIX-AT candidate at this chapter.**
2. **The taste-taking-apart figure** — `new-ordinary:29` ("This came apart on her tongue into all its separate pieces and let her count them… The onion still with a crunch to it and a clean sting") vs. **`in-his-hands:79`** ("she could take the thing apart on her tongue and tell each piece of it from the next… the clean sting of the onion"). Same figure, same shared item ("clean sting" + onion), and `in-his-hands` (#36) explicitly frames it as habitual ("as she had every time since the first night") where `new-ordinary` frames it as new ("the thing she had no frame for"). Both chapters do this well; one of them should own it. Note the framing mismatch is for the author, not this editor.
3. **"unhurried hands"** — `new-ordinary:15` vs. `vee-on-the-bench:81` ("only his unhurried hands and her own climbing need"). *Unhurried* is Pace's word by author ruling (2026-07-28) and the exact two-word pairing is a tighter collision than the register alone.
4. **"cost him something"** — `new-ordinary:45` ("the one that seemed to cost him something"), `made-up:51` ("the putting-away cost him something she could see"), `we-find-out:9` ("she could tell it cost him something"). Three renderings of the same Pace-visible-effort idiom. Worth a count at the end-of-pass harvest; if it is a ration, `:45` has a decent claim (the smile that costs).
5. **"something moved across his face"** (`:37`) — the `Something [verb]ed …` frame under ruling #32 / the logged end-of-pass harvest, and a near-twin of `gone:39` ("Something crossed her face and was gone"). Note this chapter then repeats "something" inside the same sentence ("under the puzzlement something she'd put there"). Counts toward the harvest.
6. **"before she could think any better of it"** (`:35`) — body-before-mind family (#8 / #8b). Breaks the `before s/he'd decided to` stem, so **not** counted against the #8 ration; logged for the family's end-of-pass inventory.
7. **Striking diction, verify unique:** "burned her nights down to the wick" (`:9`), "her body having just published the number" (`:15`), "the flat orange sameness" (`:29`), "cotton candy and a bowl of cut strawberries" (`:29`), "a sound like a handful of sand" (`:23`). No siblings found in `scenes/` on a first sweep.
