# Line edit — nothing-underneath (2026-08-07)

**Verdict: 27 findings** — 6 rhythm, 8 within-chapter repetition, 8 overwork, 4 word precision, 1 dialogue shape (plus 1 pre-ruled FIX-AT to apply and 2 pre-ruled items requiring no action).

The chapter is at its best when it trusts a short declarative to close a big movement: *"She had slept the afternoon down to something she could carry."* — *"So she was done."* — *"And then she stopped."* — *"So this was what the house sounded like when she wasn't in it."* — *"the bed a country away and both of them too close."* The dressing-table paragraph (:11) is exemplary, and the Kayla encounter (:37–:41) is the funniest thing in the volume, *dying by inches* over a laundry report. The doorstep reveal (:69–:73) and the pancakes/name coda (:103–:129) both land clean. Nearly every finding below is the same failure mode: a paragraph that has already made its point stopping to say what the point was. The chapter's own best paragraphs never do this, which is the standard being applied.

## Findings

1. **:7** — "Knowing now what she had to do, she got up and pushed the window shut against the cold." **Overwork.** The preceding sentence already ended on "she was going to get up and go and do this"; the participial opener re-announces the decision and repeats "get up" from ten words earlier. Cut "Knowing now what she had to do," and let the paragraph end on the action.

2. **:13** — "habit moved faster than the plan … before she knew it, and then, still running on habit, she was already correcting the choice." **Within-chapter repetition.** Three "habit"s in two sentences, where the chapter elsewhere names a thing once and moves. Cut "still running on habit," — the correcting is already inside the sentence's momentum.

3. **:13** — "…because it was the first time all day the plan had had to overrule her own hands, and won." **Overwork.** "the sound of it closing was funnier and worse than she expected at once" is the paragraph's true last clause; the causal tail explains the joke it just made. Recommend cutting from "because" to the end.

4. **:13** — "the good black everyday bra" / "the pretty one" / "the good lace she saved for him" / "the sexy bra." **Word precision.** Four names for two garments in six lines, and "good" does duty for both the everyday bra and the lace (and again for "the good coat" later). Recommend collapsing to two designations and reserving "good" for the coat.

5. **:17** — "she laughed once, quietly, at how tidily the two arrived at the same spot" and, nine lines later, "she giggled at that, alone in the room, a real giggle she didn't try to stop." **Within-chapter repetition (same beat twice).** One paragraph renders Vee alone-and-amused twice, and the second rendering is the one that earns its adverbs. Recommend cutting the first laugh and letting the shoe joke land dry.

6. **:17** — "and they sent a thrill through her even now." **Overwork / word precision.** "They were also the sexiest shoes she'd ever put on her feet — trust Randi —" has already done it; "sent a thrill through her" is the chapter's only piece of generic sensation-language. Recommend cutting the clause.

7. **:21** — "stood up on purpose in a lit room with her heels on" … "but that she had decided this, standing up, in her right mind." **Within-chapter repetition.** The distinctive figure — *standing up* as the marker of deliberateness — is spent twice in four lines, and the second occurrence is inside the sentence that explains the first. Recommend cutting "standing up," from the second.

8. **:25–:31** — "as he would look," "as he'd put it once," "as it filled his," "as he liked to," "as he made her wait," "as his did when he was in no hurry," "as he took stock of her," "the same unbearable patience he used." **Within-chapter repetition.** Eight *as-he* comparisons in four paragraphs. The move is clearly designed — she is performing him on her own body — but at this density the reader stops feeling the substitution and starts hearing the grammar. Recommend thinning to four or five, keeping the ones where the borrowed gesture is specific (rolling the nipple, taking the weight) and dropping the ones where it is only an attitude ("in no hurry," "took stock").

9. **:25** — "It was where he set his face and went still, comforted past speaking; where Pace took his comfort like it was needed and freely given both." **Overwork.** The second clause restates the first with "comfort" for "comforted," and abstracts a rendered image ("set his face and went still") into a proposition. Recommend cutting the semicolon clause.

10. **:27–:29** — "She did not hurry past them" / "She worked them without hurry" / "as his did when he was in no hurry" / "in no rush to arrive anywhere." **Within-chapter repetition.** Four statements of unhurriedness in three paragraphs, two of them in consecutive sentences. Recommend cutting two, and preferring the one that shows the waiting ("making herself wait as he made her wait").

11. **:31 / :81** — "the same unbearable patience he used" / "his damnable patience holding." **Within-chapter repetition.** The identical `[adjective]-able patience` construction at the two poles of the chapter, in the mirror and on the doorstep. The doorstep instance is the stronger (it has "damn him" set up eight lines earlier at :79); recommend recasting the mirror one.

12. **:33** — "It was not hers to finish. She could take herself over in under a minute like this, easily; but that was not the plan, and, when it came to it, not what she wanted. She wanted him to be the one." **Overwork.** Four sentences establishing one fact, with two of them ("It was not hers to finish" / "She wanted him to be the one") saying it in different directions. Recommend cutting "It was not hers to finish." — the paragraph opens harder on "And then she stopped."

13. **:33 / :35 / :45** — "slick and unfinished," "unfinished on purpose," "left unfinished," "swollen and unfinished." **Within-chapter repetition.** A distinctive word four times across thirteen lines. "unfinished on purpose" is the one carrying the argument; recommend varying or cutting at least two of the others.

14. **:35** — "The decision was worn now, on her body, no longer a thing she could turn over in her head." and "…and the knowing was the whole charge of it." **Overwork.** "It closed over her like a door" and the buttoned-to-the-collar mirror image have already made the point twice over; the paragraph then states its own theme at both ends. Recommend cutting the "no longer a thing she could turn over in her head" clause and the "and the knowing was the whole charge of it" tail.

15. **:41** — "…the cold reaching straight up under the coat and finding every bare inch of her, and into the car." **Rhythm.** The paragraph's true last beat is the cold finding her; "and into the car" is a stage direction hung off the end of a sentence that had already arrived. Recommend cutting the final four words.

16. **:43** — "And the charge of it built in her as she went, the sheer enormous fact of what she was doing: … carrying her own body to him because the words had failed her and this would not." **Overwork.** The chapter's premise restated as narration at the point where the driving is dramatising it perfectly well; "the sheer enormous fact" is also the chapter's vaguest noun phrase, and "the charge of it" repeats :35's "the whole charge of it" eight lines earlier. Recommend cutting from "the sheer enormous fact" through "and this would not," keeping the concrete list if anything.

17. **:43 / :57** — "bare under a coat on an empty road" / "bare under a coat on his doorstep in the dark." **Within-chapter repetition (and a designed callback at risk).** `my-friend-randi:85` sets this up verbatim — "turned up on a doorstep bare under a coat like something ordered in" — which makes `:57` the payoff and `:43` an accidental pre-spend of it fourteen lines early. Recommend varying `:43` and protecting `:57` exactly as written.

18. **:37 / :89** — "wound up as she was" / "wound as she was." **Within-chapter repetition.** Near-verbatim, and the chapter also carries "wound exactly this tight" (:33) and "wound tighter for the scare" (:51). The `:89` instance is load-bearing (it explains why she can't last); recommend recasting `:37`.

19. **:57** — the paragraph after "So this was what the house sounded like when she wasn't in it." **Rhythm.** That is the best sentence in the chapter and the paragraph's real end; what follows is three more endings in a row — the card metaphor, the December conditional, and two short sentences of resolve ("She made herself stand in it. She did not knock again, and she did not bolt for the car."). Recommend keeping one of the three and breaking the rest out or cutting.

20. **:57** — "The blonde arrived, tan and laughing in his kitchen, and could get no grip." **Word precision.** "could get no grip" is the almost-right idiom for an intrusive image failing to stick, and it briefly reads as *she* could get no grip. Recommend a verb that keeps the image as the subject.

21. **:61** — "the light from behind him put his face half in the dark, the horn still going low somewhere behind him." **Within-chapter repetition.** "behind him" twice in one sentence, the second doing no locating work the first hasn't. Recommend cutting the second.

22. **:61 / :65** — "the courtesy came down over him like a thing reached for in a hurry" then "The formal voice, the careful courtesy, the same wall…" followed by "She was not going to ask him again. She had come to the end of asking the man behind the wall to come out and answer her." **Overwork.** The simile at :61 is precise and complete; :65 renames it twice and then states her resolve twice. Recommend cutting "the careful courtesy," and one of the two asking-sentences.

23. **:73** — "and the two were one look and she could not have pulled them apart." **Overwork.** The second half restates the first, and "the two were one ___" also repeats :11's "the two would just have to be the same thing." Recommend cutting after "one look," or cutting the whole clause and ending on "forgiven."

24. **:75** — "his breath went out of him into her hair as she came in, all those weeks of it going out of him in one breath." **Within-chapter repetition.** "breath / went out of him / going out of him / one breath" inside twenty-two words. The second clause is the one with the weeks in it; recommend cutting the first.

25. **:77 / :87 / :93** — "his hands slid down … to her bottom, both hands, taking the round of her into his palms and lifting her the last inch" / "his hands took hold of her ass and gripped, holding her wobbling world upright" / "His hands went back to her ass and lifted her clean off the floor." **Within-chapter repetition (same gesture three times).** Ruling #36 protects the `:77` phrasing, but the *hands-take-her-and-lift* beat is staged three times in sixteen lines, and the middle one adds only the mixed "wobbling world." Recommend cutting or reducing `:87`, which sits between two stronger stagings.

26. **:79** — "unhurried even here in the cold, faster than he'd have taken it in his own warm bed, maybe, but slow enough to make her feel every stop." **Rhythm / overwork.** A hedged three-way comparison at the moment his mouth starts down her — the sentence deliberates where the scene accelerates, and "maybe" is the only place the narration second-guesses itself. Recommend cutting the middle clause.

27. **:85** — "…because she had done this, come out of hiding and driven through the dark and a police stop to put herself in his hands, and he had gone to the ground for her the moment she opened the coat." **Overwork.** A plot recap inside the chapter's most physical passage; the reader has just read all of it. The paragraph's live half is "she stood in an open door under the night sky … and the fact of it thrilled her past anything like shame." Recommend cutting from "because she had done this" to the end.

28. **:97** — "and now it surged back up, doubling, and she was climbing again before she'd finished the first time." **Within-chapter repetition.** "finished" twice in one sentence (the clause opens "She had never really finished coming off his mouth"), and "doubling" repeats the figure from :83 ("it came at her doubled"). Recommend recasting the second "finished."

29. **:11 / :99 / :101** — "building by degrees," "and then, by degrees, closer to together," "easing her down out of the frenzy by slow degrees." **Within-chapter repetition.** Three in one chapter, two of them two lines apart, and the phrase runs across eleven chapters book-wide (see Echo candidates). Recommend cutting one of the adjacent pair.

30. **:101** — "It was the sweetest thing, sensual and asking for nothing." **Overwork.** The sentence before it has already rendered exactly this ("not to start anything back up, only to keep her gently lit"); the narration then grades its own scene with two abstract adjectives. Recommend cutting the clause and keeping "easing her down out of the frenzy."

31. **:105** — "Then the name landed, her own name, easy and warm in his mouth — the same name he'd said behind her in December as she walked out his door, the one that hadn't been the word she'd come for then and hadn't been enough — and the laugh came apart in the middle." **Rhythm / dialogue shape.** This is the volume's payoff beat and the em-dash aside is thirty words of retrospective explanation wedged between the landing and the reaction; the subject has to be held across the whole gap. Recommend moving the December material out into its own sentence after "the laugh came apart in the middle and let the tears through after all," so the name lands and detonates without interruption.

32. **:107 / :109** — "After a while he got up…" / "After a while, from under the blanket, she said…" **Within-chapter repetition.** Two consecutive paragraphs opening on the identical time-phrase. Recommend cutting the second.

33. **:119** — "whisked the dry things together without looking, his hands knowing it cold." **Word precision.** "cold" is the chapter's most loaded literal word — it appears roughly twenty times as weather — and this idiomatic use collides with it inside a paragraph that is specifically about warmth. Recommend a different idiom for by-heart.

34. **:121** — "bent to some exact thing and getting it precisely right" and "the plain fact of a warm room." **Word precision + pre-ruled FIX-AT #7.** "exact" and "precisely" double each other in seven words, and "the plain fact of" is the ruled FIX-AT phrase (see Pre-ruled items). Recommend cutting "precisely" and varying "the plain fact of."
